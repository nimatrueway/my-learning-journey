Your field-linguist cat has packed a notebook, not a red correction pen. 🌍🐱
The question is not whether every language follows English grammar. It is how
different languages solve communication problems, and what evidence lets us
compare their solutions.

This course teaches **general linguistics and cross-linguistic grammar**, not
fluency in every language. No short course can catalogue the grammar of every
language, and many remain incompletely documented. Instead, learn a reusable
framework for approaching any grammar with better questions.

## Start with evidence

1. [Grammar as a scientific hypothesis](./01-scientific-foundations/01-grammar-as-science.mdx) · ~9 min.

The full learning path will move through classification, sound systems, word
structure, sentence structure, meaning, variation, and a comparative capstone.

## What you will learn

- Separate **genealogy** (shared ancestry), **typology** (structural features),
  and **contact** (interaction between communities).
- Analyze phonemes, morphemes, constituents, argument marking, grammatical
  categories, and meaning without assuming English is the default.
- Use glossed examples, minimal pairs, distributional evidence, and carefully
  sampled datasets to test an analysis.
- Treat signed languages, smaller language communities, and nonstandard
  varieties as languages with systematic grammar, not exceptions to it.

## How to work

No prior linguistics is required. A notebook is enough. Predict each widget's
result before changing its selection, attempt the exercise, then use quiz
feedback to revise your analysis. Optional folds add depth, not prerequisites.
Examples identify whether they are constructed teaching data or real language
forms. A single example never stands for an entire language or its speakers.

## Open reference shelf

[Essentials of Linguistics, 2nd edition](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/)
provides an open introductory textbook;
[WALS](https://wals.info/) compares structural features;
[Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/) catalogues languages and genealogical proposals;
and the [Leipzig Glossing Rules](https://www.eva.mpg.de/lingua/resources/glossing-rules.php)
explain the annotation system used in linguistic examples. These are optional
companions: the lessons explain their own evidence and terminology.

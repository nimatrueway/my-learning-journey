The field cat's atlas is a set of **35 comparison stops**, not a claim to
describe every language. 🌍🐾 Regions organize the itinerary; they are not
genealogical families. Languages travel and communities cross borders.

⏱️ **~9 min** · optional reference; read once, revisit often.

Use this reference alongside the lessons, not as a memorization exam. Each
row gives a family or branch label and an analytical starting point, not a
full grammar or a unique feature. Look up the specific variety, examples,
and source before using a row as a formal comparative dataset.

## Europe and the Caucasus

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English | Germanic, Indo-European | Little noun inflection coexists with obligatory clause patterns. Compare pronoun case with full nouns. |
| Spanish | Romance, Indo-European | Verb inflection, subject omission, and negative concord show why an English translation is not an analysis. |
| Finnish | Finnic, Uralic | Compare case suffixes with English prepositions; test which spatial relations are distinguished. |
| Basque | Isolate | Investigate ergative case marking without assuming all syntactic behavior follows the same alignment. |
| Georgian | Kartvelian | Examine rich verb morphology and construction-conditioned argument marking rather than assigning one alignment label. |

English and Spanish are related but differ in many details. Finnish and Basque
can share selected structures without belonging to that family. For alignment
criteria, use [WALS chapter 98](https://wals.info/chapter/98).

## Africa and neighboring Arabic-speaking regions

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arabic | Semitic, Afro-Asiatic | Root-and-pattern morphology and standardized versus spoken varieties. Name the variety being described. |
| Hausa | Chadic, Afro-Asiatic | Tone and grammatical marking contrast within the broader family: Arabic is not a template for all Afro-Asiatic languages. |
| Swahili | Bantu, Atlantic-Congo | Noun-class agreement affects more than noun endings; track concord across dependents and predicates. |
| Yoruba | Atlantic-Congo | Tone carries lexical and grammatical distinctions; many suffixes are not a prerequisite for grammatical expressiveness. |
| Zulu | Bantu, Atlantic-Congo | Compare noun classes with Swahili and investigate click consonants and contact history. |

Atlantic-Congo is commonly placed within broader Niger-Congo proposals whose
membership and structure require care. "Khoisan" is not one established family
simply because some languages have clicks. See [WALS chapter 1](https://wals.info/chapter/1)
for inventory and contact examples and [chapter 13](https://wals.info/chapter/13) for tone.

## South, west, and central Asia

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persian | Iranian, Indo-European | Verb-final patterns and object marker *rā*. Compare specificity-sensitive marking with Spanish personal *a*, not as identical systems. |
| Hindi | Indo-Aryan, Indo-European | Aspect-conditioned splits in case and agreement show why construction and argument type matter. |
| Tamil | Dravidian | Suffixing, case, and retroflex consonants invite comparison with neighboring Indo-Aryan languages without collapsing the families. |
| Turkish | Turkic | Separate suffixes in *ev-ler-de*; investigate vowel harmony, evidentiality, and clause structure independently. |
| Mongolian | Mongolic | Case, suffixing, and verb-final organization can resemble Turkish without proving shared ancestry. |

"Altaic" groupings of Turkic, Mongolic, and sometimes other families are disputed,
not a conclusion from suffixes or word order. Revisit
[families, types, and neighbors](./01-scientific-foundations/02-classifying-languages.mdx)
and [grammatical categories](./02-sounds-and-words/03-grammatical-categories.mdx).

## East and southeast Asia

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandarin | Sinitic, Sino-Tibetan | Tone, classifiers, question words in situ, and aspect. Little inflection does not mean little grammar. |
| Cantonese | Sinitic, Sino-Tibetan | Compare tone and sentence-final particles with Mandarin; ancestry and writing traditions do not make spoken systems identical. |
| Japanese | Japonic | Verb-final clauses, particles, prenominal relatives, length contrasts, and *neko o kaburu*. |
| Korean | Koreanic | Clause-final forms and speech-level/honorific distinctions; resemblance to Japanese is not proof of ancestry. |
| Vietnamese | Austroasiatic | Tone and relatively analytic morphology contrast with another Austroasiatic language, Khmer. |
| Thai | Kra-Dai | Tone and classifiers recur across family boundaries; compare constructions, not just geography. |
| Khmer | Austroasiatic | A non-tonal system contrasts with Vietnamese within the broader family. |

"Chinese" can refer to related varieties and particular standardized practices.
State the spoken variety and script conventions. Family-internal classifications
in Sino-Tibetan remain debated. Revisit
[sounds and signs](./02-sounds-and-words/01-sound-and-sign-systems.mdx) and
[non-compositional expressions](./04-meaning-and-diversity/01-composition-and-idioms.mdx).

## Islands, Australia, and New Guinea

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indonesian | Malayic, Austronesian | Affixation, reduplication, and *kita/kami* clusivity distinguish several grammatical dimensions. |
| Māori | Polynesian, Austronesian | Verb-initial clauses contrast with Indonesian within the broad family. |
| Malagasy | Austronesian | VOS patterns show that ancestry is not a location label: an Austronesian language is spoken in Madagascar. |
| Warlpiri | Pama-Nyungan | Flexible constituent order and case marking; ask how ordering choices track discourse rather than calling them random. |
| Rotokas | North Bougainville | A small consonant inventory is one sound-system dimension, not evidence of a globally simple language. |

The older WALS discussion calls Rotokas's family "West Bougainville"; catalogue
terminology can differ. "Papuan" is a geographical umbrella, not one proven
family. Australia and New Guinea both contain multiple families. See
[WALS chapter 1](https://wals.info/chapter/1) and verify current labels in
[Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/).

## The Americas

| Stop | Genealogical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navajo | Athabaskan, within Na-Dene | Tone and complex verb structure recur far from the East Asian comparison cases. |
| Inuktitut | Inuit, Eskimo-Aleut | Productive word-building packages many meanings; analyze morphemes rather than counting spaces in a translation. |
| K'iche' | Mayan | Ergative patterns invite comparison with Basque and Hindi; separate case, indexing, and syntax. |
| Hixkaryana | Cariban | OVS clauses challenge universal subject-first claims; use the construction and coding criteria in WALS. |
| Southern Quechua varieties | Quechuan | Suffixing, person marking, and evidential/discourse markers require variety-specific analysis. |
| Guaraní | Tupian | Investigate nasal harmony: phonological patterns need not align with written letters. |

"Amerindian" is not one demonstrated family encompassing these stops. English,
Spanish, and Portuguese also belong to the continent's language landscape, but
neither their spread nor this table represents its full diversity. Read
[WALS chapter 81](https://wals.info/chapter/81) for Hixkaryana's order example and
[chapter 98](https://wals.info/chapter/98) for alignment distinctions.

## Signed-language communities

| Stop | Historical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| American Sign Language (ASL) | Linked to French Sign Language traditions | Handshape, movement, location, orientation, and nonmanual marking; grammar is not signed English word order. |
| British Sign Language (BSL) | British sign-language lineage | Compare structure with ASL without predicting mutual intelligibility from surrounding spoken English. |

Not all signed languages are one family. Glottolog's
["Sign Language" pseudo-family](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/sign1238)
is an organizational heading, not evidence of ancestry. ASL and BSL do not
represent all village and emerging sign languages. Consult
[Essentials on signed articulation](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/3-7-signed-language-articulators/)
and [signed phonology](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/4-10-signed-language-phonology/).

## Shared, distinctive, or unique?

Tone occurs in Mandarin, Yoruba, and Navajo: a **shared structural property**
does not establish one family. English and Persian share Indo-European ancestry
but differ in typical order: family membership does not determine every feature.
Agglutination, gender, and ergativity describe dimensions with scope, not species
of language with fixed checklists.

"Distinctive in this comparison" and "rare in this documented sample" are
defensible claims. **Unique in the world** requires much broader evidence,
including varieties and uncertain analyses. Unusual structures belong to
ordinary human communication, not a cabinet of curiosities.

## A compact terminology shelf

- **Phoneme / allophone:** contrastive unit / contextual realization; test distributions.
- **Morpheme / allomorph:** analyzed meaning-bearing unit / alternative realization.
- **Analytic / synthetic:** relatively separate encoding / more grammatical material per word.
- **Agglutinative / fusional:** relatively separable category markers / more bundled encoding.
- **Polysynthetic:** extensive grammatical packaging; definitions vary and length alone is insufficient.
- **SOV, SVO, VSO:** comparative ordering labels for defined clause types, not all sentences.
- **Accusative / ergative alignment:** S with A / S with P in the measured behavior.
- **Tense / aspect / mood:** temporal location / event perspective / grammatical attitudes or possibilities.
- **Evidentiality:** grammatical information-source marking, not a truth guarantee.
- **Gender / noun class / classifier:** distinct systems of categorization and distribution.
- **Clusivity:** whether a first-person group includes the addressee.
- **Compositional / non-compositional:** regularly combined meaning / conventional meaning not predicted by ordinary parts and rules.

These are reminders, not substitutes for the lessons' tests and caveats.

## Extend the itinerary to any other language

1. Identify the **specific variety** in [Glottolog's catalogue](https://glottolog.org/glottolog/language). Record aliases, a stable identifier, classification version, and uncertainty. Country names and spelling similarities are not enough.
2. Find a descriptive grammar or community resource through its bibliography. Locate examples, paradigms, and stated scope, not a stereotype list.
3. Look for relevant [WALS features](https://wals.info/feature). Read their definitions and check the coded variety. A blank value means missing coverage, not absence.
4. Build the [capstone profile](./04-meaning-and-diversity/03-comparative-capstone.mdx). Compare one shared and one contrasting property, keeping ancestry, area, and modality separate.

For contact languages, add [APiCS](https://apics-online.info/) to the shelf.
For glosses, use the [Leipzig Glossing Rules](https://www.eva.mpg.de/lingua/resources/glossing-rules.php).
Investigating an unlisted language is a central outcome, not an appendix.

## Sources and scope

Original comparative guide using the lessons' terminology and examples.
Family/branch labels follow common classifications represented in Glottolog;
broader disputed proposals and older WALS labels are flagged rather than merged.
Linked WALS chapters provide feature definitions and selected examples, not
every claim about every row. To turn these starting points into research data,
follow the language descriptions and record the examples and variety. No row
is a complete grammar.

"The cat saw the dog with binoculars" has one word order and at least two
ordinary interpretations. Either the cat used binoculars, or the dog had
them. A sentence is more than a left-to-right list.

## Constituents organize the sequence

A **constituent** is a unit in a structural analysis. A **head** determines
central properties of a phrase; **dependents** include complements and
modifiers. In an introductory analysis, *the dog* is a noun phrase, and *with
binoculars* is a prepositional phrase. The latter can attach to the verb phrase
or modify the noun phrase, producing different interpretations.

```text
[The cat] [saw [the dog] [with binoculars]]
  The cat used the binoculars.

[The cat] [saw [the dog [with binoculars]]]
  The dog had the binoculars.
```

These brackets are simplified **constructed English illustrations**, not a
claim that every theory uses the same phrase labels. **Substitution**, **movement**,
and **coordination** can provide constituency evidence. Replacing *the dog with
binoculars* with *it* supports treating that sequence as a unit under the
noun-modifying reading. Use converging tests; one failed movement can reflect
other constraints rather than absence of a constituent.

## Six orders, a carefully defined comparison

For a canonical transitive clause, introductory typology often uses `S` for
subject, `O` for object, and `V` for verb. All six permutations occur in the
world. WALS chapter 81 compares declarative clauses with full nominal arguments,
using agent-like and patient-like roles to operationalize the comparison.

| Order | One comparison case in WALS 81 |
| --- | --- |
| SOV | Japanese |
| SVO | Mandarin |
| VSO | Irish |
| VOS | Nias |
| OVS | Hixkaryana |
| OSV | Nadëb |

The Japanese example in that chapter is *John ga tegami o yon-da*, "John read
the letter." `ga` and `o` mark the two argument phrases in the example, and
`yon-da` is "read-PST." The full phrases are `[John ga]` and `[tegami o]`;
do not count each particle as another subject or object.

These are scoped labels, not sentence generators. Questions, subordinate
clauses, omitted arguments, auxiliaries, or discourse contexts can change
the observed sequence. German's verb-second main clauses and verb-final
subordinate patterns show why a single three-letter label can conceal important rules.

## Heads and dependents add more dimensions

Compare **prepositions** (before their noun phrase) with **postpositions**
(after it), adjective–noun versus noun–adjective order, possessors, and relative
clauses. Verb–object order correlates with some other orders across samples,
but correlations are not exceptionless laws. A language need not have every
head on the same side of every dependent.

**Topic** is what the discourse is about; **focus** highlights a contribution
against relevant alternatives. Neither is synonymous with subject. Flexible
word order can organize these meanings while case or agreement helps identify
arguments. Calling it "free" order should not imply that every permutation is
equally natural in every context.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes drawing brackets for the two binocular readings and writing
a context for each. Then label the Japanese example's full argument phrases,
verb, and overall order. Finally, list two things an SOV label cannot tell
you: whether questions move a phrase, whether arguments can be omitted, or
where adjectives occur are good candidates.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Phrase structure and dependency approaches (optional)</summary>

Constituency models group words into nested phrases. Dependency models connect
heads to dependents. They emphasize different representations and can describe
many of the same observations. Cross-linguistic categories require diagnostics:
English nouns and adjectives do not determine the inventory of another language.
Similarly, identifying a subject can be more complicated than finding the first noun.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Constituency and attachment can distinguish meanings without changing word order.
2. SOV and similar labels summarize a specified clause comparison, not a whole grammar.
3. Head-dependent order and discourse organization add dimensions beyond S, O, and V.

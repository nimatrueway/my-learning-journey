## Asking is a function, not one universal operation

**Polar questions** ask for a yes/no-type answer. **Content questions** ask
for a value such as a person, object, place, or reason. Strategies include
intonation, particles, special verb forms, word-order changes, and combinations.
English auxiliary inversion is one strategy, not the definition of questioning.

For a simple Mandarin content question, compare these **illustrative standard
forms in pinyin**, with simplified word glosses:

```text
Nǐ  mǎi  shénme?
2SG buy  what
'What do you buy / what are you buying?'
```

`shénme` occupies the object position in this example: a **wh-in-situ** strategy.
Mandarin can form polar questions with a sentence-final particle such as `ma`,
but that does not mean every Mandarin question is made by adding `ma`.
Question type, embedding, context, and the interpretation of indefinite/question
words all matter. English also has in-situ echo questions, so even its pattern
is not a simple "always front" rule.

## Negation has a grammar, not arithmetic on words

**Standard negation** negates an ordinary declarative predicate. Other
constructions, such as negative commands and negative existence, may use
different forms. Do not infer every negative pattern from one word translated "not."

**Negative concord** combines elements associated with negation while yielding
a single semantic negation. The following Spanish examples appear in WALS
chapter 115:

```text
Nadie   vino.
nobody  came
'Nobody came.'

No   vi     nada.
NEG  I.saw  nothing
'I did not see anything.'
```

The first places `nadie` before the verb. In the second, a postverbal negative
indefinite occurs with predicate negation. This position-sensitive pattern
does not establish that "two negatives always cancel" or that they always
concord. Language variety, construction, and context determine the analysis.

**Scope** asks what an operator applies to. "Not every student arrived"
does not normally mean "No student arrived": the first allows some to have
arrived. Counting negative-looking words cannot resolve scope or concord.

## Clauses inside clauses

A **complement clause** is selected by a predicate: "I know [that they left]."
A **relative clause** modifies a nominal expression: "the book [that I bought]."
An **adverbial clause** contributes a relationship such as time or condition:
"We left [when the rain started]." Languages can use finite clauses, nonfinite
forms, nominalization, clause chaining, and other strategies.

Relative clauses can precede or follow their head noun. Japanese often places
the relative clause before the noun; English normally places it after. The
role of the relativized participant may be represented by a gap, a pronoun,
or other marking. Do not assume an English-like relative pronoun must be present.

**Valency** concerns a predicate's argument requirements. Passive and causative
constructions reorganize those requirements or their expression. A causative
adds a causing participant in a specified construction; a passive changes
how existing participants are realized. Neither is simply swapping two nouns
in the English translation.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes separating "Did you buy a book?" from "What did you buy?"
by question type. Mark the object position in the Mandarin example. Then
contrast the two Spanish negatives and bracket one complement, one relative,
and one time clause in examples you construct. State which observations come
from real cited forms and which come from your invented sentences.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Universals require narrowly defined domains (optional)</summary>

A claim about "all questions" can fail because it mixes question types,
main and embedded clauses, or special discourse contexts. The same caution
applies to negation and relativization. An implicational universal predicts
that if a language has property A, it also has B; one counterexample under
matching definitions challenges an exceptionless version. A statistical
tendency instead predicts an association, not an absolute ban.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Polar and content questions can use different grammatical strategies within one language.
2. Negative concord and scope must be analyzed from patterns and meanings, not word counts.
3. Complements, relatives, and adverbial clauses connect clauses in different ways.

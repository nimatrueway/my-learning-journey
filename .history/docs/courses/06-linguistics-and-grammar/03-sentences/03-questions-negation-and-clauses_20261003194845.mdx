## Yes/no, or a missing piece of information?

"Did you buy a book?" asks for a yes/no-type answer: a **polar question**.
"What did you buy?" asks for a missing piece: a **content question**.
Languages can use pitch, little markers called particles, special verb forms,
changed order, or a combination. English-style rearranging is one option,
not the definition of asking a question.

Here is a short **illustrative Mandarin form in pinyin**, with simple glosses:

```text
Nǐ  mǎi  shénme?
2SG buy  what
'What do you buy / what are you buying?'
```

`shénme`, "what," stays in the object position. The technical name is
**wh-in-situ**: the question word stays in place. Mandarin can use final `ma`
for a polar question, but adding `ma` is not the recipe for every question.
Type, context, and questions inside other clauses matter. Even English can
ask an echo question like "You bought WHAT?" So "always at the front" would
be too tidy for either language.

## Two negative words do not automatically make a yes

Start with how a language denies an ordinary statement: **standard negation**.
Then check commands and statements that something does not exist; these may
use other forms. One word meaning "not" cannot tell you the entire system.

In **negative concord**, negative-looking pieces work together to express
one negative meaning. These Spanish examples come from WALS chapter 115:

```text
Nadie   vino.
nobody  came
'Nobody came.'

No   vi     nada.
NEG  I.saw  nothing
'I did not see anything.'
```

In the first, `nadie` comes before the verb. In the second, `nada` comes after
it and works with `no`. Position matters. This is not a rule that two negatives
always cancel, nor a rule that they always work together in every language.
Check the variety, construction, and intended meaning.

"Not every student arrived" allows some students to have arrived. "No student
arrived" does not. What exactly does *not* apply to? That question is **scope**.
Counting negative words cannot answer it. Put the calculator away, detective.

## A sentence can carry another little sentence

- "I know **[that they left]**": this gives what is known, a **complement clause**.
- "The book **[that I bought]**": this describes the book, a **relative clause**.
- "We left **[when the rain started]**": this gives the time relation, an **adverbial clause**.

Languages have several tools for joining these pieces, including different
verb forms, turning a clause into a noun-like expression, or chaining clauses.
The describing clause can come before its noun, as often in Japanese, or after
it, as normally in English. A missing participant's role might be shown by a
gap, pronoun, or other marker. Do not invent an English-like *that* just because
the translation contains one.

A related question is how many and which core participants a predicate takes:
**valency**. A **causative** introduces someone causing an event. A **passive**
changes how existing participants appear in the grammar, as with our window
example. These constructions can alter participant requirements or expression;
they are not just swapping two nouns in an English translation.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes sorting "Did you buy a book?" and "What did you buy?" into
the two question types. Find the object position in Mandarin. Compare where
the negative words sit in Spanish. Then make your own English examples with
a knowing clause, a book-describing clause, and a time clause. Bracket the
inside clauses and mark which examples are yours versus cited language forms.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Universals require narrowly defined domains (optional)</summary>

"All questions work this way" can fail because it mixes yes/no questions,
content questions, and special contexts. The same goes for negatives and
describing clauses. An **implicational universal** says "if a language has A,
it also has B." One reliable exception challenges the exceptionless version.
"These patterns often go together" is a weaker, statistical claim, not a ban
on other possibilities.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Yes/no and who/what questions may work differently even in the same language.
2. Find how negatives work together and what they apply to; don't just count words.
3. Inside clauses can supply a missing piece, describe a noun, or add a time or condition.

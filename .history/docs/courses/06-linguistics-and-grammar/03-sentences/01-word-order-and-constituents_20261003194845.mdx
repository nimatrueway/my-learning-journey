"The cat saw the dog with binoculars."

Who has the binoculars? The cat watching the dog, or the unusually well-equipped
dog? Same words, same order, two pictures. Let's find out where the phrase belongs.

## Words travel in little groups

We can group words into units called **constituents**. *The dog* is a noun
phrase. *With binoculars* is a prepositional phrase. That second phrase can
tell us how the seeing happened, or which dog we're talking about. Here are
two simplified bracket pictures:

```text
[The cat] [saw [the dog] [with binoculars]]
  The cat used the binoculars.

[The cat] [saw [the dog [with binoculars]]]
  The dog had the binoculars.
```

These are **made-up English teaching examples**. Different theories can draw
the structure differently, but our immediate question is simple: what does
*with binoculars* describe?

Try replacing *the dog with binoculars* with *it*. That helps test whether the
whole phrase acts as one unit when it describes the dog. You can also test
moving or joining groups of words. Use several tests: one failed movement
could reflect another rule, not prove that the group does not exist.

The main word that helps determine a phrase's kind and behavior is its **head**.
Other pieces are its **dependents**. In a basic noun-phrase analysis, *dog* is
the head. We will need that idea when comparing which side extra pieces go on.

## Six ways to arrange who, action, and what

For a basic sentence like "John read the letter," use `S` for subject,
`V` for verb, and `O` for object. All six orders below occur in the world.
WALS compares ordinary statement clauses with full noun phrases, using
doer-like and affected-participant roles to keep the comparison consistent.

| Order | One comparison case in WALS 81 |
| --- | --- |
| SOV | Japanese |
| SVO | Mandarin |
| VSO | Irish |
| VOS | Nias |
| OVS | Hixkaryana |
| OSV | Nadëb |

The Japanese example in that chapter is *John ga tegami o yon-da*, "John read
the letter." `ga` and `o` mark the participant phrases; `yon-da` is "read-PST."
Read `[John ga]` and `[tegami o]` as full phrases, not as four participants.
The particles are doing marking jobs, not adding extra people to the story.

A three-letter label is a starting point, not a recipe for every sentence.
Questions, clauses inside other clauses, missing participants, and helper
verbs can change the order. German, for example, has verb-second main-clause
patterns and verb-final patterns in subordinate clauses. One badge cannot
replace those rules. The cat's "syntax complete" stamp is going back in the drawer.

## What else goes before or after what?

English puts *in* before *the house*: a **preposition**. A language can put a
similar relation word after its noun phrase: a **postposition**. Also ask
where adjectives, possessors, and describing clauses go. Some orders often
occur together across languages, but these are tendencies, not laws that
every phrase must follow. Heads need not all go on the same side.

Order can also help show what we're talking about, the **topic**, and what
we're highlighting against other possibilities, the **focus**. Neither simply
means subject. A language with flexible order can use it for these jobs while
case or agreement tracks participants. "Flexible" does not mean "shuffle
everything and hope for the best."

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes drawing the two binocular scenes and adding brackets to
show their phrase groups. Label the two participant phrases and verb in the
Japanese example. Finally, name two things "SOV" cannot tell you: where
adjectives go, how questions work, or whether a participant can be left out.

## Recap 🐾

1. Different phrase groups can give the same words different meanings.
2. SOV and similar labels describe a chosen clause pattern, not the entire grammar.
3. Ask where other phrase pieces go and what order does in the conversation.

## Variation is part of the system

A **dialect** is a language variety associated with a community or region,
including grammar and vocabulary. An **accent** concerns pronunciation.
Everyone has both a variety and pronunciation patterns; "only other people
have dialects" mistakes social familiarity for linguistic neutrality.
**Register** concerns patterns associated with activities and situations,
such as casual conversation versus a formal report.

**Sociolinguistics** investigates how linguistic variation relates to social
contexts and communities. A speaker can alternate forms systematically rather
than speaking inconsistently. For example, a contrast between conversational
and formal negative constructions should be compared by context, not averaged
into a grammar that nobody actually uses.

## Change is not decay

Sounds shift, words acquire senses, endings disappear, and constructions
develop new functions. **Grammaticalization** involves items or constructions
developing grammatical functions, often alongside changes in form and distribution.
English *going to* can express future-related meaning without literal motion,
as in "It is going to rain." Its reduced conversational form is not a reason
to declare that the language has lost its ability to express time.

**Analogy** reshapes forms based on other patterns. **Reanalysis** changes
how a structure is interpreted. A synchronic description models a system at
a chosen period; a diachronic account models change across periods. A modern
word's history can explain how it arose without being its current meaning.
This is especially important for idioms and grammaticalized expressions.

## Contact produces more than loanwords

**Borrowing** can involve vocabulary and structural patterns. **Code-switching**
alternates languages or varieties in discourse and can itself be systematic.
It is not automatically a symptom of deficient knowledge. Shared structures
may reflect inheritance, contact, independent development, or several together.
Establish plausible histories instead of treating geographical adjacency as proof.

**Pidgins** emerge in particular contact situations and typically have restricted
initial domains of use. **Creoles** develop into community languages through
complex histories, not one identical recipe everywhere. Haitian Creole and
Tok Pisin are useful comparison cases, but do not classify their whole grammars
as broken French or English. A lexifier supplies much vocabulary; it does not
provide a complete explanation of all syntax, morphology, or community history.
The boundaries of these labels and formation accounts are debated.

## Modality is not ancestry

ASL, BSL, and other natural signed languages are not universal pantomime or
spoken languages with each word replaced by a gesture. They have their own
lexicons and grammars. Their histories can involve transmission, contact,
schools, and local communities; surrounding national languages are not their
automatic genealogical parents. Deaf and signing communities must be part of
the evidence and interpretation, not just a source of movements to label in English.

## Writing is another independent layer

An **alphabet** represents consonants and vowels; an **abjad** primarily
represents consonants; an **abugida** organizes consonant signs with vowel
modifications; a **syllabary** represents syllabic units. **Morphosyllabic**
systems associate characters with syllables and morphemes, as in Chinese writing.
These are approximate structural classifications with mixed cases, not stages
on a ladder of progress. Japanese combines kanji and kana, among other resources.

A language can be written in more than one script. Many languages have primarily
oral or signed transmission, and lack of a widely used writing system does
not mean lack of grammar. Language, orthography, and literacy practices should
be studied together without equating them.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes rewriting "Language X has no grammar" into three testable
questions: which variety, which construction, and which evidence? Then create
a sampling plan with a casual and formal context. For signed languages,
describe how you would preserve manual and nonmanual information and involve
community expertise, rather than collecting only English translations.

Language endangerment concerns disrupted transmission and pressures on use,
not structural inferiority. Documentation, education, and revitalization have
different aims. Recordings require informed consent and clear decisions about
access, reuse, attribution, and community control. A public grammar does not
make all cultural knowledge public, and a small sample of convenient speakers
can systematically miss important varieties and contexts.

## Recap 🐾

1. Varieties and registers are systematic; a standard is not an entire language.
2. Change and contact alter patterns without making languages primitive or defective.
3. Signed modality, scripts, ancestry, and community practices are distinct dimensions.

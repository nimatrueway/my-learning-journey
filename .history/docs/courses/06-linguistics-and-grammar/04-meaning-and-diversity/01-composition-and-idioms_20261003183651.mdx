## What compositionality actually claims

**Semantics** studies linguistic meaning. The **principle of compositionality**
holds that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings
of its parts and how they are structurally combined. It is not "add up the
dictionary definitions in order." *The child followed the dog* and *The dog
followed the child* share words but assign participants differently. Structure matters.

A new expression such as *the purple notebook under the chair* can be understood
without having been memorized as a whole. The lexical meanings and their
combination do productive work. A **non-compositional expression**, relative
to the ordinary meanings of its parts and ordinary combination rules, has a
conventional interpretation those resources alone do not predict.

This does not mean it has "no grammar" or that all of its internal structure
is irrelevant. An analysis can assign a lexical meaning to a multiword unit
or use a construction-specific interpretation, then let that larger unit
compose normally with the rest of a sentence. The unit at which meaning is
stored is part of the scientific question.

## A small cross-language tour

These are established expressions, not quotations from conversations. Literal
renderings and explanations are teaching paraphrases; the linked dictionary
entries below document the conventional senses.

| Language | Expression | Rough literal rendering | Conventional interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| English | *kick the bucket* | strike a bucket with the foot | die, in an informal idiomatic use |
| French | *donner sa langue au chat* | give one's tongue to the cat | stop trying to guess the answer |
| Spanish | *tomar el pelo* | take the hair | tease or fool someone |
| Japanese | *neko o kaburu*, 猫を被る | wear a cat | feign innocence |
| Mandarin | *mǎmǎhūhū*, 马马虎虎 | horse–horse–tiger–tiger | careless, or so-so in relevant uses |

An idiom can have a literal reading in a suitable context too. Idiomatic
meaning, register, and interpersonal effect matter: knowing a translation
does not make an expression appropriate for every situation. These examples
show a comparison method, not evidence that every language has these exact
idiom types or that an entire culture thinks in their literal images.

## Not every surprising phrase is non-compositional

**Idioms** have conventionalized meanings with varying transparency and
syntactic flexibility. **Collocations** are conventional combinations, such
as English *strong tea*: the combination is preferred, but the meaning is
largely transparent. Frequent co-occurrence alone does not establish semantic opacity.

**Metaphor** can be creative and understood through contextual mapping, as
when a negotiator is called "a bulldozer." Some metaphors become conventional
idioms; not every new metaphor must be stored as a fixed expression.
**Pragmatics** studies how context and communicative action contribute to
interpretation. "It is cold here" can be a request to close a window without
*cold* having a dictionary sense "please close the window."

**Polysemy** is related multiple senses of a lexical item. **Structural
ambiguity** comes from different combinations or attachments. Both can
complicate interpretation without showing that a whole phrase is an idiom.
Do not use "non-compositional" as a catch-all for anything difficult to translate.

## Test flexibility rather than assuming total fixedness

Compare replacing a lexical part, changing tense, adding a modifier, and
changing voice. English *kick the bucket* can inflect its verb, but replacing
*bucket* with *pail* does not ordinarily preserve its conventional idiomatic
sense. A passive such as "the bucket was kicked" normally favors a literal
reading outside special contexts. Other idioms allow more flexibility. These
are diagnostic tendencies to test with speakers and corpora, not a universal
rule that idioms must be grammatically frozen.

Non-compositionality can also arise in grammaticalized combinations and
lexicalized words, not only colorful sayings. In such cases, the ordinary
functions of components may fail to predict the current whole. Historical
motivation, current transparency, and current grammatical distribution are
three separate questions.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes explaining the French and Japanese idioms without using
their literal images as the definition. For one idiom, propose a lexical
substitution and a tense change; predict which preserves the conventional
sense and mark what needs speaker evidence. Then compare *strong tea* and
*It is cold here*: is the issue preferred wording, conventional opacity, or
an inference from the situation?

<summary>🤓 Non-compositional grammar and analytical uncertainty (optional)</summary>

In WALS chapter 115, Haspelmath discusses Chalcatongo Mixtec negative indefinite
expressions and considers them non-compositional while explicitly noting that
more information would be needed to confirm the analysis. That is a grammatical
case, not merely a proverb. The lesson is methodological: even familiar-looking
negative components may not license an automatic interpretation or segmentation.
Whether an expression is compositional depends on the lexical entries and
construction rules assumed, so a theory can localize exceptional meaning rather
than abandoning productive composition altogether.

## Recap 🐾

1. Compositional meaning uses the meanings of parts and their structural combination.
2. Idioms can require conventional whole-expression meaning while retaining internal grammar.
3. Collocation, metaphor, ambiguity, and pragmatic inference are distinct diagnostic possibilities.

Do you talk to a friend exactly as you write a formal report? Probably not.
If our language tour only collected reports, we would miss a lot of your grammar.

Languages belong to people in different places and situations. Let's put those
people back into the picture instead of treating the textbook as everybody's voice.

## Everyone has a variety, including you

An **accent** is about pronunciation. A **dialect** includes a community's or
region's grammar and vocabulary too. These are not things only other people
have. Familiar speech is not magically variety-free.

Your casual conversation and formal report may also use different patterns.
That situation-related variation is **register**. Studying how language
patterns relate to communities and social contexts is **sociolinguistics**.
Switching forms can follow a system. Compare casual and formal negatives in
their actual settings, rather than averaging them into a grammar nobody uses.

## Change is not decay

"It is going to rain." Is rain walking somewhere? No: *going to* can mark a
future-related meaning without literal movement. When words or constructions
develop grammatical jobs, we call it **grammaticalization**. Their form and
uses can change too. A reduced conversational form does not mean English
has forgotten how time works.

Sounds shift, endings disappear, and words acquire meanings. Forms can be
reshaped to match another pattern, **analogy**, or a structure can be understood
in a new way, **reanalysis**. A **synchronic** description is a snapshot of a
period; a **diachronic** account follows changes over time. Knowing where a
word came from does not automatically tell us what it means today. Remember
that when someone offers a neat origin story for an idiom.

## Contact produces more than loanwords

Languages can borrow words and structural patterns from neighbors. Speakers
may alternate languages or varieties in a conversation: **code-switching**.
That can be systematic, not a sign of poor knowledge. A shared feature could
come from a common ancestor, contact, separate developments, or several of
these together. Being neighbors gives us a lead to investigate, not proof.

**Pidgins** emerge in particular contact settings, usually with limited early
uses. **Creoles** develop as community languages through varied, complex
histories; there is no single recipe for all of them. Haitian Creole and Tok
Pisin are worth studying, not dismissing as "broken French" or "broken English."
A major vocabulary-source language is called a **lexifier**. It does not
explain the entire grammar or community history. Researchers debate the
boundaries of these labels and how different systems formed.

## Signing is a language mode, not a family tree

ASL and BSL are not one universal set of gestures, or English words replaced
by hand movements. They have their own vocabularies, grammars, and histories.
Schools, communities, transmission, and contact matter; the nearby spoken
language is not automatically their ancestor. Work with Deaf and signing
communities to understand the structure, not just collect movements and paste
English labels onto them.

## The writing system is another question

What does a written sign represent? That gives us a useful starting comparison:

- Consonants and vowels: an **alphabet**.
- Mainly consonants: an **abjad**.
- Consonant signs with vowel modifications: an **abugida**.
- Syllables: a **syllabary**.
- Syllables and morphemes together, as in Chinese writing: **morphosyllabic** writing.

Real systems can mix tools. Japanese combines kanji and kana, among other
resources. These labels describe structure, not steps from "primitive" to
"advanced." No writing system wins a trophy for being the final stage.

A language can use more than one script, or be passed on mainly through speech
or signing. No widely used writing system does not mean no grammar. Study the
language, spelling conventions, and reading/writing practices together, but
don't mistake them for the same thing.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes replacing "Language X has no grammar" with useful questions:
which variety did we study, which construction did we test, and what evidence
did we collect? Plan one casual and one formal sample. For signed languages,
explain how you would keep hand and nonmanual information and involve community
expertise, rather than recording only English translations.

An endangered language faces pressures on use and transmission, not inferior
grammar. Recording it, teaching it, and supporting renewed use are different
goals. Get informed consent and agree on access, reuse, credit, and community
control. A published grammar does not make all cultural knowledge public.
Convenient samples can also miss important speakers and situations.

## Recap 🐾

1. Different communities and situations have patterns; one standard is not the whole language.
2. Change and contact do not make a language broken or primitive.
3. Signing, writing, family history, and community practices are separate questions to study.

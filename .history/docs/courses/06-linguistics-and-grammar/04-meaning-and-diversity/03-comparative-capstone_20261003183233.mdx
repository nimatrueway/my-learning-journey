## A profile is a bundle of scoped claims

Choose a language or variety from the [world-language atlas](../world-language-atlas.md).
Do not try to fit it into one box. Make a profile with separate fields for
ancestry, community and period, phonology, morphology, clause structure,
grammatical categories, and interpretation.

For each field, record a **claim**, an **example or source location**, a
**scope**, and a **confidence level**. "SOV in the cited neutral declaratives"
is more defensible than "always SOV." "No overt case contrast in these nouns"
is different from "no way to identify participants." Keep "not documented"
separate from "demonstrated absent."

| Profile field | A useful evidence question |
| --- | --- |
| Genealogy and contact | Which family proposal and version? Which borrowing history? |
| Phonology | Which minimal pair or distribution supports a contrast? |
| Morphology | Which paradigm supports segmentation and category packaging? |
| Syntax and alignment | Which clause type, argument type, and marking behavior? |
| Categories | What is obligatory, optional, or supplied by context? |
| Meaning | Which expressions compose regularly, and which need conventional meanings? |

For the non-compositional field, include the expression, literal rendering,
conventional sense, register, and a substitution or inflection test. A dictionary
can establish an attested sense without telling you every syntactic constraint.

## Test a deliberately small claim

The following is **invented comparative data**, not a survey of actual languages.
`VO` means verb before object; `OV` means object before verb for the construction
sampled. "Prepositions" means the specified dominant adposition strategy under
the exercise's coding. Family and area labels are also fictional.

| Language | Family | Area | Order | Prepositions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | F1 | North | VO | Yes |
| B | F1 | North | VO | Yes |
| C | F1 | North | VO | Yes |
| D | F2 | South | OV | No |
| E | F3 | East | VO | No |
| F | F4 | West | OV | No |
| G | F5 | North | VO | Unknown |

The exceptionless hypothesis "Every VO language has prepositions" is
contradicted by E under these definitions. G is missing evidence, not another
counterexample and not another confirming case. A statistical tendency is
a different claim: in the six rows with known values, three of four VO
languages have prepositions, compared with zero of two OV languages.

But A, B, and C are closely related and colocated. Their matching features
may be inherited or connected through contact rather than three independent
outcomes. A crude one-per-family illustration retaining A, D, E, and F changes
the VO proportion from three of four to one of two. That is not a corrected
estimate of the world: it merely demonstrates that sampling choices change results.

## What makes a scientific comparison stronger?

Define categories before counting. Identify the variety and construction.
Sample across genealogical groups and regions, and document sources and
coding choices. Analyze family and area dependence rather than simply adding
more convenient languages. Report uncertainty and counterexamples, and check
whether alternative definitions change the conclusion.

Large databases help organize evidence but do not remove these responsibilities.
WALS features have different coverage and definitions. Joining two feature
tables can change your sample because only some languages have both values.
Glottolog supplies genealogical classifications and bibliographies, not an
automatic grammatical description of every member of a family.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend twelve minutes on two tasks. First, reproduce the two VO proportions
above and explain why E and G have different evidential status. Second, choose
two languages from different atlas regions and draft their profiles for
genealogy, word order, morphology, and one meaning feature. Use the lessons'
examples where applicable and mark unknowns honestly. Finish with one common
attribute, one contrasting attribute, and a question that needs better evidence.

<details>
<summary>🤓 From a descriptive count to an explanatory model (optional)</summary>

An association may reflect inheritance, contact, processing pressures, shared
development pathways, or coding decisions. Statistical models can account for
some family and area structure, but model assumptions must be evaluated too.
A **unique** feature requires evidence against all relevant alternatives;
"rare in this sample" is much weaker and usually more defensible. The fixture's
small proportions are teaching calculations, not inferential tests or estimated
probabilities for undocumented languages.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. A language profile separates features and records scope, provenance, and uncertainty.
2. Universals, statistical tendencies, counterexamples, and missing values are different claims.
3. Family, area, coverage, and coding can change a comparison's apparent results.

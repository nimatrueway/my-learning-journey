English uses two words for *will walk*, but one for *walked*. Same language,
different packing choices. Some grammar travels in a separate bag; some gets
zipped into the word itself. Let's check the luggage. 🧩

## Meet the word-building kit

Start with *walk*, then add `-ed`. The core is the **root**; the added piece is
an **affix**. An affix before the base is a prefix, one after it is a suffix,
and one inside it is an infix. Studying word structure is **morphology**.

*Walk → walked* gives another grammatical form of the same verb: **inflection**.
*Happy → happiness* builds a new word, here turning an adjective into a noun:
**derivation**. Useful starting labels, though some cases need a closer look.

## Two questions, not one language box

First: **how much grammar goes inside the word?** Separate words, as in *will
walk*, are a more **analytic** strategy. Building grammar into a word, as in
*walked*, is more **synthetic**. This dimension is called **synthesis**.
**Isolating** describes a tendency toward very little word-building, roughly
one morpheme per word. Analytic is broader: a separate helper verb can still
have its own endings.

Second: **does each piece do one job, or several?** Separately identifiable
pieces with separate jobs suggest an **agglutinative** pattern. One piece
doing several jobs suggests a **fusional** pattern. The technical question is
**exponence**: how forms express grammatical categories.

A language can mix these strategies. We are inspecting constructions, not
assigning each language one permanent luggage policy.

## Tiny comparisons with real forms

The first two examples below illustrate familiar analyses; the Latin form
is also given in the Leipzig Glossing Rules. These are word forms, not full sentences.

```text
Turkish: ev-ler-de
         house-PL-LOC
         'in the houses'

Latin:   insul-arum
         island-GEN.PL
         'of the islands'

English: will walk / walk-ed
         separate auxiliary / walk-PST
```

In Turkish, `-ler` says "plural" and `-de` marks location, called **locative**.
Two pieces, two jobs. Sound patterns such as vowel harmony can change how
endings look; Turkish plural is not always spelled `-ler`.

Latin `-arum` puts genitive and plural into one ending. Two jobs, one piece.
English shows separate-word and built-in strategies in the same tiny example.
Count pieces you have evidence for, not letters or syllables.

## Beyond a string of suffixes

Endings are only one tool. Arabic often combines consonant roots with patterns
of vowels and other material: **root-and-pattern morphology**. Indonesian uses
repeated material, called **reduplication**, for several jobs, not always a
required plural. English *sing/sang* changes the stem. *Go/went* swaps in a very
different stem: **suppletion**. Not every grammar job has a little piece you
can peel off like a sticker.

Kalaallisut and Mohawk can fit extensive grammatical structure inside words,
often including markers for participants. This brings us to **polysynthesis**,
whose exact definition varies. Some languages also put a noun stem inside a
larger predicate: **noun incorporation**. That is a word-building pattern,
not just deleting spaces. A long word or compound alone does not prove either
claim. Character count is not a grammar test.

where the evidence lets you. Which one refuses to be *go* plus a tidy ending?

Researchers need a precise counting rule. WALS chapter 22 counts grammatical

## Recap 🐾

1. Ask how much grammar goes inside a word, then ask how many jobs each piece does.
2. Analytic, agglutinative, fusional, and polysynthetic describe patterns, not pure language species.
3. Grammar can use endings, changed stems, repeated pieces, and other word-building tools.

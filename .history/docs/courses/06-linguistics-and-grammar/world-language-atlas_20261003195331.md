Pick a stop. Ask a question. Follow a surprising pattern. This atlas gives
you **35 places to start**, with the field cat handling absolutely none of
the passport paperwork. 🌍🐾

Regions help us plan the tour; they are not language families. Communities
cross borders, and languages travel.

⏱️ **~8 min** · optional reference; read once, revisit often.

No memorization exam here. Each row gives a family or branch and a question
worth exploring, not a complete grammar or a claim of uniqueness. Before
using a row as research data, check the specific variety, examples, and sources.

## Europe and the Caucasus

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English | Germanic, Indo-European | Why does *she* become *her*, while *the child* stays the same? Compare pronouns and full nouns. |
| Spanish | Romance, Indo-European | How do verbs change? When can the subject be left out? How do negative words work together? |
| Finnish | Finnic, Uralic | Compare case endings with English words like *in*. Which place relationships get distinct forms? |
| Basque | Isolate | Which participants get ergative case? Do the sentence rules group participants the same way? |
| Georgian | Kartvelian | What information fits on the verb? Does participant marking change with the construction? |

English and Spanish are related but differ in many details. Finnish and Basque
can share selected structures without belonging to that family. For alignment
criteria, use [WALS chapter 98](https://wals.info/chapter/98).

## Africa and neighboring Arabic-speaking regions

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arabic | Semitic, Afro-Asiatic | How do consonant roots and word patterns combine? Compare a named spoken variety with standardized Arabic. |
| Hausa | Chadic, Afro-Asiatic | How does tone work here? Being related to Arabic does not make the grammar a copy of Arabic. |
| Swahili | Bantu, Atlantic-Congo | Which words change to agree with a noun's class? Look beyond the noun's own ending. |
| Yoruba | Atlantic-Congo | What meanings change with tone? Grammar does not need lots of suffixes to do plenty of work. |
| Zulu | Bantu, Atlantic-Congo | Compare noun classes with Swahili and investigate click consonants and contact history. |

Atlantic-Congo is commonly grouped within broader Niger-Congo proposals, but
check how a source defines the group and its branches. "Khoisan" is not one
established family just because some languages have clicks. See [WALS chapter 1](https://wals.info/chapter/1)
for inventory and contact examples and [chapter 13](https://wals.info/chapter/13) for tone.

## South, west, and central Asia

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persian | Iranian, Indo-European | When does the verb go last? Which objects get *rā*? Compare Spanish *a* without assuming identical rules. |
| Hindi | Indo-Aryan, Indo-European | How do aspect and participant type affect case and agreement? Test more than one construction. |
| Tamil | Dravidian | Explore endings, case, and retroflex sounds made with the tongue curled or drawn back. Compare neighbors without merging families. |
| Turkish | Turkic | Separate suffixes in *ev-ler-de*; investigate vowel harmony, evidentiality, and clause structure independently. |
| Mongolian | Mongolic | Case, suffixing, and verb-final organization can resemble Turkish without proving shared ancestry. |

Turkish and Mongolian can look similar, but shared endings or word order do
not settle ancestry. "Altaic" proposals grouping these and sometimes other
families are disputed. Revisit
[families, types, and neighbors](./01-scientific-foundations/02-classifying-languages.mdx)
and [grammatical categories](./02-sounds-and-words/03-grammatical-categories.mdx).

## East and southeast Asia

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandarin | Sinitic, Sino-Tibetan | Explore tone, counting classifiers, aspect, and question words that stay in place. Few endings does not mean little grammar. |
| Cantonese | Sinitic, Sino-Tibetan | Compare tone and final particles with Mandarin. Shared ancestry does not make the spoken systems identical. |
| Japanese | Japonic | Explore verb-last patterns, describing clauses before nouns, sound length, and the idiom *neko o kaburu*. |
| Korean | Koreanic | How do sentence endings mark speech levels and respect? Similarity to Japanese does not prove shared ancestry. |
| Vietnamese | Austroasiatic | Compare its tones and use of separate words with related Khmer. Relatives can differ. |
| Thai | Kra-Dai | Tone and counting classifiers also appear in other families. Which constructions match, and which differ? |
| Khmer | Austroasiatic | A non-tonal system contrasts with Vietnamese within the broader family. |

"Chinese" can mean related varieties or particular standards. Name the spoken
variety and writing conventions you are studying. Researchers still debate
some branches within Sino-Tibetan. Revisit
[sounds and signs](./02-sounds-and-words/01-sound-and-sign-systems.mdx) and
[non-compositional expressions](./04-meaning-and-diversity/01-composition-and-idioms.mdx).

## Islands, Australia, and New Guinea

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indonesian | Malayic, Austronesian | Explore added word pieces, repetition, and *kita/kami*: does we include the listener? |
| Māori | Polynesian, Austronesian | How do verb-first clauses compare with Indonesian patterns within the same broad family? |
| Malagasy | Austronesian | VOS patterns show that ancestry is not a location label: an Austronesian language is spoken in Madagascar. |
| Warlpiri | Pama-Nyungan | With flexible phrase order and case marking, what does changing the order do in conversation? Flexible is not random. |
| Rotokas | North Bougainville | What can a small set of consonants tell us? Not that the whole language is simple. |

Older WALS text calls Rotokas's family "West Bougainville"; catalogue labels
can differ. "Papuan" is a geographical umbrella, not one proven family.
Australia and New Guinea both have multiple families. See
[WALS chapter 1](https://wals.info/chapter/1) and verify current labels in
[Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/).

## The Americas

| Stop | Family or branch | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navajo | Athabaskan, within Na-Dene | Explore tone and rich verb structure, far from our East Asian stops. Similar features cross continents. |
| Inuktitut | Inuit, Eskimo-Aleut | How do word pieces pack together meanings? Count supported pieces, not spaces in an English translation. |
| K'iche' | Mayan | Compare ergative patterns with Basque and Hindi. Check noun forms, verb markers, and sentence rules separately. |
| Hixkaryana | Cariban | Study object–verb–subject clauses. Not every language puts subjects first; check the WALS example's scope. |
| Southern Quechua varieties | Quechuan | Explore endings for person, evidence, and conversation. Name the particular variety rather than treating all as identical. |
| Guaraní | Tupian | How does nasality spread across sounds? This nasal harmony pattern may not match written letter boundaries. |

"Amerindian" is not one demonstrated family encompassing these stops. English,
Spanish, and Portuguese also belong to the continent's language landscape, but
neither their spread nor this table represents its full diversity. Read
[WALS chapter 81](https://wals.info/chapter/81) for Hixkaryana's order example and
[chapter 98](https://wals.info/chapter/98) for alignment distinctions.

## Signed-language communities

| Stop | Historical anchor | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| American Sign Language (ASL) | Linked to French Sign Language traditions | What do handshape, movement, location, orientation, and facial or body actions do? Not just English in signs. |
| British Sign Language (BSL) | British sign-language lineage | Compare it with ASL. Nearby spoken English does not guarantee that their signers understand each other. |

Not all signed languages are one family. Glottolog's
["Sign Language" pseudo-family](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/sign1238)
is an organizational heading, not evidence of ancestry. ASL and BSL do not
represent all village and emerging sign languages. Consult
[Essentials on signed articulation](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/3-7-signed-language-articulators/)
and [signed phonology](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/4-10-signed-language-phonology/).

## Shared, distinctive, or unique?

Mandarin, Yoruba, and Navajo all use tone, but that does not put them in one
family. English and Persian are relatives, yet differ in typical word order.
Shared features do not prove ancestry; ancestry does not fix every feature.
Word-building, gender, and alignment are things to compare separately.

"Unusual among these examples" is a modest claim. **Unique in the world**
needs much more evidence, including varieties and unsettled analyses. An
unfamiliar structure is still ordinary communication for its users, not
an exhibit labeled "look how strange these people are."

## Forgot a term? Here's a quick reminder

- **Phoneme / allophone:** a sound category that distinguishes words / a version of that category.
- **Morpheme / allomorph:** an analyzed meaning-bearing piece / an alternative form of that piece.
- **Analytic / synthetic:** grammar in more separate words / more grammar built into words.
- **Agglutinative / fusional:** more separate pieces with separate jobs / pieces that bundle jobs together.
- **Polysynthetic:** extensive grammar inside words; definitions vary, and long words alone do not prove it.
- **SOV, SVO, VSO:** subject, object, and verb order for the clause type checked, not every sentence.
- **Accusative / ergative alignment:** S groups with A / S groups with P in the behavior tested.
- **Tense / aspect / mood:** when / what view of the event / grammatical possibilities and related meanings.
- **Evidentiality:** how the speaker knows, marked in grammar; not a guarantee of truth.
- **Gender / noun class / classifier:** grouping systems with different rules; agreement classes are not counting classifiers.
- **Clusivity:** does we include the listener?
- **Compositional / non-compositional:** ordinary parts and rules predict the meaning / the whole needs a learned meaning they do not predict.

These are reminders, not substitutes for the lessons' tests and caveats.

## Extend the itinerary to any other language

1. Find the **specific variety** in [Glottolog's catalogue](https://glottolog.org/glottolog/language). Note other names, its stable identifier, the classification version, and uncertainty. A country name or similar spelling is not enough.
2. Follow the bibliography to a grammar or community resource. Look for actual examples, sets of word forms, and limits on the description, not stereotypes.
3. Find relevant [WALS features](https://wals.info/feature). Read what each feature means and which variety was checked. Blank means no value recorded, not that the feature is absent.
4. Make the [capstone profile](./04-meaning-and-diversity/03-comparative-capstone.mdx). Compare one shared feature and one difference. Keep family, area, and speaking/signing mode separate.

For contact languages, add [APiCS](https://apics-online.info/) to the shelf.
For glosses, use the [Leipzig Glossing Rules](https://www.eva.mpg.de/lingua/resources/glossing-rules.php).
Taking the tour to an unlisted language is part of the adventure.

## Sources and scope

This is an original guide built from the lessons' concepts and examples.
Family labels follow common classifications found in Glottolog; disputed
groups and older WALS labels are flagged. Linked WALS chapters explain selected
features and examples, not every claim in the table. To use a row as research

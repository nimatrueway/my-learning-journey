"Of the islands" takes three English words. Latin can fit it into one.

Where did the other words go? Nowhere! Latin packs the meaning differently.
Let's open the word and see what is inside. 🐱🔬

## Three lines, three useful views

This Latin example comes from the Leipzig Glossing Rules. Read it from top
to bottom; the explanation is ours:

```text
insul-arum
island-GEN.PL
'of the islands'
```

The top line gives the Latin form, split into pieces. `insul-` is the stem
for "island." The ending `-arum` does two jobs: it marks plural and, here,
an "of" relationship. That second job is called **genitive**.

The middle line is a **gloss**: a short label for each piece. `GEN.PL` says
"genitive and plural, both in this one piece." The bottom line is the natural
English translation. It tells you the meaning, not how many Latin words exist.

A small piece with a word-building or meaning job is called a **morpheme**.
It need not be a whole word or have its own separate English translation.
That's why a gloss is useful: it shows the proposed pieces and jobs instead
of quietly replacing the grammar with English.

## Those tiny punctuation marks are doing work

| Notation | What it normally communicates |
| --- | --- |
| `root-affix` / `ROOT-LABEL` | A proposed split into pieces; match the hyphens on both lines |
| `word=clitic` | A proposed clitic attachment; match the equals signs on both lines |
| `GEN.PL` | Several labels for one source element |
| `1SG`, `2PL`, `3SG` | First-person singular, second-person plural, third-person singular |
| `PST`, `PRS`, `NEG`, `Q` | Past, present, negation, question marker |
| `NOM`, `ACC`, `ERG`, `ABS` | Case labels, explained in the alignment lesson |

A **clitic** leans on another word for its pronunciation but often acts more
like a separate word in sentence structure than an ordinary ending does.
Figuring that out takes evidence; a space or equals sign cannot settle it.

Keep the author's abbreviation key nearby. `S` might name the participant in
"the bird sleeps," not "singular." Labels can vary. The cat's rule: read the
key before confidently decoding the wrong thing.

## The bird gets a small wardrobe

Here is another **made-up dataset**. Compare the bird word in several forms.
A table of related forms like this is called a **paradigm**.

| Form | Supplied meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `lum` | bird |
| `lum-ta` | birds |
| `lum-ki` | of a bird |
| `lum-ta-ki` | of birds |

Now `-ta` looks like plural and `-ki` looks like genitive. In the last row,
plural comes first. Good! But have we tested `lum-ki-ta`? No. Do all nouns
work this way? Also unknown. A neat table can still leave big questions open.

You might see someone label singular with a **zero marker**: a grammatical
job with no spoken piece of its own. That is a claim about the wider system,
not a reason to add an invisible ending whenever a table has an empty slot.

Keep three things separate: what was said, what it meant in context, and how
you explain the pieces. A gloss belongs to that third part. Two researchers
can agree on the recording but disagree on its labels. Save the original and
where it came from, so someone else can check the explanation.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes labeling the pieces in `lum-ta-ki`, then give its natural
English translation. Compare it with `insul-arum`: where do the plural and
genitive jobs live in each? Ask for one new noun example to check each word
split. For our made-up bird, the expected gloss is `bird-PL-GEN`.

<details>
<summary>🤓 What a transcription leaves out (optional)</summary>

Ordinary spelling, a list of contrastive sounds, and a detailed pronunciation
record are different views. `/p/` usually names a phoneme; `[pʰ]` records
a pronunciation detail, here a puff of air. We will unpack those next.
In signed-language examples, `BOOK` is a label for a sign, not an English word
spoken by the signer. The label alone misses movement, handshape, facial
marking, and timing. For speech and sign alike, a translation cannot replace
the original recording.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. The original shows the form, the gloss labels its pieces, and the translation gives natural meaning.
2. Hyphens, equals signs, and dots tell you different things; they are not decoration.
3. Check labels against more forms and keep the original evidence, not just a tidy explanation.

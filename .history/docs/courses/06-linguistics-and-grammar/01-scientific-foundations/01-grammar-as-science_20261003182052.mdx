Someone says a sentence is "bad grammar." Do they mean it violates a publishing
style guide, differs from their dialect, or cannot occur in the variety being
studied? Those are three different claims. Linguistics starts by separating them.

## Describe the system before judging the style

**Descriptive grammar** models patterns in a language variety: which forms
occur, how they combine, and how people interpret them. **Prescriptive rules**
recommend forms for a particular institution, genre, or standard. Both can be
useful, but an editing convention is not evidence that other varieties lack
structure. A standard variety is socially privileged, not inherently more logical.

In several English varieties, "I didn't see nobody" has a single-negation
interpretation: no person was seen. This is **negative concord**. In formal
Standard English, "I didn't see anybody" is normally preferred for that meaning.
The scientific question asks which pattern a speaker's variety permits, in
which contexts. It does not deduce the meaning by treating each negative word
as a minus sign in arithmetic.

**Grammaticality** concerns conformity to a system; **acceptability** concerns
how a particular utterance is judged in a context. Memory load, familiarity,
register, and meaning can affect acceptability. Neither a teacher's dislike
nor one hesitant judgment settles a whole language's grammar.

## A tiny dataset, not an entire language

The following is **invented teaching data**, not a newly discovered language.
Hyphens show a proposed segmentation. Assume the supplied translations are
reliable observations in this exercise.

| Form | Translation |
| --- | --- |
| `dap` | one cat |
| `dap-na` | cats, more than one |
| `nim` | one dog |
| `nim-na` | dogs, more than one |

A compact hypothesis is that `-na` marks plural and `dap` and `nim` are noun
roots. It explains a repeated **form–meaning correspondence**. But four forms
do not establish that every noun pluralizes this way, that number is obligatory,
or that these forms can be used unchanged in sentences.

## What counts as evidence?

Use recordings of natural interaction, elicited examples, speaker judgments,
corpora, and experiments for different questions. Natural speech reveals use
in context; elicitation targets contrasts that may be rare in recordings.
Judgments can test proposed contrasts, but should include context and more
than one item or speaker where feasible. A corpus records what happened to
occur; it does not list every grammatical possibility.

An asterisk before a linguistic example often marks ungrammaticality in the
variety under discussion. It should report evidence or a stated analysis, not
the author's irritation. Ask whose variety, what interpretation, and which
method supports that mark. For an unfamiliar language, collaborate with its
speakers rather than translating your intuition from English.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes writing two hypotheses for the miniature dataset. For each,
write a prediction and an observation that would make you revise it. Then add
one missing contextual question: can a plural form refer to exactly two
animals, must it occur after a numeral, or can it be used for a generic kind?
Separate what the four rows demonstrate from what you only suspect.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Competing explanations and scientific models (optional)</summary>

Several models can fit a finite dataset. A suffix rule and a memorized list of
four forms both fit these rows, but they predict differently for a new root.
Productivity tests help distinguish them. A useful analysis balances explanatory
scope, explicit predictions, and complexity; "shortest explanation" alone is
not a guarantee of truth. Linguistic theories disagree about representations
and mechanisms while sharing the need to confront evidence.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Descriptive grammar models a variety; prescriptive rules serve particular social contexts.
2. A grammatical analysis must make predictions that new evidence could contradict.
3. Missing corpus examples, judgments, and translations require context before generalization.

"I didn't see nobody."

Your English teacher might reach for a red pen. A linguist reaches for a notebook:

## Are we editing, or investigating?

Imagine two useful books:

- An editor's guide says, "For this publication, write it this way."
- A linguist's notebook says, "These speakers use this pattern, with this meaning."

The first gives **prescriptive rules**: advice about what to use in a particular
setting. The second builds a **descriptive grammar**: an explanation of the
system people actually use. Editing rules have a place. They just cannot tell
us that everyone who speaks differently has no grammar.

Back to "I didn't see nobody." In several English varieties, it means that
no person was seen. The negative words work together. Linguists call that
**negative concord**. Formal Standard English usually prefers "I didn't see
anybody" for that meaning. The pattern differs; the speakers are not doing
arithmetic badly. Two negative words need not cancel like two minus signs!

One more useful split: **grammaticality** asks whether a sentence follows the
system. **Acceptability** asks whether someone finds it okay in a particular
situation. A sentence can be hard to follow because it is long or unfamiliar,
not because its grammar is broken. One raised eyebrow is a clue, not a verdict.

## Your first mini mystery

Here is a **made-up teaching dataset**, not a real language. The translations
are reliable for this exercise. The hyphens show where we think a word splits.

| Form | Translation |
| --- | --- |
| `dap` | one cat |
| `dap-na` | cats, more than one |
| `nim` | one dog |
| `nim-na` | dogs, more than one |

Spot the pattern? `dap` and `nim` name the animals. Add `-na` and there is more
than one. Our first guess: **`-na` makes a noun plural.**

That guess is a **hypothesis**: an explanation we can test. It fits four rows.
It does not yet tell us whether all nouns work this way, whether speakers must
mark plurals, or what happens inside a sentence. The cat has solved a small
mystery, not the entire language. Put the victory confetti down for a moment.

## What counts as evidence?

You have several tools. Each answers a slightly different question:

- **Listen to conversations:** what do people say in everyday situations?
- **Ask for examples:** can speakers show you a plural or a question? This is called **elicitation**.
- **Ask about a sentence:** does it work, with this meaning, in this situation?
- **Search a corpus:** a collection of recorded or written language. It shows what occurred, not everything that could occur.

Use more than one example and speaker where possible. Keep the context. And
when studying an unfamiliar language, work with its speakers. Your English
instincts do not get a free passport to every other grammar.

You will sometimes see an asterisk before an example. It often means "this
does not work in the variety we're describing." Ask what evidence supports
that label. An asterisk is not a fancy version of "I personally dislike this."

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes as the detective. Write two possible explanations for the
table. For each, add a prediction and a result that would make you change your
mind. Then ask one question the table cannot answer: can the plural refer to
exactly two animals? What happens after a number word? Mark what you know and
what you are guessing. Both belong in the notebook, on different lines.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Competing explanations and scientific models (optional)</summary>

Two explanations could fit our four rows: speakers might use a general ending,
or they might memorize each word separately. Try a new animal word. A working
ending predicts how speakers can build its plural; a four-word list does not.
Testing whether a pattern works on new words is a **productivity test**.
The shortest explanation is not always right. What matters is how well an
explanation handles both the examples you have and the next examples you collect.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Describing how people speak is a different job from recommending an editing style.
2. A useful grammar guess predicts something you can check, and could get wrong.
3. Keep the context: a missing example or one person's reaction does not settle a whole language.

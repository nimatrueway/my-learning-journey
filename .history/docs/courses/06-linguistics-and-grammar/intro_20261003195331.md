Why does one language put the verb last, another use pitch to change a word,
and another ask you to specify how you know something? And why would a French
speaker give their tongue to a cat?

Let's tour the world's grammar with a notebook and a curious field cat. 🌍🐱
You'll spot patterns, try tiny puzzles, and learn the names linguists give them.
English gets a seat on the bus, not the driver's seat.

We cannot learn every language in one short course, and many grammars are still
incompletely documented. What we **can** learn is how to ask useful questions
about any language: who is it related to, how does it build words and sentences,
and what makes it similar to or different from its neighbors?

**12 lessons · 4 modules · ~2 hr 17 min**, including practice and a brief
widget/quiz allowance. The [world-language atlas](./world-language-atlas.md)
adds 35 comparison stops and an optional ~8-minute reference read.

## Questions you'll be able to answer

- Are these languages relatives, structurally similar, or neighbors that
  influenced each other? Meet **genealogy**, **typology**, and **contact**.
- Which sound or sign differences change a word? How much grammar fits inside
  a word? How do sentences tell us who did what?
- What evidence would support your guess? Learn to read annotated examples,
  compare forms, and test a pattern instead of just trusting a translation.
- Why don't the words always predict the message? Explore **non-compositional
  expressions**, idioms, preferred word pairs, metaphors, and clues from context.
- Is a feature shared, unusual in this sample, or really unique? Learn why
  "often" and "always" are very different claims.
- What do signed languages and less familiar varieties teach us? They have
  systems to investigate too, not defective versions of somebody else's grammar.

## Bring curiosity, not prerequisites

You need no linguistics background. A notebook is plenty. Make a guess before
changing a widget, try the short exercise, and use the quiz feedback to check
your thinking. Open the optional folds when you want more detail.

We'll tell you which examples are real language forms and which are made-up
puzzles. One example never tells the whole story of a language or its speakers.

Times allow roughly 200 words per minute for the visible lesson, examples,
captions, and control text, rounded up, plus the stated practice time and one
minute for interaction. Code that wires up the page is not counted. Optional
folds and the atlas are extra. These are planning guides, not a stopwatch test.

## Open reference shelf

[Essentials of Linguistics, 2nd edition](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/)
is an open textbook when you want another explanation;
[WALS](https://wals.info/) lets you compare grammar features;
[Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/) helps you find languages, families, and sources;
and the [Leipzig Glossing Rules](https://www.eva.mpg.de/lingua/resources/glossing-rules.php)
explain those little labels under language examples. These are optional
companions, not required homework: the lessons explain their own terms and examples.

# The Aeneid

Virgil

## Before You Begin

The Aeneid is a long poem in Latin by Virgil, the most famous poet of ancient Rome. Virgil lived from 70 to 19 BC. He wrote the Aeneid during the rule of Augustus, the first Roman emperor, who had ended a long period of civil war. Virgil worked on the poem for about ten years. He died before he could finish his final revision, and some lines are still incomplete. A story says he asked for the poem to be burned, but Augustus ordered it to be published. It soon became the national poem of Rome.

The Aeneid continues the story of Homer's Iliad, the Greek poem about the Trojan War. Readers of this site can also find summaries of the Iliad and the Odyssey. But Virgil tells the story from the other side. Homer's heroes are mostly Greeks. Virgil's hero is a Trojan, one of the defeated people. His name is Aeneas. He is a prince of Troy and the son of the goddess Venus and a mortal man named Anchises. When the Greeks capture and burn Troy, Aeneas escapes with his father, his young son, and a small group of survivors. The gods tell him that it is his destiny, or fate, to sail to Italy. There he must build a new home for his people. From his descendants will come the Roman people and, much later, the city of Rome itself.

The poem has twelve parts, called books. Each section of this summary covers one book. The first six books tell of Aeneas's wanderings across the sea, a little like Homer's Odyssey. In them we see the storm that throws the Trojans onto the coast of Africa, the love story of Aeneas and Queen Dido of Carthage, the fall of Troy and the long journey told by Aeneas himself, funeral games in Sicily, and a journey to the land of the dead. The last six books tell of the war in Italy, a little like Homer's Iliad. The Trojans arrive in Latium, the region where Rome will later stand. They must fight the local peoples before they can settle there.

Because this is a Roman poem, this summary uses the Roman names of the gods, as Virgil and his translator do. These are the same gods that appear with Greek names in the site's Homer summaries. Jupiter is the Greek Zeus, Juno is Hera, Venus is Aphrodite, Neptune is Poseidon, Mercury is Hermes, Vulcan is Hephaestus, Minerva is Athena, and Mars is Ares. The Greek hero Odysseus is called Ulysses, and Achilles's son Neoptolemus is also called Pyrrhus.

Here are the main people you will meet. Aeneas is the hero. Virgil often calls him "good" or "dutiful" Aeneas. The Latin word is "pius", and the quality is "pietas", which means loyalty and duty to the gods, to your family, and to your country. Anchises is his old father, whom Aeneas carries out of burning Troy on his shoulders. Ascanius is his young son. He has a second name, Iulus, and Virgil links this name to the family of Julius Caesar and Augustus. Creusa is Aeneas's wife, who is lost on the night Troy falls. Achates is Aeneas's most faithful friend and companion.

In Africa, we meet Dido, the queen who is building the new city of Carthage, and her sister Anna. In Italy, we meet Latinus, the old king of the Latins, his daughter Lavinia, and his wife, Queen Amata. Turnus is the young and proud king of the Rutulian people. He wants to marry Lavinia, and he becomes Aeneas's greatest enemy. Evander is an old Greek king who lives on the hills where Rome will one day be built, and Pallas is his young son. Nisus and Euryalus are two close Trojan friends. Camilla is a warrior woman who fights on the side of the Italians. Mezentius is a cruel king who hates the gods, and Juturna is a water nymph, a minor goddess, who is the sister of Turnus.

The gods also play a big part in the story. Jupiter is the king of the gods, and he knows and protects what fate has decided. Juno, his wife and queen of the gods, hates the Trojans and does everything she can to stop or delay Aeneas. Venus, the goddess of love, is Aeneas's mother and always tries to help him. Neptune rules the sea. Mercury is the messenger of the gods. Vulcan is the god of fire and the smith of the gods, and he makes armour for Aeneas. Aeolus is the king of the winds, and Allecto is one of the Furies, terrible goddesses from the underworld who bring anger and war.

As you read, you may notice three big ideas. The first is fate: the future of Rome is fixed, and the gods and people can only delay it, not stop it. The second is duty, or "pietas": again and again, Aeneas must put his mission before his own wishes. The third is the cost of founding Rome: many people, including some of the most attractive characters in the poem, suffer and die so that Rome can exist. Virgil also links his story to his own time. Several times, the gods and spirits look forward to Augustus and his peace. This summary sometimes adds a short note marked as interpretation to explain such links, but the focus stays on the story.

This summary is based on the English prose translation by J. W. Mackail, first published in 1885 and now in the public domain. Mackail's English is old-fashioned, so this summary retells the story in simpler, modern words. You can read the full translation in [Project Gutenberg's edition of J. W. Mackail's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22456).

## Book 1: The Storm and Carthage

The poem opens with Virgil's own short statement of his subject. He will sing of war and of one man. This man left Troy as an exile, driven by fate, and came at last to Italy. On the way he suffered greatly on land and sea, because the goddess Juno never stopped being angry with him. In the end he founded a city and brought his gods to Latium, the region of Italy around the future Rome. From him came the Latin people, the kings of Alba Longa, and the high walls of Rome. Then Virgil asks the Muse, the goddess of poetry, a question: why was the queen of the gods so angry with such a good man? Can anger be so fierce in the hearts of gods?

The answer begins with Carthage. Carthage is a rich and warlike city on the coast of North Africa, built by settlers from the Phoenician city of Tyre. Juno loves Carthage more than any other place. She hopes it will one day rule the world. But she has heard of a prophecy, a message about the future from the gods. A people born from Trojan blood will one day destroy Carthage. Juno also has older reasons to hate the Trojans. She remembers the war she fought against Troy. She remembers the judgment of Paris, the Trojan prince who chose Venus as the most beautiful goddess instead of her. She also remembers Ganymede, a Trojan boy whom Jupiter took up to heaven and honoured. So for many years she has kept the Trojan survivors away from Italy. Virgil sums it up in one line: such a heavy task it was to found the Roman people.

The story itself starts in the middle of the journey. The Trojans have just left Sicily and are sailing happily toward Italy. Juno sees them and speaks angrily to herself. The goddess Minerva, she says, was allowed to destroy a whole Greek fleet because of the crime of one man. Yet she, the queen of heaven and the wife of Jupiter, has fought one small people for years without success. Who will honour her if she fails? So she flies to Aeolia, the island where Aeolus, the king of the winds, keeps the storms locked inside a mountain. She asks him to set the winds free and sink the Trojan ships. As a reward she promises him Deiopea, the most beautiful of her nymphs, as his wife. Aeolus agrees. He strikes the mountain with his spear, and the winds rush out.

A terrible storm falls on the sea. Clouds hide the sky, thunder roars, and lightning flashes. Aeneas, the Trojan leader, feels his body go cold with fear. He lifts his hands to heaven and cries out that the men who died at Troy, in front of their fathers, were the lucky ones. He wishes he had died there too, killed by the great Greek warrior Diomedes, where Hector and Sarpedon fell. The waves break the oars and turn the ships. Three ships are thrown onto hidden rocks. Three are driven into sandbanks. One ship, carrying the loyal Orontes and his men from Lycia, is hit by a huge wave in front of Aeneas's eyes. It spins three times and sinks. Men, weapons, and Trojan treasure float in the water. The ships of Ilioneus, Achates, Abas, and old Aletes also start to fill with water.

Neptune, the god of the sea, feels the sea moving deep below and lifts his head above the waves. He sees the Trojan fleet broken everywhere, and he understands at once that his sister Juno is behind this. He calls the winds to him and scolds them angrily. The sea belongs to him, not to Aeolus, he says. Aeolus may rule only in his own cave. Then Neptune calms the waves, chases away the clouds, and brings back the sun. Sea gods push the ships off the rocks. Virgil compares Neptune to a respected man who calms an angry crowd in a city. When the people see him, they stop shouting and listen. This comparison is famous, and some readers see in it Virgil's picture of a good Roman leader who brings order out of chaos.

The tired Trojans head for the nearest land, which is the coast of Libya in North Africa. They find a quiet natural harbour with high cliffs, a dark forest, and a cave with fresh water. Only seven ships are left of the twenty. Achates, Aeneas's most faithful friend, makes a fire. The men dry the wet grain they saved. Aeneas climbs a rock to look for the lost ships but sees none. Instead he sees a herd of deer. He shoots seven large deer, one for each ship, and shares them among his men. He also shares the wine that their friend Acestes gave them in Sicily. Then he speaks to give them courage. They have survived worse dangers, he says, such as the monster Scylla and the rocks of the Cyclops. One day they may even be glad to remember these troubles. Italy is waiting, where Troy can rise again. But inside, Aeneas is full of worry. He shows hope on his face and hides his pain in his heart. After the meal the men talk sadly about the lost friends, and Aeneas grieves most for Orontes, Amycus, Lycus, Gyas, and Cloanthus.

Now Venus, the goddess of love and the mother of Aeneas, goes to Jupiter, the king of the gods. Her eyes are full of tears. She reminds him of his promise that the Romans would come from the Trojans and rule land and sea. Why is that promise broken? Another Trojan, Antenor, was allowed to settle safely in Italy and found the town of Padua. But her son, she says, is kept far away because of the anger of one goddess. Jupiter smiles and kisses her. He tells her not to fear, because fate has not changed. Then he reveals the future. Aeneas will fight a great war in Italy, defeat warlike peoples, and give his people laws and a city. He will rule in Latium for three years. His son Ascanius, who is also called Iulus, will rule for thirty years and move the kingdom to a new city, Alba Longa. For three hundred years the descendants of Troy will rule there. Then a royal priestess named Ilia will have twin sons by the war god Mars. One of them, Romulus, raised by a she-wolf, will found Rome and call the people Romans. Jupiter says he gives them an empire with no limit in space or time. Even Juno will change her mind and support them. Later, a Caesar named Julius, whose name comes from Iulus, will rule to the ocean and be welcomed into heaven. Then wars will end, and the gates of war will be shut.

This prophecy is important for understanding the whole poem. The name Julius links Aeneas's son Iulus to Julius Caesar and to his adopted son Augustus, the emperor of Virgil's own time. The poem presents the suffering of Aeneas as the first step on a long road that leads to Augustus and to peace.

Jupiter then sends Mercury, his messenger, down to Carthage. Mercury makes the people of Carthage and their queen, Dido, kind and friendly toward the Trojans.

The next morning, Aeneas goes out with Achates to explore the strange land. In the forest he meets his mother, Venus, but she is in disguise. She looks like a young huntress carrying a bow. She asks if they have seen her sister hunting. Aeneas says she cannot be a normal girl; she must be a goddess. He asks where they are. Venus tells him that this is the land of Carthage, ruled by Queen Dido. She then tells Dido's story. Dido was married to Sychaeus, the richest man in Tyre, and she loved him deeply. But her brother Pygmalion, the king of Tyre, wanted his gold. He secretly murdered Sychaeus at an altar and hid the crime for a long time. Then the ghost of Sychaeus came to Dido in a dream. He showed her his wound, told her the truth, and showed her where old treasure was hidden. He told her to flee. Dido gathered all the people who hated or feared the tyrant. They took ships, loaded the gold, and sailed away. In Africa they bought as much land as a bull's skin could cover. They cut the skin into thin strips to surround a large hill, which became the centre of the new city.

Then Venus asks who Aeneas is. He tells her sadly that he is Aeneas, who carries the gods of Troy with him. He is looking for Italy. He began with twenty ships, and only seven are left. Venus interrupts him. She tells him to go to the queen's palace, because his lost friends and ships are safe. She points to twelve swans in the sky. An eagle attacked them, but now they fly safely together. This is an omen, a sign from the gods, that his ships have reached the harbour. As she turns away, she shines with divine beauty, and Aeneas recognizes her as his mother. He calls after her sadly: why does she always trick him with disguises? Why can they never hold hands and speak honestly? Venus does not answer, but she covers him and Achates in a thick mist so that nobody can see them.

The two men walk to a hill above the city. Aeneas is amazed. The people of Carthage are busy everywhere, like bees in summer. They build walls, a citadel, a harbour, and a theatre. They choose judges and a senate. Aeneas cries: how lucky they are, because their walls are already rising! Still hidden in the mist, he walks through the crowd. In the middle of the city there is a grove, and there Dido is building a large temple to Juno.

On the walls of this temple, Aeneas sees something that makes him cry. There are pictures of the Trojan War. He sees the Greek kings, Priam the king of Troy, and the fierce Achilles. He says to Achates that the whole world knows their suffering. People here feel pity for human troubles, he says, and this fame may save them. He looks at each picture in tears. He sees the Thracian king Rhesus killed in his sleep and his horses stolen. He sees the young Trojan prince Troilus dragged behind his own chariot. He sees the Trojan women bringing a gift to Minerva, who turns her face away. He sees Achilles dragging the body of Hector around the walls and selling it for gold, and Priam reaching out his empty hands. Aeneas even sees himself fighting the Greeks. He also sees Memnon and the Amazon warrior queen Penthesilea.

While he is looking, Queen Dido arrives with many young people around her. She is very beautiful. Virgil compares her to the goddess Diana leading her nymphs in a dance. Dido sits on a high throne in the temple, gives laws, and divides the work among her people. Suddenly Aeneas sees some of his lost men coming with a crowd: Antheus, Sergestus, Cloanthus, and others. He and Achates want to run to them, but they stay hidden in the mist to see what will happen.

Old Ilioneus speaks for the Trojans. He asks the queen to stop her people from burning their ships. They have not come to rob anyone. They were sailing to a land called Hesperia, now called Italy, when a storm scattered them. Yet the local people will not even let them land on the beach. Their king was Aeneas, the most just and good of men, and a great warrior. If he is alive, they have nothing to fear. They only ask to repair their ships. Then they will go to Italy if they find Aeneas, or back to their friend Acestes in Sicily if he is dead.

Dido answers kindly. She explains that her new kingdom is in danger, so she must guard her borders carefully. But who has not heard of Troy and of Aeneas? She offers to help them reach Italy or Sicily. She even offers them a home in Carthage, where she will treat Trojans and Tyrians as equals. She wishes Aeneas himself were here, and she promises to send men to search the coast for him.

At this moment the mist melts away. Aeneas stands before everyone, shining like a god, because Venus has made him look young and beautiful. He tells Dido that he is the man she is looking for. He thanks her warmly. She alone has pitied Troy's great suffering, he says, and has offered to share her city. He says her name and praise will last forever, as long as rivers run to the sea. Then he greets his friends.

Dido is amazed by his sudden appearance and by his story. She knows about him already. Long ago, a Greek named Teucer came to her father in Sidon and told the story of Troy's fall. She invites the Trojans into her palace. She says that she too has suffered, and so she has learned to help unhappy people. She orders a great feast and sends animals to the men on the shore.

Aeneas loves his son very much. He sends Achates back to the ships to bring Ascanius to the city, along with gifts saved from Troy. These include a cloak with gold embroidery, a veil once worn by Helen of Sparta, a sceptre, a necklace, and a crown.

But Venus has a new plan. She does not trust the people of Carthage, and she fears that Juno will use this city to harm her son. So she calls her other son, Cupid, the god of love. She asks him to take the shape of Ascanius for one night. When Dido holds the boy on her lap at the feast, Cupid will secretly fill her heart with love for Aeneas. Then no god can turn Dido against the Trojans. Cupid agrees. Venus puts the real Ascanius into a deep sleep and carries him away to her holy grove on the island of Cyprus.

At the feast, Dido sits on a golden throne, and the Trojans and Tyrians lie on purple couches. Everyone admires the gifts and the boy. Dido cannot stop looking at him. She holds him close, not knowing that a great god sits in her lap. Little by little, Cupid makes her forget her dead husband Sychaeus and fills her heart with a new love. Virgil calls her unhappy and doomed. Dido is set up as a tragic figure from her very first scene. The gods use her as a tool for their own plans.

After the meal, Dido pours wine for the gods and prays that this day will be happy for both peoples. A singer named Iopas sings about the moon, the sun, and the stars. Dido talks late into the night and asks many questions about Priam, Hector, and Achilles. Finally she asks Aeneas to tell the whole story from the start: the tricks of the Greeks, the fall of Troy, and his seven years of wandering. This request leads into the next two books, where Aeneas himself tells his story.

## Book 2: The Fall of Troy

At the banquet in Carthage everyone is silent, waiting. Aeneas begins his story. He tells Dido that she is asking him to feel again a terrible pain. He saw the fall of Troy with his own eyes and took a large part in it. Even a Greek soldier would cry to hear this story. It is late at night, but if she truly wants to know, he will tell it.

After ten years of war, the Greeks are tired and losing hope. With the help of the goddess Minerva, they build a huge wooden horse. They say it is a gift to the gods for a safe journey home. But inside its hollow body they hide their best soldiers. Then the Greek army sails to Tenedos, a small island near Troy, and hides behind it. The Trojans think the Greeks have gone home for good. Full of joy, they open their gates and walk around the empty Greek camp. They look at the places where Achilles and the other Greeks used to stay. Many stare at the giant horse. A Trojan named Thymoetes says they should pull it inside the walls. Others, led by Capys, say they should throw it into the sea, burn it, or cut it open to see what is inside. The people cannot decide.

Then Laocoon, a priest, runs down from the citadel with a crowd behind him. He shouts that they are mad. Do they really believe the Greeks have left? Do they not know how clever Ulysses is? Either Greek soldiers are hidden inside, or the horse is a machine to attack the walls. He gives a famous warning: he fears the Greeks even when they bring gifts. He throws his spear hard into the side of the horse. The wood shakes and makes a hollow sound. Aeneas says that if the gods had not been against them, this would have made the Trojans open the horse, and Troy would still be standing.

At that moment some shepherds bring in a Greek prisoner with his hands tied. His name is Sinon. He has let himself be captured on purpose, ready either to trick the Trojans or to die. The Trojans crowd around and laugh at him. Sinon looks around and cries that he has no home anywhere now. The Greeks want to kill him, and the Trojans want his blood too. This makes the Trojans curious, so they ask him to explain.

Sinon tells a clever, false story. He says he is a Greek. He was a relative of Palamedes, a good man whom the Greeks killed with false charges because he spoke against the war. Ulysses was behind that killing, and Sinon often spoke angrily about it and promised revenge. So Ulysses became his enemy. Then Sinon stops and says: why should he go on, if the Trojans hate all Greeks anyway? Let them kill him; Ulysses would be pleased. Now the Trojans want to hear more. Sinon continues. The Greeks often wanted to sail home, he says, but storms stopped them. They asked the oracle of Apollo, which is a holy place where a god answers questions. The answer was that they must kill a Greek man as a sacrifice to get a safe return, just as they once killed a girl to sail to Troy. Ulysses forced the prophet Calchas to name the victim. After ten days of silence, Calchas named Sinon. Everyone was relieved that it was not them. On the day of the sacrifice, Sinon broke free and hid all night in a marsh until the Greeks sailed away. Now he can never go home, and the Greeks may punish his children and father instead. He begs the Trojans for pity.

The Trojans feel sorry for him. King Priam himself orders his ropes untied and tells him that from now on he is one of them. Then Priam asks what the horse is for. Sinon swears by the gods that he will tell the truth. He says the Greeks always depended on Minerva's help. But Diomedes and Ulysses stole her holy statue, the Palladium, from her temple in Troy, with blood on their hands. After that the goddess was angry with the Greeks. Her statue flashed fire from its eyes and jumped three times. So Calchas told them to sail home to Greece, ask the gods again, and come back later. The horse was built to make peace with Minerva for the stolen statue. Calchas told them to make it very large so the Trojans could not bring it through their gates. If the Trojans harm it, Troy will be destroyed. But if they take it into their city, Troy will one day attack Greece and win.

The Trojans believe him. Aeneas says with bitterness that tears and lies defeated the people whom ten years of war and a thousand ships could not defeat.

Then something even more terrible happens. Laocoon, chosen as priest of Neptune, is sacrificing a bull at the altar. Two enormous snakes come swimming across the calm sea from Tenedos. Their red crests rise above the waves, and their eyes burn. The Trojans run away in fear. The snakes go straight to Laocoon. First they wrap around his two young sons and bite them. When Laocoon comes to help, they wrap around him twice at the waist and twice at the neck. He screams like a wounded bull. Then the snakes slide away to the temple of Minerva and hide under the feet of her statue. Now the Trojans are terrified. They say that Laocoon has been punished for striking the holy wood with his spear. Everyone shouts that the horse must be brought into the city.

So the Trojans break open their own walls. They put wheels under the horse and pull it with ropes, while boys and girls sing holy songs around it. Four times it stops in the gateway, and four times the weapons inside make a noise. Still the Trojans do not understand, and they place the horse in their sacred citadel. Cassandra, a daughter of Priam, warns them of the coming disaster. But a god has made it so that the Trojans never believe her. That night the Trojans decorate the temples to celebrate. Then they fall asleep.

Under the quiet moon, the Greek fleet sails back from Tenedos. A fire signal is lit on the king's ship. Sinon secretly opens the horse. The Greek leaders slide down a rope: Ulysses, Neoptolemus the son of Achilles, Menelaus, Epeus who built the horse, and others. They kill the guards and open the gates to the whole army.

Aeneas is asleep in his house. In a dream, the ghost of Hector appears to him. Hector was the greatest Trojan warrior, killed by Achilles. He looks as he did after Achilles dragged his body behind a chariot. He is covered in dust and blood, with wounds all over him. Aeneas cries and asks where he has been and why he looks so changed. Hector does not answer these questions. He only groans and says: "Run, son of a goddess, and save yourself from the flames. The enemy is inside the walls. Troy is falling. You have done enough for your country and for Priam. If Troy could be saved by force, I would have saved it." Hector gives Aeneas the holy objects and household gods of Troy. These are small statues of the family and city gods. Aeneas must take them with him and find a new city for them across the sea. Then Hector carries out the sacred eternal fire of Vesta, the goddess of the home.

Aeneas wakes up. His father Anchises's house is quiet and far from the centre, but he hears the noise of battle growing louder. He climbs onto the roof. He sees houses burning and hears shouts and trumpets. Now he understands the Greek trick. He grabs his weapons without a plan. All he wants is to fight, and he thinks it is beautiful to die in battle.

Panthus, a priest of Apollo, comes running with holy objects and his little grandson. He tells Aeneas that the last day of Troy has come. Jupiter has given everything to the Greeks. Armed men are pouring out of the horse, Sinon is setting fires, and thousands of Greeks are at the gates. Aeneas gathers a small group of brave men: Rhipeus, Epytus, Hypanis, Dymas, and a young man named Coroebus. Coroebus has come to Troy because he loves Cassandra. Aeneas tells them that the gods have left Troy. The only hope for defeated men, he says, is to hope for nothing, and to die fighting.

Like hungry wolves in the dark, they move through the burning city. Dead bodies lie everywhere. A Greek leader named Androgeus meets them and thinks they are Greeks. He asks why they are so late for the looting. Then he realizes his mistake, but it is too late. The Trojans kill him and his men. Coroebus has an idea: they should put on the Greek armour and shields. Trickery is fair against an enemy, he says. In Greek armour they kill many Greeks in the dark, and some Greeks even run back to hide inside the horse.

Then they see Cassandra being dragged by her hair out of Minerva's temple, her hands tied. Coroebus goes mad with anger and charges into the enemy, and the others follow. But now the Trojans on the temple roof think they are Greeks because of their armour, and they throw weapons at them. The Greeks also recognize the trick and attack from all sides. Coroebus is killed at the altar. Rhipeus, the most just man among the Trojans, also dies. Aeneas says sadly that the gods did not see it the same way. Hypanis, Dymas, and Panthus also die. Aeneas swears that he did not run from any danger that night. If fate had wanted him to die, he would have died.

With two older men, Aeneas goes to Priam's palace, where the fighting is worst. The Greeks are climbing ladders and attacking the doors. The Trojans on the roof tear off tiles and golden beams and throw them down. Aeneas enters through a secret back door, the one Andromache, Hector's wife, once used to bring her little son to his grandfather. From the roof, he and others push a tall tower down onto the Greeks. But more Greeks keep coming.

At the front door stands Pyrrhus, also called Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles. He shines in his armour like a snake that has just shed its old skin. With an axe he breaks through the great doors. Inside, women scream and kiss the doors. The Greeks pour into the palace like a flood. Aeneas sees Hecuba, the queen, with her daughters.

Then Aeneas tells how Priam dies. The old king sees his city taken. He puts on his old armour, which his shaking body can hardly carry, and goes out to die among the enemy. In the courtyard there is an altar under an old laurel tree. Hecuba and her daughters are holding the statues of the gods there, like doves in a storm. Hecuba sees Priam in armour and tells him it is useless. Even her son Hector could not help now. She pulls him to sit with them at the altar. Just then, Polites, one of Priam's sons, runs in wounded, chased by Pyrrhus. He falls dead in front of his parents. Priam shouts at Pyrrhus that the gods will punish him for making a father watch his son die. He says that even Achilles was better than this. Achilles respected Priam when he came to beg, and he gave back Hector's body. Priam throws a weak spear, which barely touches Pyrrhus's shield. Pyrrhus mocks him and tells him to go and report to Achilles how badly his son behaves. He drags Priam, slipping in his son's blood, to the altar and kills him with his sword. Aeneas ends this scene with a sad picture: the body of the great king who once ruled many lands of Asia lies on the shore without a head and without a name.

Now Aeneas feels fear for the first time. Seeing the old king die, he thinks of his own old father, his wife Creusa, his empty house, and his little son Iulus. He looks around. All his companions are dead or gone.

The next part of the text is printed in square brackets in this edition, because some ancient scholars doubted that Virgil wrote it. In it, Aeneas sees Helen of Sparta hiding at the altar of Vesta. Helen was the woman whose running away with the Trojan prince Paris started the war. She is afraid of both the Trojans and the Greeks. Aeneas becomes very angry. Should she go home as a queen while Troy burns? He decides to kill her, even though there is no glory in killing a woman.

At this moment his mother Venus appears to him clearly, shining as a goddess. She takes his hand and stops him. Why is he so angry, she asks? Has he forgotten his old father, his wife, and his son? Greek soldiers are all around them, and only her protection has kept them alive. She tells him that Helen and Paris are not to blame. It is the gods who are destroying Troy. Then she removes the mist from his eyes, so that for a moment he can see the gods at work. Neptune is shaking the walls with his trident. Juno stands at the main gate, calling the Greeks. Minerva sits on the citadel. Even Jupiter is helping the Greeks. Venus tells her son to run, and she promises to guide him home safely. Aeneas now sees the whole city falling, like an old tree on a mountain that farmers cut down with many blows of the axe until it falls.

With the goddess guiding him, Aeneas passes safely through fire and enemies to his father's house. But Anchises refuses to leave. He says that young people should flee, but he is old and does not want to live in exile. Long ago, Jupiter struck him with lightning, and the gods have hated him since. He will stay and die. Aeneas, Creusa, Ascanius, and the whole household beg him with tears, but he does not move. Aeneas is in despair. He cannot leave his father, so he decides to go back and die fighting. He puts on his armour again.

At the door, Creusa holds his feet and lifts up little Iulus. If you go to die, she says, take us with you. If you still have hope in fighting, then protect your own family first. At that moment a miracle happens. A soft flame appears on top of Iulus's head and plays in his hair without burning him. His parents are frightened and try to put it out with water. But Anchises is full of joy. He prays to Jupiter to confirm this omen. Immediately there is thunder on the left, which is a lucky side for such signs. A shooting star crosses the sky and falls into the forests of Mount Ida, leaving a bright trail. Now Anchises agrees to go.

Aeneas makes a plan. He will carry his father on his shoulders. Little Iulus will walk beside him, and Creusa will follow behind. The servants will go by different roads and meet at an old temple of Ceres outside the city, near an old cypress tree. Anchises will carry the household gods, because Aeneas has just come from killing and must wash in a river before touching holy things. Aeneas puts a lion skin over his shoulders and lifts his father. This image of Aeneas carrying his father and leading his son became one of the most famous pictures of Roman culture. It shows "pietas", the Roman idea of duty and loyalty to family, country, and the gods, which is Aeneas's most important quality. Aeneas admits that earlier he was not afraid of any weapon. Now every sound scares him, because he fears for the people he is carrying.

Near the gates, Anchises sees shining shields and shouts at his son to run. In panic, Aeneas leaves the familiar streets and runs through side paths. Somewhere in the confusion, Creusa is lost. Aeneas does not know if fate took her, if she took the wrong road, or if she sat down from tiredness. He does not look back. Only when they reach the temple of Ceres does he see that she is missing. He is wild with grief and blames every god and man. He leaves his father, his son, and the household gods with his friends in a hidden valley. Then he puts on his armour and goes back into the city alone.

He follows his own footsteps back to the gate and then to his house, but the Greeks have taken it and it is burning. He goes to Priam's palace. There, Ulysses and another Greek are guarding the treasure of Troy, and captured women and children stand in long lines. Aeneas shouts Creusa's name again and again in the dark streets.

Then the ghost of Creusa appears before him, taller than she was in life. Aeneas is frozen with fear. She speaks gently. Why give in to such wild grief? This has happened by the will of the gods. Jupiter does not allow her to go with him. She tells him that he will travel for a long time across the sea and come at last to Hesperia, the western land, where the Tiber river flows through rich fields. There he will find happiness, a kingdom, and a royal wife. She tells him to stop crying for her. She will never be a slave to Greek women, because she is a Trojan princess and the daughter-in-law of Venus. The great mother of the gods keeps her in this land. Her last words ask him to keep loving their son. Aeneas tries three times to put his arms around her neck. Three times her image slips away like wind or a dream.

At dawn, Aeneas returns to his friends. He is surprised to find a large crowd of men and women gathered there, ready to follow him to any land across the sea. The morning star rises over Mount Ida. The Greeks hold all the gates of Troy, and there is no hope left. So Aeneas lifts his father again and goes up into the mountains.

## Book 3: Seven Years of Wandering

Aeneas continues his story at Dido's banquet. After the gods destroyed Troy, he says, signs from heaven told the survivors to go into exile and look for a new home in faraway lands. They build a fleet below Mount Ida, near Troy, without knowing where fate will take them. At the start of summer, Anchises tells them to set sail. Aeneas cries as he leaves the shores where Troy once stood. He sails away as an exile, with his companions, his son, and the gods of his family and his city.

Their first stop is Thrace, a land to the north across the sea, which was once a friend of Troy. Aeneas begins to build a city on the shore and names it after himself. While he is making a sacrifice to his mother Venus and the other gods, he wants green branches to cover the altar. Near by there is a small hill covered with bushes. When he pulls up the first plant, drops of black blood run from its roots. He is shocked, but he tries again with a second plant, and again blood comes out. He prays to the gods of the place. When he pulls a third plant with all his strength, he hears a sad voice from under the ground. The voice says: "Why are you hurting me, Aeneas? Leave me in peace in my grave. I am Polydorus, a Trojan. Run from this cruel land." Polydorus explains that the spears that killed him have grown into these plants. Aeneas then tells Dido the background. Polydorus was a young son of Priam. When Priam began to lose hope in the war, he secretly sent Polydorus with a lot of gold to the king of Thrace for safety. But when Troy fell, the Thracian king joined the winning side, killed the boy, and kept the gold. Aeneas cries out against the terrible hunger for gold. He tells the leaders and his father what he has seen, and they all agree to leave this land of crime. First they give Polydorus a proper funeral, with an altar, dark branches, and offerings of milk and blood, so his spirit can rest.

When the sea is calm, they sail to Delos, a holy island of the god Apollo. King Anius, who is also Apollo's priest, is an old friend of Anchises and welcomes them. In Apollo's ancient temple, Aeneas prays for a lasting home, a family, and a city. He asks the god where they should go. Suddenly the whole temple and the hill seem to shake, and a voice speaks. It tells the Trojans to seek their "ancient mother", the land where their people first came from. There the family of Aeneas will rule over all lands for many generations. Everyone is happy, but which land does the god mean? Anchises thinks about the old stories. He says that their ancestor Teucer came from Crete, a large island in the south. So they make sacrifices and sail to Crete. They have also heard that the Cretan king Idomeneus has been driven out, so the island seems empty and ready for them.

In Crete, Aeneas eagerly starts building a city. He names it Pergamea, after the citadel of Troy. People marry, farm new fields, and receive homes and laws. But suddenly a terrible disease falls on the people, and the crops and trees also die. The burning summer heat dries the fields. Anchises says they should sail back to Delos and ask Apollo again. But that night, the household gods of Troy, the statues Aeneas carried out of the burning city, appear to him in the bright moonlight. They tell him that Apollo has sent them with the answer. Crete is not the right place. The land they must seek is Hesperia, which is now called Italy. Their ancestor Dardanus, the first founder of the Trojan royal family, came from there. So Italy is their true "ancient mother". Aeneas tells Anchises, who sees his mistake. The Trojans have two ancestors, Teucer from Crete and Dardanus from Italy, and he chose the wrong one. Anchises remembers that Cassandra often said they would go to Italy, but nobody believed her. So they leave Crete, leaving a few people behind.

At sea, a storm comes, and for three days and three nights it is so dark that even Palinurus, their skilled helmsman, cannot find the way. On the fourth day they reach the Strophades, small islands in the Ionian Sea. These islands are the home of the Harpies. The Harpies are terrible monsters: birds with the faces of young women, always pale with hunger, with sharp claws and dirty bodies. The Trojans find cattle and goats on the islands. They kill some and prepare a feast, offering a share to the gods. But the Harpies fly down with loud cries, steal the food, and make everything dirty and smelly. This happens twice. The third time, Aeneas tells his men to hide their swords in the grass. When the Harpies come, Misenus blows his trumpet as a signal, and the men attack. But their swords cannot hurt the Harpies' feathers or bodies, and the Harpies fly away.

Then Celaeno, the leader of the Harpies, lands on a high rock and makes a prophecy. She is angry that the Trojans killed her cattle and tried to fight her. She says that Jupiter told Apollo, and Apollo told her, that they will reach Italy. But they will not build the walls of their city until a terrible hunger forces them to eat their own tables. The men are terrified. Now they want to pray to the Harpies instead of fighting. Anchises prays to the gods to turn away this threat, and the ships leave quickly.

They sail past the Greek islands and pass Ithaca, the home of Ulysses. The Trojans curse the land that raised such a cruel enemy. They land at Actium on the western coast of Greece. There they make offerings to Jupiter and hold Trojan athletic games. They are happy to have passed so many Greek cities safely. Actium was the place where Augustus won his great sea battle in 31 BC, so Roman readers would notice that Aeneas holds games there. The Trojans stay through the winter. Aeneas hangs a shield on the temple door with a short line saying that Aeneas took these arms from the winning Greeks.

Next they come to Buthrotum, a town in Epirus in north-western Greece. There they hear surprising news. Helenus, a son of Priam, is now king of this Greek land. He has also married Andromache, the widow of Hector. Aeneas goes to find them. Outside the town, Andromache is making offerings at an empty tomb she has built for Hector beside a small river named after a river of Troy. When she sees Aeneas and his Trojan armour, she faints. When she wakes, she asks if he is real or a ghost, and if he is dead, where is Hector? Aeneas answers that he is alive. Then he gently asks about her life.

Andromache looks down and tells her sad story. She says that Polyxena, a daughter of Priam who was killed at the tomb of Achilles, was luckier than she was. Polyxena never became a slave. Andromache, however, was taken as a prize by Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles. She had to bear his child as a slave. Later Pyrrhus wanted to marry Hermione, the daughter of Helen, so he gave Andromache to Helenus, who was also his slave. Then Orestes, who loved Hermione, killed Pyrrhus at an altar. After this, part of the kingdom passed to Helenus. He built a small copy of Troy here. Andromache asks how Aeneas came here and asks about little Ascanius. Does the boy remember his lost mother? Is he growing brave like his father Aeneas and his uncle Hector?

Then Helenus arrives, crying with joy, and leads them into the town. Aeneas sees a small Troy, with a copy of the citadel, a dry stream named after the river of Troy, and a gate with the same name as Troy's main gate. The Trojans feast for days. When the winds are good for sailing, Aeneas asks Helenus for advice. Helenus is a prophet, a person who can understand the will of the gods. Aeneas says that all the signs have told him to go to Italy, but the Harpy Celaeno has promised a terrible hunger. What dangers should he avoid first?

Helenus makes a sacrifice and leads Aeneas to Apollo's temple. Then he gives a long prophecy. He says that he can tell only some things, because fate and Juno do not allow him to say more. Italy is much farther away than Aeneas thinks. He must sail a long way around before he can build his city. Helenus gives him a sign: when Aeneas finds a large white sow, a female pig, lying with thirty white piglets under oak trees beside a quiet river, that will be the place for his city. He also tells him not to fear the prophecy about eating tables, because fate will find a way. He warns Aeneas to avoid the nearest coast of Italy, because Greeks live there. When the Trojans make sacrifices in Italy, they must cover their heads with a purple cloth, so that no enemy face can spoil the omens. Helenus says this custom should continue for all their descendants. This explains a real Roman custom.

Helenus also warns him about the narrow strait between Italy and Sicily. On the right side lives Scylla, a monster with the upper body of a girl and the tail of a sea dragon, with dogs around her waist. She pulls ships onto the rocks. On the left is Charybdis, a whirlpool that swallows the sea three times and throws it up again. It is better to sail the long way around Sicily than to see Scylla even once. Most of all, Helenus repeats again and again that Aeneas must pray to Juno and win her favour with gifts. Finally, when he reaches the city of Cumae in Italy, near the dark lakes of Avernus, he must visit the Sibyl. The Sibyl is a priestess who lives in a cave and speaks the words of the god Apollo. She writes her prophecies on leaves, but when the wind blows the leaves around, she does not put them back in order. So Aeneas must beg her to speak her answers aloud. She will tell him about the peoples of Italy and the wars to come. Helenus ends by telling Aeneas to raise Troy to the sky by his deeds.

Helenus gives the Trojans rich gifts: gold, ivory, silver, and the armour of Pyrrhus. He also gives horses, guides, and extra rowers. He speaks warmly to Anchises, whom Venus once loved, and tells him that Italy is near but that they must sail past this first part of it. Andromache gives Ascanius beautiful clothes she has made. She says that he looks exactly like her own dead son, Astyanax. Her boy would be the same age now. Aeneas says goodbye with tears. He tells them that they are lucky, because their wandering has ended and they have built their own small Troy. His people are always called from one fate to the next. He hopes that one day their two peoples, in Epirus and in Italy, will be like one Troy.

They sail at night. Palinurus watches the stars, and at dawn they see the low coastline of Italy. Achates is the first to shout "Italy!", and everyone cheers. They land near a temple of Minerva. There they see four white horses grazing. Anchises says this is a sign of war, because horses are used in war. But horses can also learn to pull a cart together, so there is also hope of peace. They pray to Minerva and, following Helenus's advice, cover their heads and make an offering to Juno. Then they quickly leave this Greek coast.

They sail past the bay of Tarentum and see Mount Etna, the volcano of Sicily, in the distance. They hear the great roar of Charybdis. Anchises recognizes it and orders the men to row hard. Palinurus turns to the left, and the ships are lifted up to the sky and dropped down again by the waves, but they escape. At sunset, tired and lost, they come to the land of the Cyclopes, giant one-eyed shepherds, near Etna. The volcano roars all night and throws fire and rocks into the sky. People say a giant lies buried under it. The Trojans hide in the forest, unable to see what is making the noise.

At dawn, a thin, dirty, starving man comes out of the forest. His clothes are held together with thorns. He is clearly a Greek. When he sees Trojan armour, he stops in fear. Then he runs to the shore crying and begging. He admits that he fought against Troy. If his crime is so great, he says, they may throw him into the sea, because at least he would die at the hands of men. Anchises gives him his hand as a promise of safety. The man says his name is Achaemenides and he is one of Ulysses's men. When Ulysses escaped from the cave of the Cyclops Polyphemus, he forgot him there. He describes the cave, full of blood. He saw the giant grab two of his friends, smash them on a rock, and eat them. But Ulysses took revenge. When the Cyclops fell asleep full of wine, they pushed a sharp stake into his one huge eye. Achaemenides warns the Trojans to flee at once, because a hundred other Cyclopes live along this coast. He has lived for three months in the forest, eating berries and roots and hiding from the giants.

Just then they see Polyphemus himself on the mountain, a huge, blind shape walking with a pine tree as a stick, with his sheep around him. He walks into the sea to wash the blood from his empty eye. The water does not even reach his waist. The Trojans quietly cut their ropes and row away, taking Achaemenides with them. Polyphemus hears the oars and turns toward the sound, but he cannot reach them. He gives a huge shout that shakes the sea and the land, and the other Cyclopes run down to the shore. They stand there like tall trees, angry and helpless. In their fear, the Trojans almost sail toward Scylla and Charybdis. But a north wind comes and carries them safely south along the coast of Sicily. Achaemenides points out the places he saw before with Ulysses.

They sail around Sicily, past many towns. At last they reach the harbour of Drepanum on the western coast. Here Aeneas suffers his greatest loss of the journey. His father Anchises, who comforted him in every trouble, dies. Aeneas speaks to his dead father with deep sadness. He was saved from Troy for nothing, Aeneas says. Neither Helenus nor the Harpy warned him about this pain. This, he says, was the end of his long journey. From there, a god brought him to Dido's shores.

So Aeneas ends his story. The whole hall is silent. Books 2 and 3 are told in Aeneas's own voice, like the stories Odysseus tells at a royal feast in the Odyssey. While he speaks, Dido falls more deeply in love with him.

## Book 4: The Love and Death of Dido

Book 4 is the story of Dido, the queen of Carthage, and her love for Aeneas. It is one of the most famous love stories in world literature, and it ends in tragedy.

After hearing Aeneas's story, Dido cannot rest. She keeps thinking of his face, his words, his courage, and his noble family. The next morning she speaks to her sister Anna. She says that this guest is surely the son of a god. She admires everything about him. Then she makes a confession. After her husband Sychaeus was murdered, she promised herself never to marry again. Aeneas is the only man who has made her feel love again. She says she recognizes the signs of an old fire. But she swears that she would rather die than break her promise to her dead husband. He took her love with him, and he should keep it in the grave. Then she cries.

Anna answers with warm and practical advice. Will Dido spend all her youth alone and sad, without children and without love? Does a dead man in his grave really care? Dido has already refused many African kings, such as Iarbas. Now she has found a man she truly likes. Anna also reminds her of the danger around Carthage. Warlike African tribes live all around them, and there is a desert full of raiders. Their brother Pygmalion may also attack from Tyre. Anna thinks that the gods, and especially Juno, have brought the Trojans here. With Trojan soldiers on their side, Carthage would become very powerful. She tells Dido to make offerings to the gods and to find reasons to keep Aeneas in Carthage through the stormy winter season. These words make Dido's love burn even more strongly. Her sense of honour, her loyalty to her dead husband, slips away.

The sisters go from temple to temple making sacrifices, especially to Juno, the goddess of marriage. Dido looks inside the bodies of sacrificed animals, as priests did, to learn the will of the gods. But Virgil says that prayers and temples cannot help a woman who is mad with love. Dido is like a deer shot by a hunter's arrow. The deer runs through the forest, but the arrow stays in its side. Dido shows Aeneas around her city and its riches. She starts to speak and then stops in the middle. Every evening she asks to hear the story of Troy again. At night, when everyone has gone, she lies on the couch where he sat. She holds his son Ascanius on her lap because the boy looks like his father. Meanwhile, the building of Carthage stops. The towers are left half-finished, and the young men no longer train for war.

Juno sees what is happening. She speaks to Venus with false friendliness. She says that two gods, Venus and her son Cupid, have won a great victory by tricking one woman. She knows that Venus fears Carthage. So Juno proposes peace: let Dido and Aeneas marry, and let the two goddesses rule the joined people together. Venus understands Juno's real plan. Juno wants to keep Aeneas in Africa, so that the future empire will be in Carthage instead of Italy. But Venus pretends to agree. She says she is not sure if Jupiter will allow the two peoples to unite, but Juno is his wife and can ask him. Juno then explains her plan. Tomorrow Dido and Aeneas will go hunting. Juno will send a sudden storm with rain and hail. The hunters will scatter, and Dido and Aeneas will hide in the same cave. There Juno will join them in marriage. Venus agrees and laughs at the trick, because she knows it will not change fate.

The next morning a great hunting party gathers. Dido comes out at last, dressed in purple and gold, on a beautiful horse. Aeneas joins her, shining like the god Apollo. Young Ascanius rides happily through the valleys. He wishes a wild boar or a lion would appear instead of these harmless deer and goats. Then the sky grows dark, and the storm comes. Everyone runs for shelter. Dido and Aeneas enter the same cave. The Earth and Juno give a sign. Lightning flashes as if for a wedding, and the mountain nymphs cry out. Virgil writes that this day was the beginning of death and of all the troubles to come. From now on, Dido does not hide her love. She calls it a marriage, and with that word she covers her fault.

Rumour spreads the news at once. Virgil describes Rumour as a giant goddess. She starts small but grows as she moves, until her head is in the clouds. Under every feather on her body there is an eye, a tongue, a mouth, and an ear. She flies at night and never sleeps. She spreads truth and lies equally. Now she tells everyone that Dido and Aeneas spend the whole winter in pleasure and forget about their kingdoms.

Rumour carries the story to Iarbas, an African king and a son of the god Jupiter Ammon. Iarbas had wanted to marry Dido, but she refused him. He has built many temples to Jupiter. Now he prays to Jupiter in anger. He says that Dido, a foreign woman who bought a small piece of land on his coast, has rejected him and taken this Trojan as her king. He calls Aeneas a second Paris, a soft man with perfumed hair. He asks if Jupiter even cares.

Jupiter hears him and looks at Carthage. He sees the two lovers, who have forgotten their greater duty. He sends Mercury with a clear message. Venus did not save Aeneas twice from the Greeks for this. He was meant to rule Italy, to continue the line of Troy, and to bring the whole world under law. If he does not care about his own glory, does he want to rob his son Ascanius of Rome? He must sail. That is the whole message.

Mercury puts on his golden winged sandals and takes his magic staff. He flies past Mount Atlas, a giant who holds up the sky and has become a mountain covered with snow and ice. Then he lands in Carthage. He finds Aeneas building towers and houses for the new city. Aeneas wears a sword decorated with jewels and a purple cloak with gold thread, a gift from Dido. Mercury speaks sharply. Is Aeneas now building Carthage for a woman, forgetting his own kingdom? Jupiter asks what he hopes to gain by wasting time in Libya. If he does not care about his own fame, he should think of Ascanius, who must inherit Italy and Rome. Then Mercury disappears.

Aeneas is shocked and frightened. He wants to leave at once, but how can he tell the queen? How can he even begin? He thinks of many plans. In the end, he tells his captains Mnestheus, Sergestus, and Serestus to prepare the fleet secretly. Meanwhile he will look for the best moment and the gentlest way to tell Dido.

But Dido senses the truth before he speaks, and Rumour tells her that the ships are being prepared. She runs wildly through the city like a worshipper of Bacchus, the god of wine, in a religious frenzy. Then she faces Aeneas. Did he really think he could hide this crime and slip away in silence? Does their love not hold him? Is he sailing in the dangerous winter storms just to get away from her? She begs him by her tears and by their marriage to have pity. For him, she says, she made enemies of the African kings and her own people, and she lost her good name. She asks what she should wait for now. Should she wait for her brother to destroy her city or for Iarbas to take her as a prisoner? If only she had a child by him, a little Aeneas playing in her hall, she would not feel so completely abandoned.

Aeneas, obeying Jupiter, keeps his eyes still and hides his pain. He answers briefly. He will never deny how much she has done for him. He will remember her as long as he lives. But he did not plan to run away secretly. And he never offered her marriage or made a wedding promise. If he could choose his own life, he says, he would rebuild Troy for his people. But Apollo has ordered him to go to Italy. Italy is the place he loves now, his true country. Dido has her Carthage, so why should the Trojans not have their own land in Italy? Every night the ghost of his father Anchises warns him in his dreams. He also thinks of the wrong he is doing to Ascanius by keeping him from his kingdom. And now Mercury himself has come with Jupiter's command; Aeneas saw him in daylight and heard his voice. He asks her to stop hurting them both with her complaints. He ends with a famous short sentence: he does not go to Italy by his own choice.

Dido looks at him with growing anger. Then she explodes. His mother was no goddess, she says. He was born from hard rocks and fed by tigers. Did he cry or show any pity? She saved him when he was shipwrecked and gave him a place in her kingdom. Now he speaks of Apollo and Mercury, as if the gods cared about such things! She will not hold him back: let him go to Italy. But she hopes he will be wrecked on rocks and call her name. She will follow him like a dark fire, and after her death, her ghost will haunt him everywhere. She will hear of his punishment even in the underworld. She breaks off and runs away, leaving him with much more to say. Her maids carry her fainting to her room.

Virgil now calls him "good Aeneas". He wants to comfort her. His heart is shaken by his great love. But he obeys the gods and returns to his ships. This is the hardest test of his "pietas", his duty to the gods and his people. Readers have argued for centuries about whether he is cold or truly suffering.

The Trojans work quickly to launch the ships, like ants carrying grain before winter. Dido watches from her tower. Virgil asks her directly what she felt. Then he cries out against cruel Love, which forces human hearts to do anything. Dido decides to try once more. She sends Anna, whom Aeneas trusted, to plead with him. She did not fight against Troy, she says, or disturb his father's grave. She no longer asks for marriage, or for him to give up Italy. She asks only for a little time, until the winds are good and she learns to bear her pain. Anna carries these messages back and forth, but Aeneas does not change. Virgil compares him to a great oak tree in the Alps. The winds beat it from all sides and its leaves fall, but its roots go down deep and it stands firm. In the same way Aeneas feels great pain, but his decision does not move, and the tears fall in vain. Some readers think the tears are Dido's or Anna's; others think they are his own.

Now Dido wants to die. Terrible signs frighten her. At the altar, the holy water turns black and the wine turns to blood. In the night she hears the voice of her dead husband calling her from his little temple. An owl cries on the roof. In her dreams, Aeneas chases her, and she always walks alone on a long road, looking for her people in an empty land.

She secretly decides on the time and the way to die. But she hides her plan from Anna and speaks calmly. She says she has found a priestess from far western Africa who knows magic. This priestess can either bring Aeneas back or free Dido from her love. Dido asks Anna to build a pyre in the inner courtyard. A funeral pyre is a large pile of wood used to burn a dead body. Dido says that on this pyre she will burn Aeneas's weapons, his clothes, and the bed where they slept, to remove every trace of him. Anna does not understand. She does not imagine that her sister plans to die, so she does as Dido asks. The pyre is built. Dido places Aeneas's clothes, his sword, and an image of him on the bed. The priestess calls on the gods of the underworld and performs magic rites.

That night everything sleeps except Dido. She thinks about her choices. Should she go back to the African kings she refused? Should she follow the Trojan ships like a servant? They would not even take her, and the Trojans have always broken their promises. Should she take all her people to sea again, after they escaped Tyre with so much trouble? No, she decides. She deserves to die. She blames Anna for pushing her into this love. And she blames herself for breaking her promise to Sychaeus.

Meanwhile Aeneas, ready to leave, is sleeping on his ship. Mercury appears to him again in a dream. He tells Aeneas to wake up and flee at once. Dido is planning something terrible, and if dawn finds him still here, he will see the sea full of burning ships. He adds that a woman is always changeable. Aeneas jumps up and wakes his men. He cuts the ship's rope with his sword, and the whole fleet rows out to sea.

At dawn Dido sees from her tower that the harbour is empty and the fleet is far out at sea. She beats her breast and tears her hair. At first she wants her people to chase them with fire and weapons. Then she stops. What is she saying? She realizes, too late, that her mistake was giving away her kingdom to him. Bitterly she mocks him: this is the man who carries his household gods and carried his old father on his shoulders! She wishes she had torn him to pieces, killed his men and even Ascanius, and burned his ships.

Then she makes a terrible curse. She calls on the Sun, on Juno, on Hecate the goddess of magic, and on the Furies, the goddesses of revenge. If Aeneas must reach Italy, let him be attacked by a brave people. Let him be driven from his land and separated from Iulus. Let him beg for help and see his friends die. Let him accept a bad peace and not enjoy his kingdom. Let him die before his time and lie unburied on the sand. And she asks her own people, the Tyrians, to hate his people forever. Let there be no friendship between the two nations. She calls for an unknown avenger to rise from her bones and attack the Trojans with fire and sword. Roman readers would think here of Hannibal, the great general of Carthage, and of the three Punic Wars between Rome and Carthage, fought centuries later.

Dido then sends Barce, her old nurse, to bring Anna for the "magic ceremony". As soon as she is alone, she climbs onto the pyre. She takes out the sword Aeneas gave her, a gift never meant for this. She looks at his clothes and their bed, cries, and lies down. She speaks her last words. She has lived her life and finished the course that Fortune gave her. She has built a great city, seen its walls rise, and punished her brother for her husband's murder. She would have been happy, too happy, if the Trojan ships had never touched her shore. She will die without revenge, but she will die. Let Aeneas see the flames from the sea and carry the omen of her death with him. Then she falls on the sword.

Her servants see her and scream. The whole palace fills with cries, as if Carthage itself were falling to an enemy. Anna runs in. She understands now that the pyre and the rites were a trap. Why didn't Dido let her die with her? By building the pyre with her own hands, Anna has helped to destroy her sister, herself, the people, and the city. She climbs the pyre, holds her dying sister, and tries to stop the blood with her dress. Dido tries three times to lift herself up, and three times she falls back. Her eyes search for the light of the sky, and she groans when she finds it.

Juno finally pities her long and painful death. She sends Iris, the rainbow goddess and messenger of the gods, to free Dido's soul from her body. Dido is not dying by fate, Virgil explains, and she has not earned her death. She is dying early, driven by sudden madness. So Proserpina, the queen of the underworld, has not yet cut the lock of her hair that frees the soul. Iris flies down on her colourful wings, cuts the golden lock, and says she is giving it to the god of the dead. At once the warmth leaves Dido's body, and her life goes out into the winds.

Many readers see Dido as the greatest victim of the Roman mission. Her story shows the human cost of Aeneas's duty and of Rome's destiny.

## Book 5: Funeral Games in Sicily

Aeneas and his fleet are now far out at sea. They look back at Carthage and see a great fire burning in the city. It is the pyre of Dido, but the Trojans do not know this. Still, they remember the pain of a great love betrayed, and they know what a woman in despair can do. So they guess, with dark hearts, what the fire may mean.

When no land is in sight, a storm cloud gathers. Palinurus, the chief helmsman, tells Aeneas that even if Jupiter promised it, he could not reach Italy in this weather. The winds are against them. They must follow Fortune and go where she leads. He thinks the friendly shores of Sicily are close, where Eryx, a half-brother of Aeneas and a son of Venus, once ruled. Aeneas agrees at once. No land could be more welcome to him than Sicily. There his friend Acestes lives, and there his father Anchises is buried. So they sail back to Sicily. Acestes, a king born of a Trojan mother, sees the ships from a hill. He runs down to meet them, dressed in a bearskin, and welcomes them warmly.

The next morning Aeneas calls everyone together. He reminds them that exactly one year has passed since they buried his father here. He will always remember this day with grief and honour, wherever he is. He believes the gods have brought them back to Anchises's grave for a reason. So they will hold a sacrifice now. And on the ninth day, he will hold games in his father's honour: a boat race, a foot race, an archery contest, and boxing. Then he goes to the tomb with thousands of people. He pours wine, milk, and blood on the ground and throws flowers. He greets his father's spirit and says sadly that Anchises could not share the search for Italy. Suddenly a huge snake with shining, colourful scales comes out from under the tomb. It goes quietly around the altars, tastes the offerings, and slides back under the tomb without harming anyone. Aeneas does not know if it is the spirit of the place or a servant of his father's ghost. He makes more sacrifices, and his men feast.

On the ninth day, people from all over the area come to watch. The prizes are shown in the middle: holy tripods, green crowns, palm branches, armour, purple clothes, silver, and gold. A trumpet signals the start of the games. Funeral games like these also appear in Homer's Iliad, at the funeral of Patroclus, and Virgil is clearly following that model. Many of the captains also share names with famous Roman families of Virgil's time. Virgil says that the Memmii come from Mnestheus, the Sergii from Sergestus, and the Cluentii from Cloanthus.

The first contest is a race of four large ships. Mnestheus commands the Dragon. Gyas commands the Chimaera, a huge, heavy ship. Sergestus commands the Centaur, and Cloanthus commands the Scylla. Out at sea there is a rock, and Aeneas places a green tree branch on it. The ships must go around this rock and come back. The trumpet sounds, and the ships leap forward while the crowd cheers. Gyas takes the lead, and Cloanthus is second. Near the rock, Gyas shouts at his helmsman, Menoetes, to steer closer to the rock. But Menoetes is afraid of hidden rocks and steers out to the open sea. Cloanthus slips in between Gyas and the rock and takes the lead. Gyas is so angry that he throws poor old Menoetes into the sea and steers the ship himself. Menoetes climbs up onto the rock, wet and spitting salt water, and everyone laughs.

Now Mnestheus and Sergestus hope to pass Gyas. Mnestheus encourages his men. He does not ask them to win, he says, only not to come in last, because that would be shameful. Sergestus tries to go too close to the rock. His ship hits it, his oars break, and he is stuck. Mnestheus passes him, then passes Gyas, and finally chases Cloanthus. The crowd shouts loudly, and the two ships might have finished together. But Cloanthus prays to the sea gods and promises them a white bull. The sea gods hear, and the god Portunus pushes his ship with his own hand. Cloanthus wins.

Aeneas gives prizes to everyone. Cloanthus receives a cloak embroidered with gold. It shows the Trojan prince Ganymede being carried up to heaven by Jupiter's eagle. Mnestheus receives a heavy coat of golden armour that Aeneas himself took from a Greek at Troy. Gyas receives bronze pots and silver cups. Finally Sergestus arrives, rowing slowly with broken oars, like an injured snake. Aeneas is glad the ship and crew are safe and still gives him a prize: a slave woman from Crete, skilled in weaving, with her twin baby boys.

Next is the foot race, held in a grassy valley shaped like a theatre. Many young men take part. The most famous are Nisus and Euryalus. Euryalus is very young and beautiful, and Nisus loves him deeply as a friend. Others are Diores from Priam's family, Salius, Patron, and two Sicilians, Helymus and Panopes. Aeneas promises a gift to every runner and special prizes to the top three. Nisus quickly runs far ahead. Salius is second and Euryalus third. But near the finish, Nisus slips on blood from the animals sacrificed earlier and falls into the dirt and blood. Even then he does not forget his friend Euryalus. He gets up and throws himself in front of Salius, who trips and falls. So Euryalus wins, Helymus is second, and Diores is third.

Salius protests loudly that his prize was stolen by a trick. But the crowd likes Euryalus, who is young, beautiful, and crying. Diores also supports him, because he would lose his third prize if Salius moved up. Aeneas solves the problem kindly. The order of the prizes stays the same, but he gives Salius a lion skin to comfort him. Then Nisus shows his face and body covered in dirt. If Aeneas pities the losers, he says, what will he give to Nisus, who would have won without bad luck? Aeneas laughs and gives him a fine shield. This funny scene introduces Nisus and Euryalus and their deep loyalty to each other, which will matter again in Book 9.

The third contest is boxing. Ancient boxers wrapped their hands in heavy leather straps called gloves. The prize for the winner is a bull, and the loser will receive a sword and helmet. Dares, a huge Trojan, steps forward. He once fought the Trojan prince Paris and killed a great boxer at Hector's tomb. He swings his arms proudly, but nobody dares to fight him. So he takes the bull by the horn and asks Aeneas to give him the prize.

Then old King Acestes speaks to his friend Entellus, a Sicilian. He was once the best boxer in Sicily and a student of Eryx. Will he let this prize go so easily? Entellus says he still wants glory, but he is old and his body is slow. Still, he throws down a pair of enormous gloves made of seven layers of leather sewn with lead and iron. They once belonged to Eryx, who fought the hero Hercules with them on this very beach. They still have blood on them. Everyone is amazed, and Dares steps back in fear. Entellus offers to fight with equal gloves, so Aeneas gives them matching ones.

The fight begins. Dares is young and quick on his feet. Entellus is heavy and strong, but his knees shake and he breathes hard. Dares attacks again and again, like a soldier trying every way into a city. Entellus stands still and blocks. Then Entellus throws a huge punch, but Dares moves aside. Entellus misses, loses his balance, and falls heavily to the ground like an old tree. Everyone shouts, and Acestes helps him up. But the fall makes Entellus angry and ashamed. He chases Dares across the field and hits him again and again with both hands, like hail on a roof. Aeneas stops the fight before it goes too far. He tells Dares that a god is now on the other side, and he must give in to heaven. Dares's friends carry him away, spitting blood and teeth. Entellus then shows what his strength was. He punches the prize bull between its horns and kills it with one blow. He offers it to Eryx in place of Dares's life, and he says he will never box again.

The fourth contest is archery. Aeneas sets up a ship's mast and ties a live pigeon to the top with a string. Hippocoon shoots first and hits the mast. Mnestheus shoots next and cuts the string, so the bird flies free. Eurytion, the brother of the famous Trojan archer Pandarus, prays to his dead brother and shoots the pigeon in the sky. It falls dead with the arrow in it. Old Acestes has no target left. He shoots his arrow high into the air anyway, to show his skill. Then something amazing happens. The arrow catches fire in the sky, leaves a trail of flame, and burns away like a shooting star. Everyone is astonished, and Virgil says that prophets later explained its meaning. Aeneas accepts this omen with joy. He declares Acestes the winner and gives him a beautiful bowl that once belonged to Anchises. Eurytion does not complain about this. Then the other archers receive their prizes.

Before the games end, Aeneas sends a message to Ascanius to bring out his group of boys on horseback. The boys ride in front of their parents, wearing garlands and carrying spears. There are three groups of twelve riders, each led by a boy captain. One leader is young Priam, the grandson of old King Priam. Another is Atys, the close friend of Iulus. The third leader is Iulus himself, riding a horse that Dido gave him as a gift. The boys ride in complicated patterns. They charge, turn back, pretend to fight, and then ride together in peace, weaving in and out like the Labyrinth, the famous maze of Crete. Virgil explains that Ascanius later taught this game to the people of Alba Longa, and from them it came to Rome. In Virgil's day the game was still called "Troy". Augustus revived this "Game of Troy" in Rome, so Virgil connects a custom of his own time to the Trojan past.

But now Fortune changes. Juno, still angry, sends Iris down to the Trojan ships. The Trojan women are sitting alone on the beach, away from the games. They are crying for Anchises and looking at the endless sea. They are tired of travelling and want a city. Iris disguises herself as Beroe, an old Trojan woman. She tells them that it is now seven years since Troy fell. They have wandered over every sea, chasing an Italy that always runs away. Why not build a city here in Sicily, with friendly people? She says that Cassandra came to her in a dream, gave her burning torches, and said: "Here is your Troy." She grabs a burning stick from an altar of Neptune and throws it.

Pyrgo, the oldest woman and once the nurse of Priam's children, tells them this is not Beroe. She points out the shining eyes, the voice, and the walk of a goddess. She herself left the real Beroe sick at home a short time ago. The women hesitate between love for this land and the call of fate. Then Iris flies up into the sky and makes a great rainbow. Now the women go mad. They take fire from the altars and throw it onto the ships. The flames spread quickly.

A man named Eumelus runs to the games with the news, and everyone sees the black smoke. Ascanius rides at once to the shore. He shouts at the women: "What madness is this? You are not burning an enemy camp. You are burning your own hopes. It is me, your Ascanius!" He throws down his helmet so they can see his face. Aeneas and the Trojans arrive. The women run away in shame and hide in the woods and caves. They now recognize their own people, and Juno's madness leaves them. But the fire keeps burning inside the wet wood, and water does not stop it. Aeneas tears his clothes and prays to Jupiter. If Jupiter does not hate every last Trojan, let him save the ships. Otherwise let him kill them all with lightning now. At once a great storm comes with heavy rain, and the fire goes out. All the ships are saved except four.

Aeneas is shaken. Should he forget fate and stay in Sicily, or go on to Italy? An old and wise man named Nautes advises him. They must follow fate, he says, and fortune can be overcome only by bearing it. Acestes is of Trojan blood and is willing to help. Let the old men, the tired women, and all those who are weak or afraid stay here in Sicily. They can build a city and call it Acesta.

That night the ghost of Anchises comes down from the sky and appears to his son. He says Jupiter has sent him, the same Jupiter who saved the ships. Aeneas should follow Nautes's advice. He should take only the bravest people to Italy, because in Latium he must fight a hard and wild people. But first he must come down to the underworld, the land of the dead, to meet his father. Anchises is not in Tartarus, the place of punishment, but in Elysium, the happy home of good souls. The Sibyl, the priestess he heard about in Book 3, will lead him there. Then he will learn about all his descendants and the city he will found. At dawn the ghost disappears like smoke. Aeneas calls after him and asks why he is running away and why they cannot embrace.

Aeneas tells Acestes and his men about Jupiter's command and his father's words. They quickly agree. They choose the people who will stay, all those who do not want glory. The others repair the ships. Aeneas marks out the borders of the new town with a plough and calls it Ilium and Troy. Acestes becomes its king, makes laws, and forms a senate. A temple to Venus is built on top of Mount Eryx, and a priest and holy wood are given to Anchises's grave.

After nine days of feasting, the wind is good for sailing. There is a great deal of crying on the shore. Even those who hated the sea now want to come along. Aeneas comforts them and leaves them in the care of Acestes. He makes sacrifices, and the fleet sails away.

Meanwhile Venus goes to Neptune. She complains that Juno's anger never ends. Juno destroyed Troy, raised the storm near Libya in Neptune's own sea, and now made the women burn the ships. Venus asks Neptune to give the Trojans a safe journey to the Tiber, if fate allows it. Neptune answers kindly. He reminds her that he once saved Aeneas at Troy, wrapping him in a mist when Achilles was about to kill him. He promises that Aeneas will reach the harbour of Avernus safely. But one man will be lost at sea. One life will be given for many. Then Neptune drives his chariot over the waves and calms the sea, followed by whales and sea gods.

Aeneas feels joy and orders the sails raised. Palinurus leads the fleet, and the other ships follow his. In the middle of the night, when the sailors are sleeping, the god of Sleep comes down from the sky. He takes the shape of a sailor named Phorbas and sits beside Palinurus. He tells him that the sea is calm and the wind is steady. Palinurus should rest for an hour, and he will steer in his place. Palinurus refuses. He has been fooled by a calm sea too often, he says, and he will not trust Aeneas to it. He holds the tiller and watches the stars. Then the god shakes a branch wet with the water of Lethe, the river of forgetting in the underworld, over his head. Palinurus falls asleep, and the god throws him into the sea. He takes part of the rudder with him and cries out to his friends, but no one hears.

The fleet still sails on safely, as Neptune promised. They are now passing the rocks of the Sirens, once deadly creatures whose songs killed sailors. Aeneas notices that his ship is drifting without a helmsman. He takes the tiller himself and steers through the dark sea. He is very sad. He believes his friend trusted the calm sea and sky too much. He says that Palinurus will lie naked and unburied on a foreign beach. Palinurus is the "one life for many" that Neptune asked for. He is another person who pays a price for the Trojan journey.

## Book 6: The Underworld

Still crying for Palinurus, Aeneas lets his fleet run on, and at last they reach the coast of Cumae in Italy. The Trojans jump eagerly onto the shore of Hesperia. Some make fires, some search the forest, and some find streams of water. But Aeneas goes up to the hill where Apollo's temple stands, near the deep cave of the Sibyl. The Sibyl is a priestess who speaks for Apollo. When the god fills her mind, she can see the future.

Apollo's temple was built by Daedalus, the great craftsman of old stories. Daedalus escaped from King Minos of Crete by flying with wings he made himself, and he landed here first. On the temple doors he carved scenes from his own life. They show the Athenian children sent every year as a punishment to Crete. They show the queen of Crete and her monster son, the Minotaur, who was half man and half bull. They show the maze, called the Labyrinth, that Daedalus built to hold the monster, and how he helped a lover of the princess find his way out with a thread. Twice Daedalus tried to carve the death of his own son Icarus, who flew too close to the sun and fell, but both times his hands dropped from grief. Aeneas would look at all of it, but Achates returns with the priestess, Deiphobe. She tells him this is not the time to look at pictures, and orders a sacrifice of bulls and sheep.

The Sibyl's cave is cut into the rock, with a hundred openings, and her answers come out through all of them. At the entrance the god takes hold of her. Her face and colour change, her hair falls loose, her chest heaves, and she seems to grow taller. Her voice is no longer human. She tells Aeneas to pray quickly, because the great doors will not open until he does. The Trojans shiver with fear. Aeneas prays to Apollo. He reminds the god that he guided Paris's arrow to kill Achilles, and that he has led the Trojans across all these seas. He asks the gods who hated Troy to stop punishing them now. He asks the Sibyl for a home in Latium for the Trojans and their wandering gods. In return he promises to build a marble temple to Apollo and Diana and to keep the Sibyl's prophecies in a holy place in his kingdom. Roman readers would think of the real Sibylline books kept in Rome, and of the temple of Apollo that Augustus built. Aeneas also begs her not to write her answers on leaves that the wind might scatter, but to speak them aloud.

The Sibyl rages in her cave, trying to throw the god out, but Apollo controls her like a rider controls a wild horse. The hundred doors open, and her answer comes out. Aeneas has finished with the dangers of the sea, but worse dangers wait on land. The Trojans will reach Lavinium, but they will not be glad that they came. She sees wars, terrible wars, and the river Tiber foaming with blood. There will be a new Simois and a new Xanthus, the rivers of Troy, and a new Greek camp. A new Achilles, also born of a goddess, is already waiting in Latium. Juno will never stop chasing the Trojans. Once again a foreign bride will be the cause of all the trouble, just as Helen was at Troy. But Aeneas must not give in to his troubles. He must go against them even more bravely. And the first help, though he will not expect it, will come from a Greek city.

Aeneas answers calmly. None of these troubles is new or surprising to him, he says; he has already imagined them. He has only one request. The entrance to the underworld, the land of the dead, is said to be here, near the dark lake of Avernus. He wants to go down and see his father face to face. He carried Anchises out of burning Troy on his shoulders, and the old man shared all his dangers at sea. Anchises himself told him to come here. Other heroes have gone down to the dead and come back, like Orpheus, who went to bring back his wife with his music, and Hercules and Theseus. Aeneas too is descended from Jupiter.

The Sibyl replies with famous words. It is easy to go down into the underworld; its door is open night and day. But to climb back up to the air, that is the hard work. Only a few sons of the gods have done it. If he really wants to try, he must first do two things. First, somewhere in the dark forest there is a tree with a golden branch, which is sacred to Proserpina, the queen of the dead. Only someone who carries this Golden Bough can enter the underworld. If fate calls him, the branch will come away easily. If not, no strength or knife can cut it. When one golden branch is taken, another grows in its place. Second, one of his friends lies dead, and his body makes the whole fleet unclean. He must bury him first. Then he can see the land where no living person goes.

Aeneas walks away sad and thoughtful, with Achates. Which dead friend did she mean? On the beach they find the body of Misenus. He was the best trumpet player of all, who once fought beside Hector and then followed Aeneas. He had foolishly blown his shell trumpet over the sea and challenged the gods to play better. The jealous sea god Triton, if the story is true, pulled him into the waves among the rocks and drowned him. Everyone mourns, and they cut down trees in the old forest to build his funeral pyre. As Aeneas works with them, he looks at the huge forest and wishes the Golden Bough would show itself. Just then two doves fly down and land in front of him. He knows they are his mother's birds, and he prays for them to guide him. The doves fly ahead, staying in sight, and lead him to the dark lake of Avernus. There they land on a tree, and among its green leaves a golden branch shines, like mistletoe growing on a tree in winter. Aeneas breaks it off eagerly, even though it seems to resist a little, and carries it to the Sibyl.

Meanwhile the Trojans give Misenus a full funeral. They build a large pyre with dark leaves and cypress trees around it, and they place his armour on top. They wash his body, lay him on the pyre, and light it with their faces turned away, as was the custom. Afterwards, Corynaeus collects his bones in a bronze jar and purifies the men with water. Aeneas builds a great tomb for Misenus under a high hill, with his oar and trumpet. Virgil says that this hill is still called Misenus. Virgil often explains the names of places and customs of his own Italy by linking them to Aeneas.

Now Aeneas follows the Sibyl's instructions. There is a deep cave by a black lake, surrounded by dark woods. Poisonous air rises from it, so no bird can fly over safely. The Sibyl sacrifices four black bulls and calls on Hecate, the goddess of magic and the underworld. Aeneas also sacrifices animals to the goddesses of night and to Proserpina, and he burns whole bulls for Pluto, the king of the dead. At sunrise the ground groans, the forest moves, and dogs seem to howl as the goddess comes. The Sibyl shouts to all the others to stay away. She tells Aeneas to draw his sword, because now he needs courage. Then she runs madly into the cave, and Aeneas follows her without fear.

Here Virgil stops to pray to the gods of the dead. He asks them to let him tell what he has heard about the hidden world under the earth.

The two travellers walk through darkness, like people walking in a forest under a weak moon. At the entrance live dark figures: Grief, Worry, Disease, Old Age, Fear, Hunger, Poverty, Death, Pain, Sleep, War, the Furies, and Discord, with snakes in her hair. In the middle stands a great elm tree. False dreams hang under all its leaves. Monsters also wait at the gate: Centaurs, Scyllas, the hundred-armed giant Briareus, the many-headed snake of Lerna, the fire-breathing Chimaera, Gorgons, and Harpies. Aeneas is frightened and draws his sword. But the Sibyl reminds him that these are only empty shapes without bodies, so his sword would cut only air.

The road leads to the river Acheron, which flows into the river Cocytus. Here Charon, the old ferryman of the dead, takes souls across the water in his boat. He is dirty and frightening, with a long grey beard and burning eyes. A huge crowd of souls rushes to the river bank: mothers, men, heroes, boys, girls, and young people who died before their parents. They are as many as leaves falling in autumn or birds flying south in winter. They all stretch out their hands, wanting to cross. But Charon takes only some of them and pushes the others back. Aeneas asks why. The Sibyl explains that the souls left behind are those whose bodies were never buried. They must wander on this shore for a hundred years before they can cross. Aeneas stops, full of pity. He sees there Leucaspis and Orontes, the captain whose ship sank in the storm in Book 1.

Then he sees Palinurus, his helmsman, who fell into the sea in Book 5. Aeneas asks which god drowned him. Apollo promised that Palinurus would reach Italy safely, Aeneas says; was that a lie? Palinurus answers that no god drowned him. He fell while he was steering and took the rudder with him. He was more afraid for Aeneas's ship than for himself. He swam for three nights in stormy seas, and on the fourth day he reached the coast of Italy. But as he climbed the rocks, heavy in his wet clothes, the local people attacked him, thinking he had something worth stealing, and killed him. Now his body lies on the beach, moved by the waves and winds. He begs Aeneas to bury him or to take him across the river now. The Sibyl tells him sharply that he cannot cross unburied, because prayers cannot change the laws of the gods. But she gives him comfort. The people living near that place will be frightened by signs from heaven. They will build him a tomb, bring him offerings every year, and the place will always have his name. Palinurus is happy for a while. Like Misenus, Palinurus gives his name to a real place in Italy, Cape Palinuro.

When Charon sees a living man with a sword coming toward the river, he shouts at him to stop. Living bodies may not cross. He remembers that Hercules and Theseus came here before and caused trouble. Hercules dragged away the guard dog of the underworld, and Theseus tried to steal Proserpina. The Sibyl calms him. They have no evil plan, she says. This is Aeneas of Troy, famous for his goodness and his courage, coming to see his father. If such love does not move Charon, then he must at least respect this. She shows him the Golden Bough under her robe. Charon has not seen it for a very long time. He becomes quiet and brings his boat. He throws out the souls sitting on the benches and takes Aeneas on board. The small boat groans under the weight of a living man, and water leaks in, but they cross safely.

On the other side lies Cerberus, the giant guard dog with three heads, with snakes rising on his neck. The Sibyl throws him a cake made with honey and sleeping drugs. He eats it with his three hungry mouths and falls asleep across the whole cave. Aeneas passes by.

Soon they hear crying voices. These are the souls of babies who died young. Near them are people who were put to death on false charges. Minos, a judge of the dead, examines their lives and decides their places. Next are those who killed themselves. They hated the light of life, but now they would gladly suffer poverty and hard work in the upper world. They cannot go back. Then come the Fields of Mourning, where people who died because of love walk hidden in myrtle woods. Even death does not end their pain. Aeneas sees several famous women from old stories there.

Among them he sees Dido, with her wound still fresh, walking in the great forest. Aeneas recognizes her dim shape in the darkness, like someone who sees, or thinks he sees, the new moon behind clouds. He cries and speaks to her lovingly. So the news was true, he says, and she died by the sword. Was he the cause? He swears by the stars and the gods that he left her shore against his will. The gods forced him to go, the same gods who now send him through this dark place. He never thought his leaving would cause her such pain. He begs her to stop and not run away, because this is the last time fate allows him to speak to her. But Dido keeps her eyes on the ground. His words move her no more than if she were made of hard stone. At last she turns and runs angrily into the shady forest, where her first husband Sychaeus waits. He returns her sadness and her love. Aeneas, shaken by her fate, follows her with his eyes for a long time, crying. This silent meeting is one of the most powerful scenes in the poem. Dido, who spoke so much in Book 4, now refuses to say a single word.

Aeneas continues. He comes to the fields of famous warriors. Here he sees Greek heroes of older wars and many Trojans who died in the war, and he sighs at the sight. The Trojan souls crowd around him, happy to walk beside him and ask why he has come. But when the Greek leaders see him shining in armour, they are terrified. Some run away as they once ran to their ships, and some try to shout but only make a weak, thin sound.

Then he sees Deiphobus, a son of Priam, whose face is terribly cut. His ears, nose, and hands have been cut off. Aeneas barely recognizes him. He asks who did this. He heard that Deiphobus died on Troy's last night, so he built an empty tomb for him and called his spirit three times. Deiphobus answers that Aeneas did everything right. It was his fate and the crimes of Helen that destroyed him. After Paris died, Deiphobus had married Helen. On the last night of Troy, Helen led the Trojan women in a false religious dance and waved a torch to signal the Greeks. Deiphobus was sleeping, worn out. Helen took all the weapons from the house, even his sword from under his pillow. Then she opened the door to her first husband Menelaus, hoping to win back his favour. Ulysses came with him. Deiphobus asks the gods to punish the Greeks in the same way. Then he asks why Aeneas has come here alive.

They have talked so long that half the day has passed. The Sibyl warns Aeneas that time is short and they are wasting it in tears. The road divides here. The right path leads past the walls of the great god Dis, or Pluto, to Elysium. The left path leads to Tartarus, where the wicked are punished. Deiphobus says goodbye. He tells Aeneas, the glory of their people, to go and enjoy a better fate than his.

Aeneas looks to the left. He sees a large city with three walls, surrounded by a river of fire called Phlegethon. Its gate has columns that no army, not even the gods, could break. Tisiphone, one of the Furies, sits there day and night in a bloody robe. From inside come groans, the sound of whips, and the noise of iron chains. Aeneas is frightened and asks what crimes are punished here. The Sibyl explains that no pure person may enter this place, but Hecate once showed her everything. The judge Rhadamanthus rules here. He forces people to confess the hidden crimes they did not pay for while they were alive. Tisiphone whips them and threatens them with snakes. Inside, a monster with fifty mouths guards the door, and the pit of Tartarus goes down twice as deep as the sky is high.

The Sibyl describes the people punished there. There are the Titans, the old giants who fought the gods. There are two giant brothers who tried to pull down heaven. There is Salmoneus, a king who pretended to be Jupiter and imitated his thunder, until Jupiter struck him with real lightning. There is the giant Tityos, whose liver is eaten forever by a vulture. Others sit before rich feasts but are never allowed to eat, or have a heavy rock always about to fall on them. The Sibyl lists the kinds of sinners. There are those who hated their brothers, hit their parents, or cheated people who trusted them. There are those who kept their wealth for themselves and did not share it, and they are the largest group. There are those who were killed for adultery, those who fought in wicked wars, and those who betrayed their masters. Some roll huge stones. Some are tied to wheels. Theseus must sit forever. One sinner keeps shouting a warning to all: learn to be just and do not ignore the gods. There are men who sold their country for gold and put it under a tyrant, and men who made and broke laws for money. All of them dared to do terrible things and succeeded. The Sibyl says that even with a hundred tongues and a voice of iron she could not name all the crimes and punishments.

Then she tells Aeneas to hurry. They come to the walls of Dis's palace. At the gate, Aeneas washes himself with fresh water and places the Golden Bough there as a gift for Proserpina.

Now they reach Elysium, the home of the blessed. It is a land of green meadows and happy woods with its own sun and its own stars. Here people do sports and wrestle on the sand. Others dance and sing, while Orpheus plays music. Aeneas sees the founders of Troy, Ilus, Assaracus, and Dardanus, great heroes born in happier times. Their chariots and spears stand nearby, and their horses graze freely, because the things they loved in life still please them here. Others feast and sing in a laurel grove beside a river. Here are those who were wounded fighting for their country, pure priests, good poets, people who made life better with their inventions, and people whose good deeds made others remember them. All wear white bands on their heads. The Sibyl asks Musaeus, a famous poet who stands taller than all the others, where Anchises lives. He answers that nobody here has a fixed home; they live in the shady woods and meadows. He leads them to the top of a hill and shows them the bright plains below.

Deep in a green valley, Anchises is looking at the souls who are waiting to be born. He is studying all his future descendants, their fortunes and deeds. When he sees Aeneas coming across the grass, he stretches out both hands with tears running down his face. Has his son's love finally overcome the hard road? He was counting the days, he says. He worried most that the land of Libya would harm him. Aeneas says that his father's sad ghost came to him many times and brought him here. He asks to hold his father's hand. With tears he tries three times to put his arms around his father's neck. Three times the shape slips from his hands like wind or a dream. These are the same words Virgil used for Creusa's ghost in Book 2.

Aeneas then sees a quiet valley with a forest and the river Lethe. Countless souls are flying around it like bees around flowers in summer. He asks what this is. Anchises explains that these souls are going to receive new bodies. At the river Lethe they drink the water of forgetfulness. Aeneas is surprised. Can any soul really want to go back to the heavy prison of a body? Anchises explains. A divine spirit lives inside the whole universe: the sky, the earth, the sea, the moon, and the sun. From it come all living things. Their souls are made of heavenly fire, but the body makes them heavy and dull. Because of the body they feel fear, desire, sorrow, and joy, and they cannot see the light. Even after death, the stains of the body do not disappear at once. So the souls are cleaned by punishment. Some hang in the wind, some are washed in water, and some are burned in fire. After this, a few stay in Elysium. The others, after a thousand years, are called by a god to the river Lethe. There they forget their past life, so they can return to the world above and want to live in bodies again.

Then Anchises leads Aeneas and the Sibyl to a small hill where they can see the long line of souls coming. He will now show his son the glory of his future family, the great Romans who will carry his name. This is the famous parade of heroes. It is Virgil's way to show Roman history, from its beginnings to his own time, as the result of Aeneas's journey.

First comes Silvius, leaning on a spear. He will be the son of Aeneas and Lavinia, born late in Aeneas's life and raised in the woods. He will be a king and the father of kings in Alba Longa. After him come other kings of Alba: Procas, Capys, Numitor, and another Silvius Aeneas. They wear crowns of oak leaves and will found many towns, places that have no names yet. Then comes Romulus, son of the war god Mars and the priestess Ilia. Under him, Rome will rule the whole earth and enclose seven hills in one wall. Anchises compares Rome to the mother goddess Cybele, proud of her hundred divine grandchildren.

Then Anchises tells Aeneas to look at "his Romans". Here is Caesar and all the descendants of Iulus. And here is the man so often promised, Augustus Caesar, son of a god. He will bring back the Golden Age to Latium, the age of peace when the god Saturn ruled. His empire will reach Africa and India and lands beyond the stars. Even now, distant kingdoms fear his coming. Not even Hercules or Bacchus travelled so far. Anchises asks: how can Aeneas still hesitate to show courage, or be afraid to settle in Italy?

Next Anchises shows the early kings of Rome. Numa, an old man crowned with olive, will give the young city laws. Tullus will lead the Romans to war again, and Ancus will love popular praise too much. Then come the Tarquin kings and Brutus, who drove out the last king and became the first consul. When his own sons plot to bring back the king, Brutus will order their death for the sake of freedom. Anchises calls him unhappy, but says that love of country and desire for honour will win. Other great Romans follow: families famous for sacrifice and courage, and Camillus, who brought back the Roman standards.

Then Anchises points to two souls who now stand together in peace. When they reach life, they will fight a terrible civil war. One will come down from the Alps, and the other will face him with the armies of the East. These are Julius Caesar and Pompey. Anchises begs them not to turn their strength against their own country. He asks the one descended from the gods, Caesar, to be the first to put down his weapons. Next he shows the generals who will defeat Corinth and Greece and so take revenge for Troy. He names Cato, the Gracchi family, the two Scipios who will destroy Carthage, Fabricius who was great though poor, Serranus the farmer, and Fabius, who will save Rome by delaying.

Then Anchises speaks his most famous words about Rome's mission. Other peoples, he says, will make better statues from bronze and marble. They will speak better in court and better describe the movement of the stars. But the Roman's task is to rule the peoples of the world. His art will be to create the habit of peace, to spare those who are defeated, and to crush the proud.

Anchises then points to Marcellus, a great general who will save Rome in a time of danger and defeat the Carthaginians and the Gauls. Beside him walks a beautiful young man in shining armour, but his face is sad and a dark shadow hangs over his head. Aeneas asks who he is. With tears Anchises says: do not ask about this great sorrow. Fate will show this young man to the world only briefly. If he had lived, the gods would have thought Rome too powerful. Anchises describes the great funeral that Rome will see by the Tiber. No young Trojan will give the Latins such hope. If only he could break free of his hard fate, he would be a second Marcellus. Anchises asks for handfuls of lilies and flowers to scatter for him, a useless gift but all he can give. This young man is the younger Marcellus, the nephew of Augustus, who was expected to be the next emperor but died young in 23 BC. An ancient story says that when Virgil read this passage aloud, the young man's mother, Octavia, fainted.

Anchises shows Aeneas the whole place and fills his heart with desire for future glory. Then he tells him about the war that he must fight next in Italy, about the Laurentine people and the city of King Latinus, and how to avoid or bear each difficulty.

There are two gates of Sleep. One is made of horn, and true visions go out through it. The other is made of shining white ivory, and the spirits send false dreams through it. Anchises leads his son and the Sibyl out through the ivory gate. Scholars still argue about why Virgil sends Aeneas out through the gate of false dreams, and the question has no single accepted answer. Aeneas goes back to his ships and his friends. They sail along the coast to the harbour of Caieta, drop anchor, and pull the ships onto the beach. This book ends the first half of the poem, the half about wandering.

## Book 7: Arrival in Latium and the Start of War

The book begins with a death. Caieta, the old nurse of Aeneas, dies, and the Trojans bury her in the harbour where they landed at the end of Book 6. Virgil says the place still carries her name, the town of Gaeta in Italy. When the funeral is over and the sea is calm, Aeneas sails on. They pass by night the land of Circe, a goddess and witch who sings while she weaves. From her palace come the angry roars of lions, bears, wild pigs, and wolves. These animals were once men, and Circe changed them with her magic herbs. To protect the good Trojans from such a fate, Neptune fills their sails with good winds and carries them safely past her shores.

At dawn the wind stops, and the men must row. Then Aeneas sees a great forest from the sea, with the pleasant river Tiber flowing through it into the sea, yellow with sand. Many birds sing in the trees along its banks. Aeneas tells his men to turn toward land, and full of joy they row into the shady river. They have reached the place that fate promised.

Here Virgil calls on Erato, one of the Muses, for new help. He says a greater story and a greater task now begin. He will tell of the kings and the situation of ancient Latium, and of terrible wars, armies, and kings driven to their deaths by their pride. This is a second beginning. The first half of the poem was like Homer's Odyssey, a story of travel. The second half will be like the Iliad, a story of war.

Latium is ruled by King Latinus, who is now old and has ruled for a long time in peace. He is descended from the god Faunus, and through him from Saturn, the old god of Italy. Latinus has no living son, because his son died young. He has only one daughter, Lavinia, who is now old enough to marry. Many young men of Italy want to marry her. The most handsome and powerful is Turnus, the king of the Rutulian people, from an old and noble family. Queen Amata, Lavinia's mother, wants very much for Lavinia to marry him. But terrible signs from the gods stand in the way.

In the middle of the palace grows a holy laurel tree, which Latinus once dedicated to Apollo. One day a great swarm of bees flies through the air and hangs from the top of this tree. A prophet explains the sign. A foreign man and a foreign army will arrive from the same direction as the bees and will rule from the citadel. Then, while Lavinia stands next to her father at an altar, her long hair seems to catch fire. Her crown and her clothes burn, and she spreads smoke and fire through the whole palace. The prophets say that she will become famous and fortunate, but that her people will face a great war.

Worried by these signs, Latinus goes to the oracle of his father Faunus, in a dark forest with a holy spring. People sleep there on the skins of sacrificed sheep, and in their dreams they hear the voices of the gods. Latinus sacrifices a hundred sheep and lies down. A voice tells him not to marry his daughter to a Latin man. Foreign men will come and become his sons-in-law. Their blood will raise his family's name to the stars, and their descendants will rule the whole world. Latinus does not keep this message secret, and Rumour has already spread it through Italy by the time the Trojans tie their ships to the river bank.

Aeneas, Iulus, and the leaders sit under a tree for a meal. They put their food on flat round cakes of bread, as Jupiter guides them to do. When they have eaten everything else, they are still hungry, so they eat the bread cakes too. Iulus laughs and says, "Look, we are even eating our tables!" Aeneas immediately understands. This was the strange prophecy about eating tables. He greets the land with joy. This is their promised home, he says, their true country. He says he now remembers that his father Anchises told him this secret: when hunger makes them eat their tables on an unknown shore, there they must build their home. In Book 3 the Harpy Celaeno made this prophecy, not Anchises. Virgil did not finish revising his poem, and this may be one of the small differences he would have corrected. Aeneas prays to the spirit of the place, the Earth, the nymphs, the rivers, Night, Jupiter, and his own parents. Jupiter thunders three times and shows a cloud full of golden light. The Trojans celebrate, sure that the day has come to found their city.

The next morning they explore the area. They learn the names of the river Tiber, the spring of Numicus, and the people, the Latins. Aeneas sends a hundred messengers carrying olive branches, a sign of peace, to the king's city with gifts. Meanwhile he marks out his first settlement on the shore with a ditch and builds walls like a military camp.

The messengers reach the Latin city, where young men are training with horses, bows, and spears. Latinus receives them in his great palace. It stands on a hundred columns among holy trees. It is a temple, a senate house, and a hall for sacred feasts. Statues of old kings and gods stand in the entrance, like Italus, Sabinus, Saturn, and the two-faced god Janus. Captured weapons hang on the doors. There is also the statue of Picus, an ancient king, whom Circe turned into a woodpecker when he refused her love.

Latinus speaks kindly. He knows who the Trojans are. He asks what they want: did they lose their way or come because of a storm? He tells them that the Latins are the people of Saturn. They are just by nature, not because laws force them. He remembers an old story that Dardanus, the founder of Troy, was born here in Italy.

Ilioneus answers for the Trojans. No storm or mistake brought them here, he says. They came on purpose. They are refugees from the greatest kingdom the sun ever saw. Their family comes from Jupiter, and so does their king, Aeneas. Everyone in the world has heard of the war between Europe and Asia at Troy. They ask only for a small piece of land for their gods, a safe shore, and the air and water that are free to all. They will bring honour to Latium, he says. Many peoples have wanted to make friends with them, but the gods sent them to this land. Dardanus came from here, and Apollo has ordered them to return. Then he gives Latinus gifts saved from Troy, including Priam's sceptre, his crown, and robes made by Trojan women.

Latinus listens, looking down and thinking. He is less interested in the gifts than in the question of his daughter's marriage. He remembers the oracle of Faunus. This must be the foreign son-in-law that fate has promised. He answers with joy. The Trojans will have land, and he will be their friend. He asks Aeneas to come in person and shake his hand as a sign of peace. Then he tells the messengers that he has a daughter whom the gods forbid him to marry to a Latin man. He believes Aeneas is the man fate has chosen. He gives each Trojan a fine horse with golden decorations, and he sends Aeneas a chariot pulled by two horses of divine breed that breathe fire. The messengers ride back with the promise of peace.

But Juno, flying back from Greece, sees the Trojans happily building their homes in Italy. She is struck with sharp pain. She speaks with fury. This hated people! They could not be killed at Troy, or captured, or burned. She has used the forces of the sky and sea against them, and storms, sandbanks, Scylla, and Charybdis, and still they have reached the Tiber. Other gods were allowed to destroy whole peoples for small reasons, she says, but she, the wife of Jupiter, is defeated by Aeneas. Then she says one of the most famous lines of the poem: if she cannot move the gods of heaven, she will move the powers of hell. She knows she cannot stop Aeneas from ruling in Latium or from marrying Lavinia, because fate has decided this. But she can delay it, and she can destroy the peoples of both kings. Let the blood of Trojans and Rutulians be Lavinia's wedding gift, she says, and let the goddess of war be her bridesmaid. She says that Venus's son will be a second Paris, and his marriage will burn a new Troy.

Juno goes down to earth and calls Allecto from the underworld. Allecto is one of the Furies, the goddesses of revenge. She loves war, anger, lies, and hatred. Even her father Pluto and her sisters hate her. She changes her face into many terrible shapes, and black snakes grow from her body. Juno asks her to break the peace between the Latins and the Trojans and to make everyone want war.

Allecto goes first to Queen Amata, who is already angry that the Trojans have come and that Turnus may lose Lavinia. Allecto throws a snake from her hair into Amata's dress. The snake slides over her body without being felt. It becomes her golden necklace and the ribbon in her hair, and its poison slowly enters her heart. At first, before the poison is complete, Amata speaks gently to Latinus, crying over her daughter. Will he give Lavinia to a Trojan exile? That man will sail away at the first wind and take the girl with him, just as Paris carried Helen to Troy. What about his promise to Turnus, their relative? If Lavinia must marry a foreigner, Amata argues, then Turnus himself counts as a foreigner, because his ancestors came from Mycenae in Greece. But Latinus does not change his mind.

Then the poison fills her completely, and Amata goes mad. She rushes through the city like a spinning top that boys whip around a hall. She pretends to be possessed by Bacchus, the god of wine, and takes Lavinia into the mountain forests to hide her and stop the wedding. She cries that Lavinia belongs only to Bacchus. Other women, also driven mad, leave their homes and follow her with their hair loose, wearing animal skins. Amata holds a burning torch and sings the wedding song of Turnus and Lavinia. She calls on all the mothers of Latium to join her.

Next, Allecto flies to Ardea, the city of Turnus. It is midnight and he is sleeping. Allecto takes the form of an old woman named Calybe, a priestess of Juno. She tells Turnus that he is being cheated. The king has refused him his bride and is giving the kingdom to a foreigner. Juno herself commands him to arm his people and burn the Trojan ships in the river. If Latinus does not keep his promise, let him feel the power of Turnus's army.

But Turnus laughs at her. He already knows about the ships, he says, and he does not need to be frightened. Juno has not forgotten him. He tells the "old woman" that age has made her weak and foolish. Her job is to look after the temple. War and peace are the business of men. These words make Allecto burn with anger. Suddenly Turnus shakes with fear. The snakes in her hair hiss, and she grows huge. She mocks him by repeating his own words: "Look at me, the weak old woman! I come from the home of the Furies, and I carry war and death in my hand." She throws a burning torch into his chest. Turnus wakes up covered in sweat and screams for his weapons. He is filled with love of war, like water boiling over a fire. He orders his chief soldiers to march against Latinus, saying that the peace has been broken. He will defend Italy and drive out the enemy, and he alone is enough to fight both Trojans and Latins. The Rutulians follow him eagerly, because of his beauty, his royal family, and his brave deeds.

Then Allecto flies to the Trojans. Iulus is out hunting by the river. Allecto puts the scent of a deer into the noses of his dogs. This deer is no ordinary animal. It is a tame, beautiful stag raised by the children of Tyrrheus, the man who keeps the king's herds. Their sister Silvia loves it very much. She puts flowers on its antlers, combs its fur, and washes it in the spring. The stag wanders in the woods but always comes home at night. Now the dogs chase it, and Iulus, wanting the glory of the hunt, shoots an arrow. A god guides his hand, and the arrow goes deep into the stag's side. The wounded animal runs home and fills the house with cries, as if it were begging for help. Silvia beats her arms and calls the farmers for help. They come quickly, armed with whatever they find: burnt sticks, heavy branches, and axes. Tyrrheus himself comes with the axe he was using to split wood.

Allecto climbs onto a farm roof and blows a war trumpet. The terrible sound echoes through the forests and over rivers and lakes, and mothers hold their babies close in fear. The farmers grab their weapons and gather. The Trojans also run out of their camp to help Ascanius. Soon this is no longer a fight with sticks. Real swords flash in the fields, and the battle grows like the sea rising in a storm. Almo, the eldest son of Tyrrheus, is killed by an arrow in his throat. Old Galaesus, the most just and the richest man in the region, is killed while he tries to make peace between the two sides.

Allecto flies to Juno, proud of her work. War has begun, she says. Now let them try to be friends! She offers to do even more and spread war to all the nearby towns. But Juno says that enough has been done. The fighting has started and blood has been spilled. This will be the "wedding" of Aeneas and Latinus's daughter. Jupiter would not like Allecto to wander freely in the upper world, so she must go home. Juno will handle the rest herself. Allecto flies away and sinks into a deep, poisonous valley in the middle of Italy, an opening to the underworld.

The shepherds carry their dead into the city: young Almo and Galaesus with his face destroyed. They call on the gods and on Latinus. Turnus is there and makes the fear worse. He shouts that the Trojans are being invited to rule them, that Trojan blood is mixing with theirs, and that he is being thrown out. The families of the women whom Amata led into the forest also gather. Everyone, against all the signs and oracles of the gods, shouts for war and surrounds the king's palace. Latinus stands firm like a rock in the sea, while waves crash all around it. But in the end he cannot stop them. He cries out that fate has broken them and they are driven by a storm. The people will pay for this with their own blood. Turnus will face a terrible punishment and will pray to the gods too late. As for himself, Latinus says, he was close to rest and a peaceful harbour. He loses only a happy death. Then he shuts himself in his palace and gives up the rule of the country.

Virgil explains an old Latin custom that Rome still keeps. There are two Gates of War, sacred to Mars, the god of war, and closed with a hundred bronze bolts and iron bars. Janus, the god of doors, guards them. When the senate decides on war, the consul, the highest Roman official, opens these gates with his own hand and calls out the war. The people want Latinus to open the gates and declare war on the Trojans. But he refuses and hides himself. So Juno herself comes down from the sky, pushes open the gates with her own hand, and throws the doors of war wide. In Virgil's time, Augustus was proud of closing these gates as a sign of peace. Here Virgil shows how they were first opened in Italy.

All of Italy, quiet until now, is suddenly on fire with war. Men prepare their horses and weapons, polish their shields, and sharpen their spears and axes. Five great cities make swords. Farmers turn their tools into weapons and reforge their fathers' old swords. The trumpets sound.

Now Virgil asks the Muses to help him name the kings and armies who went to war. This is a long list of the Italian peoples, like the famous list of ships in Homer's Iliad. First comes Mezentius from the Etruscan coast, a cruel man who hates the gods. With him is his son Lausus, the most handsome young man after Turnus, a great horse rider and hunter. He leads a thousand men from the city of Agylla. Virgil says Lausus deserved a happier rule and a better father.

Next comes Aventinus, a son of Hercules, in a chariot. He wears a lion skin like his father, and his shield shows the many-headed snake that Hercules killed. Then come twin brothers from Tibur, Catillus and Coras, who rush into battle like two Centaurs coming down from a mountain. Caeculus, the founder of Praeneste, believed to be a son of Vulcan, leads a group of country soldiers. Many of them have no armour and fight with slings that throw lead bullets. They wear wolfskin caps and have one bare foot. Messapus, a son of Neptune who cannot be harmed by fire or iron, calls his peaceful peoples back to war. They march singing, like a flock of swans. Clausus, of old Sabine blood, leads a huge army. Virgil says that the Claudian family of Rome comes from him. Halaesus, an old enemy of Troy who once served Agamemnon, leads a thousand men from the wine country. Oebalus leads men who throw spears and wear cork-bark helmets. Ufens leads a rough people from the mountains who farm in armour and live by hunting and raiding.

Umbro, a priest of the Marsian people, comes too. He can put snakes to sleep and heal their bites with his songs and touch. But he will not be able to heal himself from a Trojan spear, and the woods and lakes of his land will cry for him. Then comes Virbius, the son of Hippolytus. Virgil tells the story of Hippolytus. He was torn apart by his own frightened horses after his stepmother falsely accused him. Diana loved him and brought him back to life with the help of a healer, but Jupiter was angry that a dead man returned to life. So Diana hid him in a forest in Italy under a new name, Virbius. That is why horses are not allowed in Diana's sacred grove. Still, his son drives horses to war.

Among the first is Turnus himself, taller than everyone by a whole head. His helmet carries the Chimaera, a monster breathing fire, which seems to grow wilder as the battle grows more bloody. On his golden shield is Io, a girl turned into a cow, with Argus, her guard, and her father, the river god Inachus. These Greek images remind us that Turnus's family came from Argos. Behind him march many peoples from the Tiber valley, the Rutulian hills, and the coast.

Last comes Camilla, a warrior girl from the Volscian people, leading her cavalry in shining bronze. She has never used her hands for spinning or weaving like other women. She is trained to fight and to run faster than the wind. She could run over the tops of wheat without bending them, or over the sea without wetting her feet. All the people, especially the mothers, come out of their homes to look at her in amazement. They admire her purple cloak, the gold in her hair, her quiver, and her shepherd's spear tipped with iron. Book 7 ends with this bright image, just before the war begins.

## Book 8: Evander and the Shield of Aeneas

Turnus raises the flag of war on the towers of the Latin city, and the trumpets sound. All of Latium rises in arms. The Italian leaders Messapus, Ufens, and Mezentius, the man who hates the gods, gather soldiers from everywhere and take the farmers from their fields. They also send a man named Venulus to the city of Diomedes, the great Greek hero who fought at Troy and now lives in southern Italy. Venulus must ask him for help. He is to tell Diomedes that the Trojans have landed with their defeated gods, that Aeneas calls himself the king chosen by fate, and that many peoples are joining him. Diomedes, they say, should know better than anyone what Aeneas really wants.

Aeneas sees all this, and his mind is full of worry. His thoughts jump from one idea to another, like light reflected from water in a bowl that dances over the walls and ceiling. At night, sick at heart about the war, he lies down on the river bank under the cold sky and at last falls asleep. In a dream the god of the river, old Father Tiber, rises from the water among the trees, dressed in grey and crowned with reeds. He tells Aeneas that this is truly his home and that he should not be afraid of war. The anger of the gods has passed. As a sign that this is not just a dream, Aeneas will find a great white sow lying under oak trees on the shore with thirty white piglets. Here Virgil explains the sign in a new way: after thirty years, Ascanius will found the city of Alba, whose name means "white". Then the river gives practical advice. Not far upriver lives a people from Arcadia in Greece, led by King Evander. They have built a city on the hills called Pallanteum, named after their ancestor Pallas. They are always at war with the Latins. Aeneas should make them his allies. The river will carry him there. Aeneas should also pray to Juno to soften her anger. Then the god sinks back into the water.

Aeneas wakes and prays to the nymphs and to Father Tiber, promising to honour him always. He prepares two ships with rowers and soldiers. Suddenly they see the white sow with her thirty white piglets lying on the green bank, just as the river god said. Aeneas sacrifices her and her young to Juno. All night the Tiber calms its current, so that the water is as still as a lake and easy to row on. The Trojans row upstream for a night and a day, under the shade of trees. The river and the woods are amazed to see shining shields and painted ships. At midday they see walls, a citadel, and a few scattered houses. Virgil notes that this is the place where the power of Rome now rises to the sky. But at that time Evander ruled only a small, poor state.

On this day Evander and his people are holding their yearly festival for Hercules in a grove outside the city. With him are his young son Pallas, the leaders of the city, and his small senate. When they see the ships gliding silently through the woods, they are frightened and get up from the feast. But Pallas tells them not to stop the holy rites. He grabs a spear, runs to a small hill, and calls out. Who are these strangers? Where are they going? Do they bring peace or war? Aeneas stands on the high stern of the ship and holds out an olive branch. He says that they are Trojans, enemies of the Latins, who have attacked them. They have come to ask Evander for a military alliance. Pallas is amazed at the great name of Troy. He invites Aeneas to come ashore and speak to his father, and he takes his hand warmly.

Aeneas speaks politely to Evander. He is not afraid to come to a Greek king, he says, because the gods, his own courage, and their shared family have led him here. He explains their family tree. Dardanus, the founder of Troy, was the son of Electra, and Electra was the daughter of the giant Atlas. Evander's father is the god Mercury, whose mother Maia was also a daughter of Atlas. So both families come from the same blood. That is why Aeneas has come himself instead of sending messengers. The same enemy, the Rutulians, attacks both of them. If the Trojans are driven out, the Rutulians will rule all of Italy. He asks Evander for friendship and offers brave soldiers in return.

Evander has been looking at Aeneas's face the whole time. He greets him with joy. He recognizes the words, the voice, and the face of Anchises. When Evander was a young man, Priam visited Arcadia with Anchises. Evander admired Anchises more than anyone else and wanted to shake his hand. Anchises gave him gifts: a quiver with arrows, a cloak with gold, and two golden horse bits that now belong to Pallas. So Evander says that their alliance is already made. Tomorrow he will send the Trojans away with help. But today they must join the festival. He puts Aeneas in the seat of honour on a lion skin, and they eat the meat of the sacrifice together.

After the meal, Evander explains why they honour Hercules. This festival is not empty superstition. They celebrate it because Hercules saved them from great danger. He points to a hillside where huge rocks lie broken. There was once a deep, dark cave here where the monster Cacus lived. Cacus was half man, a son of Vulcan who breathed black fire. The ground of his cave was always wet with blood, and human heads hung on his door. Then Hercules came this way, driving the great cattle he had taken from the three-bodied giant Geryon. Cacus stole eight of the best animals. To hide his crime, he pulled them backward by their tails into his cave, so their footprints pointed the wrong way. When Hercules was leaving, his cattle mooed, and one stolen cow answered from inside the cave.

Hercules became wild with anger. He grabbed his heavy wooden club and ran up the hill. For the first time, people saw Cacus afraid. He ran into his cave and blocked the door with a huge rock hung on iron chains. Hercules went around the mountain three times, attacked the door three times, and rested three times. Then he found a tall sharp rock above the cave. He pushed it from the side until he tore it loose. It fell with a great crash, the river banks jumped apart, and the river flowed backward in fear. The cave was opened to the daylight, like the underworld opened to the sky. Hercules threw rocks and branches down at Cacus. Cacus filled the cave with smoke and fire so that nothing could be seen. Then Hercules jumped down into the smoke and flames, grabbed Cacus, and choked him until his eyes came out. The stolen cattle were freed, and the monster's body was dragged out. People could not stop looking at his terrible face. Since then they have celebrated this day at the Great Altar of Hercules. This story shows a hero who defeats a monster of disorder, and many readers see it as a model for Aeneas, and later for Augustus.

Evander invites everyone to put leaves on their heads and pour wine for Hercules. In the evening, the priests come with torches and make more offerings. Two choirs, one of young men and one of old men, sing songs about Hercules. They sing how, as a baby, he strangled the two snakes Juno sent to kill him. They sing about his twelve great labours, his victory over monsters and Centaurs, his trip to the underworld, and his fight with the snake of Lerna. Most of all they sing of Cacus. The woods and hills echo with their songs.

Then they walk back to the city. Old Evander walks with Aeneas and Pallas and tells stories on the way. Aeneas looks around with pleasure and asks about every place. Evander, the founder of what will become Rome, explains the history of the land. Once, Fauns and nymphs lived in these woods, with a people born from trees. They had no laws, no farming, and no stored food, and lived by hunting. Then Saturn came, driven from heaven by his son Jupiter. He brought the wild people together, gave them laws, and called the land Latium, from a word meaning "to hide", because he had hidden here safely. His reign was the Golden Age of peace. But slowly a worse age came, with the madness of war and the love of money. Other peoples came, and the land changed its name many times. Kings came, including the giant Thybris, from whom the river Tiber took its name. Evander himself was driven from his country. Fate, his mother the nymph Carmentis, and Apollo brought him here.

Evander shows Aeneas places that every Roman reader would know. There is the altar and gate of Carmentis, who first sang of the future greatness of Aeneas's people. There is the grove where Romulus later gave shelter to people, and the Lupercal, the cave sacred to the god Pan. There is the Tarpeian rock and the Capitol, today golden with temples but then covered with wild bushes. Even then, people felt a god living there. The Arcadians believe that Jupiter himself has appeared there in storms. Evander also shows the ruins of two old towns built by Janus and Saturn. As they walk to Evander's simple house, they see cattle mooing in the place that will later be the Roman Forum, the city's great public square. This walk is one of the most loved passages of the poem. Virgil lets us see the simple countryside under the great city of his own time.

At the door, Evander says that Hercules himself once came into this small house. He tells his guest to dare to despise riches, to make himself worthy of a god, and not to look down on their poverty. He gives Aeneas a bed of leaves covered with a bearskin. Night falls.

Meanwhile Venus is afraid because of the threats of the Latins. She goes to her husband Vulcan, the god of fire and the smith of the gods, in their golden bedroom. She reminds him that during the Trojan War she never asked him to make weapons for Troy, even though she cried often for Aeneas. Now Aeneas has reached Italy by Jupiter's command, so she comes as a mother to ask for armour for her son. Other goddesses have asked him for armour for their sons, she says, like Thetis for Achilles. Then she puts her soft white arms around him. Vulcan feels the old fire of love run through him. He tells her that she does not need to plead. Even at Troy he could have helped, and fate would have allowed Troy to stand ten more years. Now he will do everything his art can do. Then he sleeps in her arms.

In the middle of the night Vulcan gets up early, like a poor woman who wakes before dawn to spin wool by firelight to feed her children. He goes to his workshop on a volcanic island near Sicily. In the caves under the island the Cyclopes work at their forges, hitting iron on anvils. They are making a thunderbolt for Jupiter, out of rain, cloud, fire, and wind. They are also making a chariot for Mars and the terrible shield of Minerva with the head of the Gorgon on it. Vulcan tells them to stop everything. They must now make armour for a hero, and quickly. They all start working. They melt bronze, gold, and steel, and make an enormous shield, strong enough to stop all the weapons of the Latins, with seven layers of metal.

In Pallanteum, Evander wakes at dawn to the songs of birds. He dresses simply, with a panther skin and his sword, and two dogs follow him. He meets Aeneas, who is also up early. Pallas comes with his father, and Achates with Aeneas. Evander says that his own people are too few to help such a great hero. On one side the Tiber blocks them, and on the other the Rutulians attack their walls. But he has found a much greater ally for Aeneas. Not far away is the old city of Agylla, in Etruria, where people of Lydian origin live. For many years their king was Mezentius, a cruel tyrant. He did terrible things. He even tied living people to dead bodies, hand to hand and face to face, and let them die slowly. At last the people rose against him, killed his guards, and set fire to his house. He escaped to the Rutulians, and Turnus protects him. Now all of Etruria is angry and wants to punish him. Their ships are ready, but an old prophet has stopped them. He said that no Italian may lead them, and that they must choose a foreign leader. So their leader, Tarchon, has sent Evander the crown and sceptre and asked him to lead them. But Evander is too old. His son Pallas cannot lead them either, because his mother was Italian. Aeneas, foreign and in the prime of life, is the leader that fate has chosen. Evander will also send Pallas with him, to learn the hard work of war by watching Aeneas. He will give Aeneas two hundred Arcadian horsemen, and Pallas will bring two hundred more of his own.

Aeneas and Achates sit silently, thinking sadly of the many hard tasks ahead. Then Venus sends a sign. Lightning flashes and thunder crashes from a clear sky, and a sound like an Etruscan war trumpet roars through the air. They look up and see armour shining red through a cloud and hear it clashing. The others are amazed, but Aeneas understands. His mother promised to send this sign and to bring him armour made by Vulcan if war came. He says: "Do not ask what this means. It is I who am called by heaven." Then he speaks of the coming war. How many Latins will die! How Turnus will pay! How many shields, helmets, and bodies the Tiber will roll under its waves! Let them break the treaty and call for war.

Aeneas makes offerings at the altar of Hercules and to the household gods. Then he chooses his bravest men to come with him. The others sail back down the river to tell Ascanius what has happened. The Arcadians give horses to the Trojans, and Aeneas gets a special horse covered with a lion skin with golden claws. News spreads through the small town that they are riding to the Etruscan king. The mothers pray in fear.

Evander holds his son's hand and cries. He wishes he were young again, as when he killed King Erulus in battle. Erulus had three lives, given by his goddess mother, so he had to be killed three times. If Evander were still young, he would never be separated from his son, and Mezentius would never have killed so many of his people. He prays to Jupiter. If the gods will keep Pallas safe and let him see his son again, then he wants to live and will bear any pain. But if Fortune plans some terrible disaster, let him die now, while he can still hope, while he is still holding his dear boy, before any worse news reaches him. After these words, he faints, and his servants carry him inside.

The horsemen ride out of the gates, with Aeneas and Achates in front. Pallas rides in the middle in his bright cloak and decorated armour, shining like the Morning Star. Mothers stand on the walls and watch the cloud of dust. The riders come to a holy grove near the river of Caere, where the Etruscan army is camped under Tarchon. There they rest.

Then Venus comes down from the clouds with her gifts. She finds her son alone by the cold river and gives him the armour Vulcan has made. Now, she says, he will not need to fear Turnus or the proud Latins. She places the shining armour under an oak tree. Aeneas is full of joy and cannot stop looking at each piece. There is a helmet with a terrible crest that seems to shoot out flames, a breastplate red as blood, shining leg guards of gold, and a spear. Most wonderful of all is the shield, whose design is beyond words. On it Vulcan, who knows the future, has made the story of Italy and the victories of the Romans.

Virgil describes the pictures on the shield. This is like the famous shield of Achilles in Homer's Iliad, but here the pictures show the future history of Rome. There is the she-wolf in the cave of Mars, feeding the twin boys Romulus and Remus while they play without fear. She licks them into shape with her tongue. There is Rome, with the Romans taking the Sabine women at the games, and the war that followed, and then peace made at Jupiter's altar between Romulus and the Sabine king. There is Mettus, a traitor from Alba, torn apart between two chariots because he broke his word. There is Porsena, an Etruscan king, trying to bring back the exiled king Tarquin and surrounding Rome. The Romans fight for their freedom. Porsena looks angry because Horatius Cocles breaks down the bridge, and the girl Cloelia escapes and swims across the river.

At the top of the shield, Manlius defends the Capitol. A silver goose flies through the golden temple halls, crying out that the Gauls are coming in the night. The Gauls have golden hair, golden clothes, and white necks with golden chains, and they carry Alpine spears and long shields. There are also priests dancing and other religious rites of Rome. Further away is the underworld. The traitor Catiline hangs on a rock and trembles before the Furies, and in another place the good people are given laws by Cato.

In the middle of the shield is the sea, made of gold with white waves, and silver dolphins swimming in circles. In the centre is the great sea battle of Actium. On one side stands Augustus Caesar on the high stern of his ship. He leads Italy, with the senate, the people, and the gods of Rome. Flames shine around his head, and his father's star appears above him. Agrippa, his general, leads another part of the fleet. On the other side is Antony with foreign armies from the East, from Egypt and far Bactria. With him, to Virgil's shame, is his Egyptian wife, Queen Cleopatra. The ships crash together like moving mountains. Fire and spears fly, and the sea turns red with blood. Cleopatra calls her ships with an Egyptian rattle and does not yet see the two snakes behind her, a sign of her coming death. The strange animal-headed gods of Egypt fight against Neptune, Venus, and Minerva. Mars rages, and Discord and the war goddess Bellona walk among the fighters. Then Apollo of Actium draws his bow above them. In terror, Egypt, India, the Arabs, and all the eastern peoples turn and run. Cleopatra lets out her sails and flees, pale with death. The great river god Nile opens his robes to welcome the defeated people into his waters.

Finally Augustus rides into Rome in a triple triumph, a great victory parade. He dedicates three hundred temples to the gods. The streets are full of joy, games, and shouting. There are altars and sacrifices in every temple. Augustus sits at the white door of Apollo's temple and receives gifts from the nations of the world. The defeated peoples walk past in a long line, each with its own language, clothes, and weapons: Africans, peoples of Asia, and peoples from the far north. Even the great rivers, the Euphrates and the Rhine, seem calmer and conquered.

Aeneas looks at all of this with wonder. He does not understand these events, because they have not happened yet. But he is happy with the pictures. He lifts the shield onto his shoulder, carrying the fame and the fate of his descendants. This image of Aeneas carrying the future of Rome on his back reminds us of Book 2, where he carried his father, the past of Troy.

## Book 9: The Attack on the Trojan Camp

While Aeneas is far away with Evander and the Etruscans, Juno sends Iris, the rainbow goddess, to Turnus. Turnus is sitting in a holy forest of his ancestor. Iris tells him that the day has given him something no god would have dared to promise. Aeneas has left his town, his friends, and his ships to visit Evander, and he has gone even further to gather Etruscan farmers into an army. Why wait? Now is the time to attack the Trojan camp while they are confused. Then Iris flies up into the sky in a great bow of colour. Turnus recognizes her and prays. He says he will follow this sign, whoever is calling him to war. He takes water from the river and makes many vows to the gods.

The Italian army moves across the open plain, rich in horses and golden clothes. Messapus leads the front, the sons of Tyrrheus lead the rear, and Turnus commands the centre. A Trojan named Caicus sees a dark cloud of dust rising on the plain. He shouts to his friends to grab their weapons and climb the walls, because the enemy is coming. The Trojans run into their camp and fill the walls. Before leaving, Aeneas gave them clear orders. If anything happened, they must not go out and fight on the open plain. They must stay inside the walls and defend the camp. So even though they feel ashamed and angry, they close the gates and wait inside the towers.

Turnus rides ahead of his slow army with twenty chosen horsemen. He rides a white-spotted horse and wears a golden helmet with a red crest. He throws a spear into the air to begin the battle. His men follow with terrible shouts. They are surprised that the Trojans are such cowards that they will not come out and fight in the open. Turnus rides around and around the walls looking for a way in, like a hungry wolf outside a sheep pen at night. He can hear the lambs safe inside, but he cannot reach them, and his anger grows. Then he sees the Trojan ships, which are next to the camp, protected only by earth walls and the river. He calls for fire, takes a burning pine torch, and his men follow with burning sticks. Smoke and sparks rise to the sky.

Here Virgil asks the Muses which god saved the Trojan ships from the fire. He tells an old story. When Aeneas first built his fleet from the trees of Mount Ida near Troy, the trees belonged to Cybele, the great Mother of the Gods. She gave them gladly, but she was afraid for her holy pines. She asked her son Jupiter to make these ships safe from storms and wrecks. Jupiter said that ships made by human hands cannot be immortal. But he made a promise. When the ships had done their work and carried Aeneas to Italy, he would take away their wooden bodies and turn them into goddesses of the sea. He swore this by the river Styx, the strongest oath of the gods, and all of Olympus shook.

Now the day has come. A strange light fills the sky, and the voice of the goddess speaks loudly to both Trojans and Rutulians. She tells the Trojans not to worry about guarding her ships. Turnus will sooner burn the sea than these holy pines. Then she tells the ships to go free and become goddesses of the sea. At once, each ship breaks its rope and dives into the water like a dolphin. Then, amazingly, they rise again as young women, the same number as the ships, and swim out to sea.

The Rutulians are struck silent. Messapus is frightened and his horses are confused, and even the river Tiber stops and turns back. But Turnus does not lose courage. He tells his men that these signs are against the Trojans. Jupiter has taken away their ships, so now they cannot escape by sea. They have lost half the world, and the land belongs to the Italians. He is not afraid of the Trojans' prophecies. Fate and Venus have been satisfied, he says, because the Trojans have touched Italy. But he has his own fate: to destroy the people who stole his bride. The Greeks went to war over a stolen wife, and so can he. The Trojans hide behind walls, but they saw the walls of Troy burn, even though Neptune built them. He does not need armour made by Vulcan or a thousand ships. He will not use tricks like the Greeks, hiding in a horse or stealing statues in the night. He will attack openly in daylight, and they will not hold out for ten years as Troy did. He tells his men to rest for the night and be ready for battle.

Messapus places guards at the gates and lights fires around the camp. Fourteen Rutulian captains, each with a hundred men, keep watch. But they lie on the grass drinking wine and playing games through the night. The Trojans watch from their walls, checking the gates and preparing weapons. Mnestheus and Serestus are in command, because Aeneas left them in charge.

At one gate stand Nisus and Euryalus, the two friends from the foot race in Book 5. Nisus is a brave warrior with spear and arrows. Euryalus is the most beautiful of the Trojans, a boy whose face is just beginning to show a beard. They love each other and always fight side by side. Nisus speaks. Do the gods put this burning desire into our hearts, he wonders, or does each man's strong wish become his god? For a long time he has wanted to do some great deed. He sees that the Rutulians are drunk and asleep, and their fires are going out. Everyone in the camp wants someone to go and tell Aeneas what has happened. Nisus believes he can find a way through to Pallanteum. If the leaders promise a reward to Euryalus, the glory will be enough for Nisus.

Euryalus is shocked. Does Nisus want to leave him behind? His father did not raise him to be afraid, and he has followed Aeneas through all dangers. He too thinks that honour is worth more than life. Nisus explains gently. He never doubted Euryalus's courage. But if something goes wrong, and it often does on such missions, he wants Euryalus to live, because he is younger. Then someone will be left to bury Nisus, or at least give him a funeral. Also, he does not want to cause pain to Euryalus's mother. She is the only mother who followed her son all the way, instead of staying safely in Sicily. But Euryalus will not change his mind. They wake other guards to take their place and go to find the leaders.

The Trojan leaders are holding a council in the middle of the night. They are discussing what to do and who can carry a message to Aeneas. Nisus and Euryalus ask to speak. Iulus lets them in and tells Nisus to talk. Nisus explains that the Rutulians are asleep and drunk. They have found an open path near the gate by the sea, where the line of fires is broken. They know the way, because they have often hunted along the river. They promise to come back with Aeneas and with rich spoils.

Old Aletes is moved to tears. The gods have not abandoned Troy, he says, if they give the Trojans young men with such hearts. He holds their hands and promises that the gods and Aeneas will reward them. Ascanius is even more emotional. He begs Nisus, by the household gods, to bring his father back. He promises many gifts: silver cups, tripods, gold, and an old bowl given by Dido. If the Trojans win Italy, Nisus will receive the horse and golden armour of Turnus, as well as women, prisoners, and the king's own fields. Then he turns to Euryalus, who is close to him in age. He will treat him as a true friend in everything, in peace and in war.

Euryalus says he hopes he will always be brave. He asks for only one thing above all gifts. His old mother, who comes from Priam's family, followed him from Troy and refused to stay in Sicily. He is leaving her now without saying goodbye, because he cannot bear to see her tears. He asks Ascanius to comfort her and care for her if he dies. All the Trojans cry, especially Iulus, who thinks of his own love for his father. Ascanius promises that Euryalus's mother will be like a mother to him, like his own lost mother Creusa in all but name. Whatever happens, she will receive all the rewards promised to her son. He gives Euryalus his own golden sword with an ivory sheath. Mnestheus gives Nisus a lion skin, and Aletes exchanges helmets with him. The leaders walk them to the gate with prayers. Iulus gives them many messages for his father. But Virgil says sadly that the winds scattered all these messages into the clouds.

The two friends cross the ditch and enter the enemy camp in the darkness. Everywhere soldiers are lying asleep and drunk on the grass, among chariots, weapons, and wine. Nisus tells Euryalus to keep watch behind them while he clears a path. He kills Rhamnes, a king and Turnus's favourite prophet. But his prophecies could not save him. Nisus kills several of Rhamnes's servants and others near him. He kills young Serranus, who had played games late into the night. He would have been lucky if he had played until morning. Nisus is like a hungry lion in a sheep pen. Euryalus kills just as many. One man, Rhoetus, is awake and hides behind a large wine jar, but Euryalus stabs him as he stands up.

Euryalus is now carried away by the desire to kill. He moves toward the men of Messapus, where the fires are dying and horses are grazing. Nisus sees this and stops him. Daylight is coming, he says, and they have taken enough revenge. They leave behind many treasures. But Euryalus takes the decorated belt of Rhamnes, a golden gift with a long history, and puts it on. He also puts on Messapus's shining helmet with its fine crest. Then they leave the camp.

At this time, three hundred Latin horsemen are riding toward the camp with a message for Turnus, led by Volscens. In the dim light they see two figures moving to the left. Euryalus's stolen helmet shines in the moonlight and gives them away. Volscens shouts at them to stop and say who they are. They do not answer and run into the forest. The horsemen block all the paths. The forest is thick with bushes and dark trees, and the path is hard to see. Euryalus, slowed by the branches and his heavy spoils, loses his way in fear. Nisus escapes. He has passed the enemy and gone far when he stops and looks back for his friend. Euryalus is not there. Nisus cries out in grief. Where has he left him? How can he find him in this confusing forest? He goes back through the dark woods.

He hears horses and shouts. Then he sees that the whole group of horsemen has caught Euryalus, who is struggling uselessly. What can Nisus do? How can he save him? Should he run into their swords and die a glorious death? He raises his spear and prays to the Moon, the goddess Diana, to guide his weapon. He throws it with all his strength. It hits the shield of Sulmo, breaks, and goes through his heart. Sulmo falls dead. The Rutulians look around in confusion. While they hesitate, Nisus throws a second spear. It goes through the head of Tagus. Volscens is wild with anger, but he cannot see who threw the spears. So he says that Euryalus will pay for both deaths. He draws his sword and goes toward Euryalus.

Nisus cannot hide any longer or bear this pain. He screams: "Me! Me! I am here, I did it! Turn your swords on me, Rutulians! It is all my fault. He did not dare and could not do it. The sky and the stars know it. He only loved his unhappy friend too much." But while he is speaking, Volscens's sword goes deep into Euryalus's chest. Euryalus falls dying. His blood runs over his beautiful body, and his head falls onto his shoulder, like a bright flower cut by a plough, or like a poppy bending its head when it is heavy with rain. Nisus rushes into the middle of the enemy. He looks only for Volscens. They push him back from all sides, but he keeps going, swinging his sword like lightning. At last he drives it into the face of the screaming Volscens and kills him as he himself is dying. Then, wounded all over, he throws himself onto the body of his dead friend and finally finds the peace of death.

Here Virgil speaks directly to them. "Happy pair!" he says. If his poetry has any power, no day will ever erase them from memory, as long as the family of Aeneas lives on the Capitol and the ruler of Rome holds power. This is one of the rare moments when the poet speaks in his own voice. The story is both noble and sad. Their courage and love are real, but their desire for spoils and killing leads to their deaths.

The Rutulians carry the body of Volscens back to their camp with the recovered spoils. There they find Rhamnes and many other leaders dead, and there is great mourning. They recognize the stolen belt and helmet.

At dawn, Turnus wakes his men for battle. In a terrible sight, they put the heads of Nisus and Euryalus on spears and carry them toward the Trojan walls with loud shouts. The Trojans stand on the walls of their camp and see the faces they know so well, dripping with blood.

Rumour quickly brings the news to Euryalus's mother. She is weaving, and the shuttle falls from her hands. She runs out screaming and tearing her hair, straight to the walls and the front lines, without caring about the soldiers or weapons. She cries out to her son. Was he, the last comfort of her old age, able to leave her so cruelly? Did he not let his poor mother say one last word? Now he lies in a strange land, food for the dogs and birds of Italy. She could not wash his body, close his eyes, or cover him with the clothes she was weaving for him day and night. Is this head all that is left of him? Is this what she followed over land and sea? She asks the Rutulians to kill her with their spears, or Jupiter to strike her with lightning, since she cannot otherwise escape this cruel life. Her cries break the Trojans' courage, and they groan in sadness. Ilioneus and the weeping Iulus order two men to carry her home.

Now the trumpets sound and the battle begins. The Volscians come forward under a roof of shields held over their heads. They fill the ditches and try to tear down the wooden walls. Others climb ladders where the defence is thin. The Trojans throw weapons and push them down with long poles. They roll a huge rock down that breaks through the roof of shields. Elsewhere Mezentius throws burning torches, and Messapus tears down the wooden fences and calls for ladders.

Virgil asks Calliope, the Muse of epic poetry, to help him tell what Turnus did that day. There is a tall wooden tower that the Italians try hard to destroy. Turnus throws a burning torch at it, and the wind spreads the fire. The men inside rush to the safe side of the tower, and their weight makes the whole tower fall with a great crash. Many die. Only two escape, Helenor and Lycus. Helenor is a young man, the son of a king and a slave woman. When he sees he is surrounded, he throws himself at the enemy like a wild animal jumping onto the hunters' spears, and dies. Lycus runs to the wall and tries to climb up to his friends' hands. But Turnus catches him, mocks him, and pulls him down together with part of the wall, like an eagle catching a swan or a wolf catching a lamb. Many others die on both sides. Mezentius kills a beautiful young man, the son of Arcens, with a lead bullet from his sling.

Now, for the first time, Ascanius shoots an arrow in war. Before this he had shot only at animals. A man named Numanus Remulus, who has just married Turnus's younger sister, is walking in front of his soldiers and shouting insults at the Trojans. He calls them "twice-captured Phrygians", because Troy was taken twice, and he mocks them for hiding behind walls. He says the Italians are a hard people. They dip their newborn babies in icy rivers. As boys they hunt all night. As men they farm with weapons in their hands and live by raiding. Even their old men wear helmets. But the Trojans, he says, wear colourful clothes and love dancing. They wear long sleeves and ribbons in their hair. They are like women, not men. He tells them to go and play their flutes and drums for the mother goddess, and to leave weapons to men.

Ascanius cannot bear it. He prays to Jupiter and promises him a white bull with golden horns. Jupiter thunders on the left, a good sign. Ascanius shoots, and the arrow goes through Remulus's head from one side to the other. Ascanius says: "Go now and mock courage with proud words! This is the answer the twice-captured Phrygians send to the Rutulians." The Trojans cheer. From the sky, Apollo sees this and praises him. This is the way to the stars, he says, for a son of gods who will be the father of gods. All future wars will end in peace under his family, and Troy is too small for him. Roman readers would hear a reference to Augustus, a descendant of Iulus, and his peace. Then Apollo comes down in the shape of old Butes, who was once Anchises's armour-bearer and now guards Ascanius. He tells Ascanius that he has had his first honour, killing Remulus, and Apollo allows it. But now he must stop fighting. Then the god disappears, and the Trojan leaders recognize him by the sound of his arrows. They keep Ascanius away from the battle and fight on themselves.

Now two huge brothers, Pandarus and Bitias, as tall as pine trees, open the gate that Aeneas ordered them to keep closed. They are confident in their strength. They stand on each side of the gate inside the camp, like two great oak trees, and invite the enemy to come in. The Rutulians rush in. Some are driven back, and others die in the gateway. The fighting grows, and the Trojans even come out of the gate.

Turnus hears that the gate is open and rushes there. He kills a son of the hero Sarpedon and several others. Then he kills Bitias, not with an ordinary spear but with a huge heavy one thrown like a thunderbolt. It goes through his shield and his double armour. Bitias falls like a great stone pier falling into the sea. Mars, the god of war, now fills the Latins with courage and the Trojans with fear. Pandarus sees his brother fall. He pushes the gate closed with his broad shoulders, shutting some Trojans outside. But in his foolishness he does not see that he has shut Turnus inside with them, like a tiger among sheep. Turnus's eyes flash and his armour shines. The Trojans recognize his hated shape. Pandarus, furious about his brother, shouts that this is not the palace Amata promised as Lavinia's dowry. This is an enemy camp, and Turnus cannot escape. Turnus smiles calmly. Let Pandarus fight, he says; he can tell Priam in the underworld that here too an Achilles was found. Pandarus throws his spear, but Juno turns it away and it sticks in the gate. Then Turnus raises his sword and splits Pandarus's head in two. The Trojans run in terror.

Virgil says that if Turnus had thought of opening the gate and letting his army in, that day would have been the last day of the war and of the Trojan people. But anger and the madness of killing drive him on. He kills many more, running after the fleeing Trojans. Juno gives him strength. Among those he kills is Cretheus, a friend of the Muses, who loved songs and the lyre and always sang about horses, warriors, and battles.

Finally the Trojan leaders Mnestheus and Serestus arrive and see their men running and the enemy inside. Mnestheus shouts at them. Where are they running? What other walls or city do they have? Will one man, trapped inside their own camp, kill their best warriors? Do they feel no shame before their country, their gods, and Aeneas? These words give them courage, and they gather together. Slowly Turnus moves back toward the river side of the camp. He is like a lion surrounded by hunters. He is angry and does not want to turn his back, but he cannot break through. Twice he still attacks and drives them back along the walls. But now all the Trojans come against him. Juno cannot give him more strength, because Jupiter has sent Iris to order her to let Turnus leave the camp. Weapons rain down on him. His helmet rings with blows, his crest is torn off, and his shield cannot stop the stones. Sweat pours from his body and he can hardly breathe. At last he jumps, still in full armour, into the river. The yellow water receives him, washes away the blood, and carries him back safely to his friends.

## Book 10: The Death of Pallas

On Mount Olympus, Jupiter calls a council of the gods in his great hall, from where he can look down on the whole earth, the Trojan camp, and the Latin peoples. He asks why the gods are fighting each other. He had forbidden Italy to go to war with the Trojans. Why have they disobeyed? The right time for war will come later, he says, when Carthage crosses the Alps and attacks Rome. This is a reference to Hannibal, who brought an army over the Alps two hundred years before Virgil's time. Then the gods may hate each other. For now they should make peace.

Venus answers with a long, emotional speech. She points out that Turnus is proud and rides in his chariot through the battle. The Trojans are no longer safe even behind their walls, and blood fills their ditches. Aeneas is away and does not know. Will the siege never end? A new enemy threatens this young Troy, and Diomedes may join the war. She lists all of Juno's attacks: the burned ships in Sicily, the storm from Aeolus, Iris sent down from the clouds, and now even Allecto from the underworld. Venus says she no longer cares about an empire. Let whoever Jupiter wants win. But she begs him to save Ascanius. Let Aeneas be thrown about on unknown seas, but let her hide the boy in one of her holy places, in Cyprus or Cythera, where he can live quietly without glory. Let Carthage rule Italy. What was the point of escaping from Troy and suffering so much? It would have been better to stay among the ashes of their home. She asks Jupiter to give the Trojans back their old rivers and let them suffer the fate of Troy again.

Juno answers angrily. Did anyone force Aeneas to start a war or attack Latinus? He came to Italy because of prophecies; maybe so, she says, but those were the ravings of Cassandra. Did she tell him to leave his camp, to give command of his walls to a child, or to stir up the peaceful Etruscans? Is it terrible that the Italians attack this new Troy, and that Turnus fights on his own land? What about the Trojans burning Latin land, stealing fields, and taking brides promised to others? Venus has saved Aeneas from the Greeks with a cloud, and she turned his ships into nymphs. So why is it wrong if Juno helps the Rutulians? Juno reminds Venus that it was her favourite, Paris, who caused the Trojan War by stealing Helen from Sparta. Venus should have worried about her people then. Now her complaints are too late.

The gods murmur, some on one side and some on the other, like the wind in a forest before a storm. Then Jupiter speaks, and everything becomes silent, the sky, the earth, and the sea. Since Trojans and Italians cannot make peace, and the goddesses cannot stop quarrelling, he will treat both sides equally today. Whether the siege is the result of Italy's fate or of Trojan mistakes, he will not help either side. Each man's own actions will bring him good or bad fortune. Jupiter is king of all, and fate will find its way. He swears this by the river Styx, and all of Olympus shakes. The council ends.

On earth, the Rutulians attack all the gates of the Trojan camp and surround its walls with fire. The Trojans are trapped inside without hope. A thin line of defenders stands on the towers, among them Asius, Thymoetes, Castor, old Thymbris, and others. Acmon carries a huge stone. In the middle of them stands Ascanius without a helmet, shining like a jewel set in gold. His hair falls on his white neck, held by a golden band. Mnestheus is there too, famous because he drove Turnus from the walls the day before.

Meanwhile Aeneas is sailing back in the middle of the night. He has met Tarchon, the Etruscan leader, and told him who he is, what he wants, and what help he can offer. He has explained the danger of Mezentius and Turnus. Tarchon has made an alliance with him at once. Free from the prophecy, because now they have a foreign leader, the Etruscans have put their army on ships under Aeneas's command. Aeneas's ship leads the fleet. On its front are carved Phrygian lions and Mount Ida, a welcome sight for the Trojan exiles. Aeneas sits thinking about the war. Young Pallas sits beside him and asks about the stars and about his adventures on land and sea.

Virgil now calls on the Muses again and lists the Etruscan leaders who sail with Aeneas in thirty ships. Massicus leads a thousand archers from Clusium and Cosae in the ship Tiger. Grim Abas leads men from Populonia and from Elba, the island of iron mines. Asilas, a prophet who can read the stars, animals, birds, and lightning, leads a thousand spearmen from Pisa. The handsome Astyr leads three hundred men from Caere and nearby towns. There is also Cinyras of the Ligurians and Cupavo, who wears swan feathers on his helmet. His father Cycnus was turned into a swan while singing sadly for his dead friend Phaethon. Ocnus leads men from Mantua, a city named after his mother. Mantua was Virgil's own home town, so he gives it a proud place in the list. Aulestes rows the great ship Triton, whose carved figure is half man and half sea monster.

In the night, while Aeneas himself steers the ship, a group of sea nymphs swims to meet him. These are his own ships, which Cybele turned into goddesses in Book 9. The best speaker among them, Cymodocea, holds onto the ship and speaks. She tells him to wake up and raise his sails. They are his fleet, made from the holy pines of Mount Ida. When Turnus attacked them with fire, they broke their ropes, and Cybele made them goddesses of the sea. She tells him that Ascanius is trapped inside the camp. The Arcadian and Etruscan horsemen are already at the meeting place, and Turnus plans to stop them from reaching the camp. Aeneas must call his men to arms before dawn and take up the shield that Vulcan made. Tomorrow, she promises, will see many Rutulians dead. Then she pushes the ship forward with her hand, and it flies faster than an arrow. Aeneas is amazed but encouraged. He prays to Cybele to lead him in battle.

At dawn Aeneas orders his men to prepare for battle. As soon as he can see the camp, he stands up on the stern and lifts his blazing shield. The Trojans on the walls see it and shout to the sky. Hope gives them new strength, and they throw their weapons harder. The Rutulian leaders are surprised by this, until they turn and see the ships coming toward the shore, the whole sea covered by one great fleet. Aeneas's helmet blazes, flames seem to pour from its crest, and his golden shield shines like a comet or the dangerous Dog Star that brings fever and drought.

But Turnus is still confident. He plans to stop them at the shore before they can land. He tells his men that they now have what they prayed for, a chance to fight hand to hand. Each man should remember his wife and home and the glory of their fathers. They must attack while the enemy is still slipping on the wet sand. Fortune helps those who are brave.

Aeneas lands his men by gangways. Some jump into the shallow water, and some slide down the oars. Tarchon finds a place where the sea is calm and orders his rowers to drive their ships straight onto the land, even if the ships break. All the ships reach the land safely except Tarchon's own. It hits a sandbank, rocks back and forth, and breaks apart, throwing its crew into the water.

Turnus brings his whole army to the beach. The trumpets sound. Aeneas attacks first and kills a giant named Theron, a good sign for the battle. He kills many more: Lichas, who was born by being cut from his dead mother's body; strong Cisseus and huge Gyas, who were companions of Hercules; and Pharus, hit in the mouth while he is shouting. The seven sons of Phorcus throw their spears at Aeneas at once. Some bounce off his shield and helmet, and Venus turns others aside. Aeneas asks Achates for more spears, the same spears that killed Greeks at Troy. He kills Maeon, and the same spear goes through the arm of his brother Alcanor. Their brother Numitor throws a spear that wounds Achates in the thigh. Clausus, Halaesus, and Messapus fight hard on the Italian side. The two armies are equal, like opposing winds fighting in the sky, with neither side giving way.

In another part of the field, Pallas sees his Arcadians running back. They are horsemen, but the rough ground has forced them to fight on foot, which they are not used to. Pallas tries to give them courage. He asks them, by their leader Evander and by his own hopes of matching his father's glory, not to run. They must cut a path through the enemy with their swords. Their enemies are not gods, but men with the same number of lives and hands. The sea is behind them, so where can they run? To the sea, or to Troy?

Then Pallas charges into the enemy. He kills Lagus while he is lifting a stone, and then Hisbo, who attacks him in anger. He kills Sthenius, then Anchemolus, a man who had committed a crime with his stepmother. He kills the twin brothers Larides and Thymber, who looked so alike that even their parents confused them. He cuts off Thymber's head and Larides's hand, which still tries to hold the sword. The Arcadians, ashamed and inspired by his example, turn and fight. Pallas kills Rhoeteus as he flies past in his chariot, and his comrades gather around him like a fire spreading across a field. Then the warrior Halaesus attacks and kills several Arcadians. His father, a prophet, had hidden him in the woods to save him, but the Fates had marked him for death by Evander's family. Pallas prays to the river Tiber and promises it Halaesus's armour. He throws his spear and kills Halaesus.

Lausus, the son of Mezentius, does not let his men lose courage. He kills Abas and many Arcadians, Etruscans, and Trojans. The armies press together. Pallas fights on one side and Lausus on the other. They are almost the same age and both are beautiful. Virgil says that fate has denied both of them a return home. But Jupiter does not let them fight each other, because both will soon die at the hands of a greater enemy.

Juturna, the sister of Turnus, tells him to go to Lausus's aid. Turnus rides through the lines in his chariot. He tells his men to stop fighting, because he alone will fight Pallas. Pallas is his. He wishes Pallas's father were there to watch. The Rutulians step back. Pallas is amazed at this proud order and looks at Turnus's huge body. He answers that he will win glory either by taking rich spoils or by a glorious death, and that his father can bear either. He tells Turnus to stop making threats. He steps into the open space, and the Arcadians feel their blood go cold. Turnus jumps down from his chariot and comes forward like a lion attacking a bull.

When Turnus is close enough, Pallas prays to Hercules. He reminds the hero of the welcome Evander gave him at his table. Let Hercules help him, Pallas prays, so that the dying Turnus sees Pallas taking his bloody armour. In heaven, Hercules hears him. He groans deeply and cries useless tears, because he knows what will happen. Jupiter comforts him kindly. Every man has his fixed day, he says. Life is short and cannot be repeated for anyone, but a brave man can make his name live through his deeds. Many sons of gods died at Troy, even Jupiter's own son Sarpedon. Turnus too will soon meet his fate. Then Jupiter turns his eyes away from the battlefield.

Pallas throws his spear with all his strength. It goes through the edge of Turnus's shield and scratches his shoulder, drawing a little blood. Then Turnus aims his heavy spear for a long time. He says: "See if my weapon has a sharper point." The spear goes through Pallas's shield, through the iron and bronze and leather, through his breastplate, and into his chest. Pallas pulls it out, but his blood and life come out with it. He falls on his face on enemy ground, and his armour clashes over him.

Turnus stands over him. He tells the Arcadians to take a message to Evander. He is sending Pallas back as Evander deserves. He freely gives him the honour of a tomb and a funeral. Evander will pay dearly for welcoming Aeneas. Then Turnus puts his left foot on the dead body and tears off Pallas's heavy sword belt. The belt is decorated in gold with a terrible story: fifty young bridegrooms murdered by their brides on their wedding night. Turnus is proud and happy with his prize. Here Virgil comments directly. How little the human mind knows of fate, he says, and how hard it is to stay modest in success! A day will come when Turnus will wish he had paid any price to keep Pallas alive, and he will hate the sight of these spoils. This warning prepares the reader for the very last scene of the poem. Pallas's friends lay him on his shield and carry him away, crying. Virgil speaks to the dead boy: what grief and what glory he will bring back to his father! This one day sent him to war for the first time, and this same day takes him away, though he leaves many enemies dead.

Not a rumour but a sure messenger reaches Aeneas. His men are close to defeat, and it is time to help them. Aeneas cuts a wide path through the enemy with his sword, looking for Turnus, who is proud of his new killing. Pallas, Evander, the table where he was welcomed as a stranger, and the hands they shook all come before his eyes. Now Aeneas is full of terrible anger. He captures eight young men alive, four sons of Sulmo and four raised by Ufens. He plans to sacrifice them to Pallas's spirit and pour their blood on his funeral fire.

A man named Magus kneels before Aeneas and begs for his life, in the name of Anchises and Iulus. He has silver and gold hidden in his house and will pay a ransom. One life will not change the war, he says. Aeneas answers that he can keep his treasure for his children. Turnus ended all such bargaining when he killed Pallas. That is the judgment of Anchises's spirit and of Iulus. Then he pulls back Magus's head and drives his sword into him. He kills a priest of Apollo in white robes. He kills Anxur and Tarquitus, the son of a god of the woods. Tarquitus also begs for mercy, but Aeneas cuts off his head and says that no mother will bury him; he will be left for the birds or the fish. Aeneas kills many others. He rages over the field like the hundred-armed giant who once fought Jupiter.

Then two brothers, Lucagus and Liger, drive at him in their chariot. Liger shouts that this is not Troy, and Aeneas will die here. Aeneas does not answer with words. He throws his spear and kills Lucagus, who falls from the chariot. Aeneas mocks him bitterly. Liger jumps down and begs for his life in the name of Aeneas's parents. Aeneas answers that those were not his words a moment ago. He tells him to die and not be separated from his brother, and he kills him. These scenes show a darker side of Aeneas, driven by grief and rage, and readers often debate how to judge them. At last Ascanius and the Trojans break out of the camp, and the siege is over.

In heaven, Jupiter teases Juno. She was right, he says: it is Venus who keeps the Trojans going, not their own courage. Juno answers sadly. If she still had his love as before, he would let her save Turnus for his father Daunus. Turnus also comes from the gods, and he has always given Jupiter rich gifts. Jupiter says that if she wants only to delay Turnus's death, she may take him away from the battle for now. But if she hopes to change the result of the whole war, her hope is empty. Juno cries and says she fears that Turnus will die innocent.

Juno flies down to the battle wrapped in a storm cloud. She makes a ghost of Aeneas out of mist, with his weapons, his shield, his helmet, his voice, and his walk. It looks like the ghosts of the dead or like a dream. The phantom dances in front of the lines and mocks the enemy. Turnus throws a spear at it, and the phantom turns and runs. Turnus is full of false joy. He shouts after it: "Where are you running, Aeneas? Don't leave your promised bride! This hand will give you the land you came so far to find!" He follows it. Nearby, a ship is tied to a rock with its gangway down. The phantom runs onto this ship and hides, and Turnus runs after it. As soon as he is on board, Juno breaks the rope, and the ship drifts out to sea. The phantom flies up and disappears into a dark cloud. Meanwhile, the real Aeneas searches for Turnus everywhere and kills many men.

Out at sea, Turnus looks back. He does not understand what has happened, and he is not grateful to be saved. He lifts his hands to heaven. What crime has he committed to deserve this punishment? Where is he going? Will he ever see the Latin camp again? What about the men who followed him to war, whom he has left to die? He feels deep shame. He begs the winds to wreck his ship on the rocks, or carry him to some far place where no Rutulian knows his name. Three times he thinks of killing himself with his sword. Three times he thinks of jumping into the sea to swim back to the battle. Three times Juno stops him, out of pity. The ship carries him down the coast to Ardea, the old city of his father Daunus.

Meanwhile, by Jupiter's will, Mezentius enters the battle. The Etruscans, who hate him, all attack him at once. But he stands like a rock in the sea against wind and waves. He kills Hebrus, Latagus, and Palmus. He gives Palmus's armour to his son Lausus. He kills Mimas, a Trojan born on the same night as Paris. He is like a wild boar that the hunting dogs are afraid to approach. The Etruscans throw spears from a distance, but no one dares to fight him close up. He kills Acron, a Greek who left his bride behind before the wedding. He kills Orodes face to face, not from behind. The dying Orodes warns that Mezentius will soon die too. Mezentius smiles grimly. Let Jupiter decide about him, he says. Many others die on both sides, and the fighting is equal. The gods pity the useless anger of both sides and the suffering of men. Venus watches from one side, Juno from the other, and the Fury Tisiphone rages among the soldiers.

Then Mezentius walks across the plain with his huge spear, tall as the giant hunter Orion. Aeneas sees him and comes to fight. Mezentius waits without fear. He prays only to his own right hand and his spear, because he does not respect the gods. He promises to dress Lausus in the armour of Aeneas. He throws his spear. It bounces off Aeneas's shield and kills Antores nearby, a friend of Hercules who came from Argos and lived with Evander. As he dies, he looks at the sky and remembers his sweet home in Argos. Then Aeneas throws his spear. It goes through Mezentius's shield and wounds him in the groin, though not deeply. Aeneas, glad to see his blood, draws his sword and rushes at him.

Lausus sees his father in danger and groans with love for him, tears running down his face. Here Virgil promises not to be silent about Lausus's hard death and noble deed. Mezentius moves back, wounded and slowed by the spear still hanging from his shield. Lausus jumps forward between them just as Aeneas is about to strike. He catches the blow and holds Aeneas back. His friends shout and throw spears so that Mezentius can escape behind his son's shield. Aeneas, angry, protects himself under his shield, like a traveller waiting for a hailstorm to pass. He warns Lausus: "Why do you run to your death? You are trying something beyond your strength. Your love for your father makes you careless." But Lausus keeps attacking. Aeneas's anger rises, and he drives his sword through Lausus's body, through his light shield and the tunic his mother wove for him with gold thread. His life leaves his body and goes sadly to the underworld.

Then Aeneas sees the face of the dying boy, strangely pale, and he groans with pity. He reaches out his hand, because he thinks of his own love for his father. He speaks to the dead boy. What can good Aeneas give him, worthy of such a noble heart? Lausus may keep the armour he loved. Aeneas will give his body back to his family, if that means anything to the dead. And this will be some comfort: he fell by the hand of great Aeneas. Then he scolds Lausus's hesitating friends and lifts the body himself, its hair wet with blood. Lausus is like a mirror of Aeneas, a son who risks everything for his father. That is why Aeneas feels pity for him, even after his rage over Pallas.

Meanwhile Mezentius rests by the river Tiber, washing his wound and leaning against a tree. His helmet hangs on a branch, and his heavy armour lies on the grass. He keeps asking for Lausus and sends many messengers to call him back. But Lausus's friends are already carrying his body on his shield, crying. From far away, Mezentius hears them and knows. He pours dust on his grey hair, lifts his hands to heaven, and holds his son's body. He cries out that he loved life too much, if he let his son take the blow meant for him. He lives because his son died. Now at last his exile is truly bitter, and his wound truly deep. He admits that his own crimes stained his son's name, and that he deserved to be driven from his throne by his people's hatred. He should have paid for this with his own life in every possible way of dying. He is still alive, he says, but not for long.

Although he is badly wounded, he gets up and calls for his horse, Rhoebus, his pride and comfort, which always carried him home victorious. He speaks to the horse. They have lived long, if anything lasts long for mortals. Today the horse will either carry back the head and armour of Aeneas, so they can avenge Lausus, or die with him. He does not think the horse would ever accept a Trojan master. He mounts, holding spears in both hands, his bronze helmet shining. Shame, madness, and grief fill his heart. He calls Aeneas three times by name.

Aeneas hears and is glad. He prays to Jupiter and Apollo and comes to meet him. Mezentius tells him that his threats are useless now that his son is gone. That was the only way Aeneas could destroy him. He does not fear death and does not care for any god. He has come to die, but first he brings Aeneas some gifts. He throws spear after spear, riding around Aeneas in a wide circle three times. But they all stick in Aeneas's golden shield. Aeneas grows tired of waiting and of pulling spears from his shield. At last he throws his spear at the horse's head. The horse rears up, throws its rider, and falls on top of him.

The Trojans and Latins shout. Aeneas runs up with his sword and asks where is the fierce Mezentius now? Mezentius looks up at the sky. He tells Aeneas there is no need for insults. There is no shame in killing him, and he did not come to battle expecting mercy, nor did Lausus make any deal for his life. He asks only one thing, which even a defeated enemy may ask: let his body be buried. He knows his people hate him, so he asks Aeneas to protect his body from their anger and to let him lie in the same grave as his son. Then he calmly receives the sword in his throat, and his blood pours over his armour.

## Book 11: The Truce, the Council, and Camilla

At dawn, Aeneas wants to bury his dead friends, and his heart is troubled by death. But first he keeps his promise to the gods for the victory. On a small hill he sets up a large oak tree with its branches cut off. He dresses it in the shining armour of Mezentius, as a trophy for Mars, the god of war. He hangs on it the bloody helmet crest, the broken spears, and the breastplate pierced in twelve places. He ties the bronze shield to its left side and hangs the ivory sword from its neck. Then he speaks to his captains. The greatest task is done, he says. This is what is left of a proud king, killed by his hand. Next they will march on the city of King Latinus. But first they must bury their comrades, because burial is the last honour the dead can receive. Above all, the body of Pallas must be sent back to Evander's grieving city.

Aeneas goes, crying, to the place where Pallas's body lies. Old Acoetes, once Evander's armour-bearer and later the guardian of Pallas, is watching over it. Trojan women stand around with their hair loose in mourning. When Aeneas comes in, they beat their breasts and cry loudly. Aeneas sees Pallas's handsome face and the deep wound in his chest, and he weeps. He speaks to the dead boy. Fortune came with victory, he says, but it did not let Pallas see their kingdom or return in triumph to his father. This was not what Aeneas promised Evander. Evander warned him that the enemy was brave and hard in battle. Now, perhaps, Evander is still praying and making offerings to the gods for his son's safe return. Instead, they will bring him a dead body. Evander will see his son cruelly killed. Is this the triumphant return he was waiting for? What a protector Italy and Iulus have lost!

Aeneas orders a thousand chosen men to carry the body back to Evander. They make a soft frame of branches and lay Pallas on it, like a flower cut by a girl's hand. It still has its colour and beauty, but the earth no longer feeds it. Aeneas brings out two purple robes with gold thread. Dido made them for him long ago with her own hands, happy in her work. He wraps Pallas in one of them as a last honour and covers his hair with it. He sends many spoils of the battle, horses and weapons taken from the enemy. He also sends the prisoners with their hands tied behind them, who will be killed so their blood can be poured on the funeral fire. His captains carry tree trunks dressed in enemy armour with the names of the dead enemies. Old Acoetes follows, beating his chest and tearing his face, and throwing himself on the ground. Pallas's chariot is led along, red with Rutulian blood. Behind it walks his war horse, Aethon, without his decorations, with big tears running down his face. Others carry Pallas's spear and helmet; Turnus has the rest. The Trojans, Etruscans, and Arcadians follow with their weapons turned down in mourning. Aeneas stops and says goodbye: war calls him to other tears. He greets Pallas forever, and says farewell forever. Then he goes back to his camp.

Messengers come from the Latin city carrying olive branches. They ask Aeneas to give back their dead lying on the field, so they can bury them. No one makes war on the dead, they say, and they were once his hosts. Aeneas grants this kindly. He tells them that he wishes he could give peace to the living too. He came here only because fate gave him this land. He is not fighting the Latin people. It was their king who broke their agreement and trusted Turnus. It would be fairer if Turnus himself had faced death today. If Turnus wants to drive out the Trojans, he should fight Aeneas man to man, and the one favoured by the gods or by his own hand would live. Now they may go and burn their dead.

The messengers look at each other in silence. Then old Drances speaks. He has always hated Turnus. He praises Aeneas as great in fame but even greater in war, and admires his justice. He promises to bring these words back to the city and make peace between Aeneas and Latinus, if he can. Let Turnus find his own allies. The Latins will even be glad to help build the walls of the new Troy. Everyone agrees. A truce of twelve days is made, a pause in the fighting. During the truce, Trojans and Latins walk together safely in the forests and cut trees for the funeral pyres.

Rumour flies to Evander's city. The same Rumour that just told of Pallas's victories now tells of his death. The Arcadians run to the gates with funeral torches, making a long line of light across the fields. They meet the Trojan procession. The women fill the city with crying. Nothing can hold back Evander. When the frame is put down, he throws himself on his son's body and holds him, crying, until he can barely speak. Pallas promised to be careful, he says, but Evander knew how strong the young love of glory is in a first battle. His prayers to the gods were useless. He says his wife was lucky to die before seeing this. He has lived too long, outliving his own son. He wishes he had gone to war himself and died instead.

But he does not blame the Trojans, or the alliance, or the friendship he made with Aeneas. If his son had to die young, it is good that he died leading the Trojans into Latium after killing many enemies. He could not wish for a better funeral than the one Aeneas, the Trojans, and the Etruscans have given him. If Pallas had been as old and strong as Turnus, Turnus would now be a trophy of armour on a tree. Then Evander sends a message to Aeneas. The only reason he still lives, though life is now hateful, is his hope for Aeneas's right hand. That hand owes Turnus's life to both son and father. That is the only task left for Aeneas's courage and fortune. Evander does not want joy in life anymore. He only wants to carry this news to his son in the underworld.

The next morning, Aeneas and Tarchon build funeral pyres on the shore. Each group burns its own dead in the old way. The smoke hides the sky. Three times the soldiers march around the burning pyres in shining armour, and three times they ride around them on horseback, crying out in grief. Tears fall on the ground and on their armour, and the trumpets sound. Some throw Latin spoils into the fire, such as helmets, swords, bridles, and chariot wheels. Others throw in the shields and weapons of the dead men themselves. Many animals are sacrificed. They watch their friends burn and stay by the fires until night.

The Latins also build countless pyres. They bury some of their dead, carry others home, and burn the rest in great unnamed heaps. On the third day they cover the bones and ashes with earth. In the city of Latinus the crying is loudest. Mothers, young widows, sisters, and orphaned boys curse the war and Turnus's marriage plans. They say Turnus should fight Aeneas himself, since he claims the first place and wants to rule Italy. Drances makes this anger worse, saying that Aeneas has challenged Turnus alone. But others support Turnus. Queen Amata protects him, and his past victories give him fame.

In the middle of this confusion comes more bad news. The messengers sent to Diomedes, the Greek hero, return with nothing. Their gifts, gold, and pleading were useless. The Latins must find other allies or ask the Trojans for peace. King Latinus is overcome with grief. The anger of the gods and the fresh graves show him that fate is clearly on Aeneas's side. He calls a great council in his palace. He sits sadly in the middle and asks the messengers to report.

Venulus, the chief messenger, tells what happened. They reached Diomedes, who was building a new city in southern Italy, and they offered him gifts. Diomedes answered calmly. He called the Italians a lucky people from the old kingdom of Saturn. Why do they want to start a war they do not understand? All the Greeks who fought at Troy have been punished around the world, he said. Many died in storms on the way home. Menelaus wandered as far as Egypt. Ulysses saw the Cyclops. Agamemnon, the great commander, was murdered by his own wife as soon as he got home. The gods did not even let Diomedes return to his own country and wife. His companions were turned into birds that cry on the cliffs. He expected this punishment from the day he was mad enough to wound the goddess Venus with his spear at Troy. So he will not fight the Trojans again. He has no quarrel with Troy now that it is destroyed, and he does not like to remember the old war. He told them to give their gifts to Aeneas instead. He has fought Aeneas himself and knows how he rises behind his shield and throws his spear like a whirlwind. If Troy had had two more men like him, the Trojans would have attacked Greece, and Greece would be mourning. Hector and Aeneas held back the Greek victory for ten years. Both were brave and great warriors, but Aeneas was greater in "pietas", in his duty to the gods and his people. Diomedes told them to make peace any way they could, but never to fight against Aeneas.

The council murmurs like a river blocked by rocks. Then Latinus speaks. He wishes they had decided this earlier, not with the enemy at their walls. They are fighting a war against a people descended from the gods, who cannot be beaten and never give up. There is no hope of help from Diomedes. They can see with their own eyes the ruin of their fortunes. He does not blame anyone; they fought with all their strength. So he proposes a plan. He owns a large area of land by the Tiber river, toward the west. He will give this land to the Trojans in friendship, invite them as allies, and let them build a city. If they prefer to go to another country, the Latins will build them twenty ships, or more, and give them all the materials and help they need. He will also send a hundred noble messengers with olive branches and gifts of gold, ivory, and royal robes to make the treaty.

Then Drances rises. He is rich and a clever speaker, but a coward in battle. He is jealous of Turnus's fame. He says that everyone knows the truth, but they are afraid to say it because of one man. He means Turnus, whose bad leadership has caused so many deaths and so much mourning, while he attacks the Trojan camp and then runs away. Drances tells the king to add one more gift to the Trojans: his daughter Lavinia, as a wife for Aeneas, to make the peace last forever. Then he turns to Turnus and begs him, with false humility, to give up his claim and let peace come. If Turnus wants glory and a royal bride, let him go and fight Aeneas alone. Why should the common soldiers die and lie unburied so that Turnus can have his princess? If Turnus has any courage, he should face the man who challenges him.

Turnus explodes with anger. Drances always has many words, he says, when the council meets safely behind the walls, but never any deeds in battle. Let Drances call him a coward, when Drances has never killed a single Trojan. The enemy is right outside, so why does Drances not go and fight? Will his courage always stay in his windy tongue and his running feet? Turnus says that he has not been defeated. He has filled the Tiber with Trojan blood, destroyed Evander's family, and killed Pandarus, Bitias, and a thousand others in one day, alone inside the enemy camp. Then he speaks to the king. If they truly have no hope, he says, then let them ask for peace. But they still have an army, many cities and peoples of Italy, and allies like Messapus and Tolumnius. Camilla is with them too, with her horsemen. The Trojans have also lost many men. Fortune often changes. And if the Trojans want Turnus alone, and if he alone stands in the way of the common good, he is ready. He will meet Aeneas bravely, even if he is greater than Achilles and wears armour made by Vulcan. He gives his life to his people and to Latinus. Let Aeneas call him to fight. He would much rather die for this than let Drances win glory or pay with his life.

While they argue, a messenger rushes in. The Trojans and Etruscans are marching down the Tiber valley in battle order. The city is thrown into confusion. Young men shout for weapons, and old men cry. Turnus seizes the moment. He mocks the council for sitting and praising peace while the enemy attacks. He runs out and gives orders. Volusus must arm the Volscians and Rutulians. Messapus and the brothers Coras and Catillus must spread the cavalry over the plain. Others must guard the gates and towers, and the rest will follow him. Latinus leaves the council in despair. He blames himself for not welcoming Aeneas earlier as his son-in-law. People bring stones and poles to the walls, and boys and women stand on them. Queen Amata goes with the mothers of the city to the temple of Minerva, bringing gifts. Beside her walks Lavinia, the cause of so much trouble, with her eyes looking down. The women pray to Minerva to break the spear of the Trojan robber and throw him down before their gates.

Turnus arms himself eagerly in golden armour and runs down from the citadel like a horse that has broken free and gallops joyfully across the fields. At the gate he meets Camilla and her Volscian horsemen. She jumps down from her horse, and her riders do the same. She offers to fight the Trojan and Etruscan cavalry herself, while Turnus stays on foot to guard the walls. Turnus looks at her with admiration. He says she is the pride of Italy and he cannot thank her enough. He tells her his plan. His scouts say that Aeneas has sent his light cavalry ahead across the plain, while he himself is coming over the mountain by a lonely path. Turnus will set an ambush in a narrow valley on that path. Camilla will lead the cavalry battle on the plain with Messapus and the others. Then Turnus goes to the valley, a curved, narrow pass with thick woods on both sides and high ground above, perfect for a trap. He hides his men there.

In heaven, Diana, the goddess of hunting, speaks sadly to Opis, one of her nymphs. Camilla is going into a cruel war, and Diana loves her more than anyone. Then Diana tells Camilla's story. Her father Metabus was a king who was driven from his city of Privernum because of his harsh rule. He fled with his baby daughter and named her Camilla, after her mother Casmilla. Enemies chased him through the forests. He came to a river swollen with rain, too dangerous to swim with a baby. So he made a sudden decision. He wrapped the baby in tree bark and tied her to the middle of his heavy spear. He prayed to Diana and gave the child to her as a servant. Then he threw the spear across the river. Camilla flew over the roaring water. Metabus swam across and found her safe on the grass. After that he lived as a wild shepherd in the mountains, because no city would accept him. He fed the baby with milk from a wild horse. When she could walk, he gave her a small spear, a bow, and arrows. Instead of golden jewels and long dresses, she wore a tiger skin. As a little girl she killed cranes and swans with her sling. Many mothers wanted her to marry their sons, but she loved only Diana, hunting, and her freedom. Diana wishes Camilla had not gone to this war. But since her bitter fate is coming, Diana gives Opis an arrow. Whoever wounds Camilla, Trojan or Italian, must die by it. Afterwards Diana will carry Camilla's body and armour in a cloud to be buried in her own land.

Now the Trojan and Etruscan cavalry come near the walls. The Latin cavalry, with Messapus, Coras, and Camilla, rides out against them. When they are close, they charge with a shout, and spears fall like snow. Two riders, Tyrrhenus and Aconteus, crash into each other and fall first. Aconteus is thrown off like a thunderbolt and dies. The Latins turn and ride back toward their city. The Trojans, led by Asilas, chase them. Near the gates, the Latins turn around and drive the Trojans back. The battle moves back and forth like the sea coming in and going out. Twice the Etruscans chase the Rutulians to the walls, and twice they are driven back. The third time, the two lines crash together and every man fights his own enemy. Men and horses fall together in blood.

In the middle of the fighting rages Camilla, like an Amazon, one of the warrior women of old stories. One shoulder is bare. She throws spears, swings a heavy axe, and carries Diana's golden bow. Even when she pulls back, she turns and shoots arrows. Around her ride her chosen companions, Italian girls named Larina, Tulla, and Tarpeia. Virgil asks Camilla whom she killed first and whom last. She kills Euneus, then Liris and Pagasus, then Amastrus and many others. For every spear she throws, a Trojan falls. She kills Ornytus, a giant hunter wearing a bull's hide and a wolf's head as a helmet. She mocks him: did he think he was hunting animals in the forest? Now a woman's weapons have answered his words. But it will be some glory for him to tell the dead that he fell by Camilla's hand. She kills Butes and Orsilochus, two of the biggest Trojans.

Then she meets the son of Aunus, a Ligurian. Ligurians had a reputation for trickery. He sees he cannot escape her, so he tries a trick. What glory is there, he says, in a woman trusting her horse's speed? Let her get down and fight him on foot. She is angry, gives her horse to a companion, and stands ready on foot. But as soon as she does, he turns his horse and rides away as fast as he can. She runs after him faster than fire, catches his bridle, and kills him, like a falcon catching a dove.

Jupiter watches all of this. He fills the Etruscan leader Tarchon with anger. Tarchon rides among his retreating men and shames them. A woman is chasing them! What use are their weapons? They are not slow when it comes to love, feasts, and wine. Then he charges into the enemy, grabs Venulus off his horse, and carries him away on his own saddle, struggling, like an eagle flying off with a snake in its claws. The Etruscans follow his example and attack.

Now Arruns, an Etruscan, marked for death, follows Camilla closely with his spear. Wherever she goes, he goes, waiting for his chance. At that moment, Camilla sees Chloreus, a former priest of Cybele, dressed in splendid Phrygian armour, purple and gold, on a horse covered with golden scales. She wants his golden armour, either to hang in a temple or to wear herself. She chases him alone across the battlefield, without thinking of anything else. Virgil says she is driven by a woman's love of spoils. Arruns sees his chance. He prays to Apollo to let him kill this terrible enemy. He does not want her armour or any glory, he says. He will gladly go home without fame. Apollo grants half of his prayer: he may kill Camilla. But he does not grant the other half, his safe return home. The wind carries that part of the prayer away.

The spear flies. All the Volscians see it and turn to look at their queen. Only Camilla does not hear it or see it coming. It strikes her below her breast and goes deep. Her companions run to catch her. Arruns runs away, frightened and happy at once, like a wolf that has killed a bull and runs into the mountains with its tail between its legs. Camilla tries to pull out the spear, but the point is stuck between her ribs. She loses blood, and the colour leaves her face. She speaks to Acca, her most loyal friend. Her strength has carried her this far, she says, but now the wound is too strong, and everything is going dark. She asks Acca to tell Turnus to take her place in the battle and keep the Trojans from the city. Then she says goodbye. She slides from her horse. Her neck goes weak, her head falls, and with a groan her life flies angrily into the dark.

A huge cry rises to the stars. With Camilla dead, the battle becomes even bloodier. The Trojans, Etruscans, and Arcadians charge together. On a hill, Opis has been watching. When she sees Camilla dead, she groans. Camilla has paid too high a price for attacking the Trojans, she says. Her love for Diana and her life in the woods did not help her. But Diana will not leave her without honour or revenge. Opis flies to the tomb of an old king, where she sees Arruns shining in his armour and boasting. She calls to him to come and receive his reward for Camilla. She draws her bow until the two ends meet and shoots. Arruns hears the arrow whistle just as it enters his body. His friends forget him, and he dies alone in the dust of an unknown field. Opis flies back to heaven.

Now the Latin army breaks. Camilla's horsemen flee, the Rutulians flee, and their leaders flee. They ride back toward the city, and the dust rises in clouds to the walls. Mothers on the towers beat their breasts and cry. The Trojans chase the first runners through the open gates and kill them inside their own city, near their own homes. Some Latins shut the gates and will not let their own friends in. Then terrible fighting begins between those inside and those outside. Shut out in front of their crying parents, some fall into the ditches, and some ride wildly against the locked gates. Even the women on the walls, inspired by Camilla's example and their love of their country, throw spears and use burned stakes and poles as weapons. They are ready to die first for their city.

In the forest, Turnus hears the terrible news from Acca. The Volscians are destroyed, Camilla is dead, and the enemy is winning everywhere. Angry, and following the will of Jupiter, he leaves his ambush in the hills and the rough woods. He has just left the valley and reached the plain when Aeneas comes through the empty pass, crosses the ridge, and comes out of the dark forest. If Turnus had stayed only a little longer, his trap might have worked. Both armies hurry toward the city, not far apart. Aeneas sees the plain covered with dust and the Latin soldiers. Turnus sees Aeneas in his terrible armour and hears his army coming. They would fight at once, but the sun is setting and night is coming. So both armies make camp in front of the city and dig ditches around their positions.

## Book 12: The Final Duel

Turnus sees that the Latins are broken and losing heart. Everyone is now looking at him and expecting him to keep his promise to fight Aeneas alone. His anger burns like that of a wounded lion in Africa, which finally stands up, shakes its mane, breaks the hunter's spear, and roars with bloody jaws. He speaks to King Latinus. He will not delay. The cowardly Trojans have no excuse to take back their words. He will fight. Let the king bring the holy objects and swear the treaty. Either Turnus will send Aeneas, the runaway from Asia, down to the underworld, while the Latins sit and watch, or Aeneas will win and take Lavinia as his wife.

Latinus answers calmly. The braver Turnus is, the more carefully the old king must think. Turnus already has his father Daunus's kingdom and many cities he has taken. There are other noble girls in Latium he could marry. Then Latinus speaks honestly. The gods and the prophets all told him not to give his daughter to any of her old suitors. But because he loved Turnus, because they are relatives, and because his crying wife begged him, he broke every rule. He took Lavinia from her promised husband and started an unjust war. Since then, terrible things have happened. The Latins have lost two battles and can barely defend their city. The Tiber is still warm with their blood, and their bones lie white on the plain. If he is ready to make peace with the Trojans after Turnus dies, why not make peace now, while Turnus is alive? What will the Rutulians and all of Italy say if Turnus dies because of Latinus, while asking to marry his daughter? He asks Turnus to think of the changing fortunes of war and to pity his old father waiting sadly at home in Ardea.

These words only make Turnus angrier. He asks the king to stop worrying about him and to let him buy honour with death. His own hand also throws spears, he says, and he too can make wounds bleed. This time Aeneas's goddess mother will not be there to hide him in a cloud, as she did at Troy.

Queen Amata is terrified by this new plan. She cries and holds Turnus, ready to die. He is the only hope and comfort of her unhappy old age, she says. The honour and the kingdom of Latinus are in his hands, and he holds up their whole falling house. She begs him not to fight the Trojans. Whatever happens to him, will happen to her. She will die with him, rather than live as a prisoner and see Aeneas as her son-in-law. Lavinia hears her mother's words. Her cheeks turn red with a deep blush, like ivory stained with red dye, or white lilies mixed with roses. Virgil never tells us what Lavinia feels or thinks. She is silent through the whole poem. Turnus looks at her, and love makes him even more eager to fight. He tells Amata not to weep for him or send him to battle with such a bad omen. He is not free to delay his death. Then he sends his messenger Idmon to Aeneas with a message. Tomorrow at dawn, neither army will fight. Their blood alone will decide the war, and on that field the winner will win Lavinia.

Turnus goes home and looks with pride at his horses, whiter than snow and faster than the wind. He puts on his golden breastplate, his sword, his shield, and his red-crested helmet. The sword was made by Vulcan for his father Daunus. He picks up his strong spear, which he once took from an enemy, and speaks to it. It has never failed him, he says. Now let it help him kill the soft Trojan and drag in the dust his hair, curled with hot iron and wet with perfume. Sparks seem to fly from his face and fire from his eyes, like a bull preparing for its first fight, charging a tree with its horns.

Aeneas also prepares for battle in the armour his mother gave him. He is glad that the war will be decided by a treaty. He comforts his friends and calms Iulus's fear by telling them about fate. He sends his answer to Latinus and sets out the terms of the peace.

The next morning, the Rutulians and Trojans prepare a field under the city walls. In the middle they build altars of grass for the gods of both peoples. Priests in holy dress bring water and fire. The Italian army marches out of the gates, and on the other side come the Trojans and Etruscans, armed as if for battle. The captains ride up and down in gold and purple. Then they all return to their places, stick their spears in the ground, and lean their shields against them. Mothers, old men, and the crowd climb onto the towers and roofs to watch.

Juno watches from the top of a hill, which will later be called the Alban Mount. She speaks to Juturna, the sister of Turnus, a nymph of lakes and rivers. Jupiter once loved Juturna and made her immortal in return for taking her virginity. Juno tells her that she has always protected Turnus and his city while fate allowed it. But now she sees him going to fight against a stronger fate. His day of death is near. Juno cannot bear to watch. But Juturna may dare to help her brother, if she can. Juturna begins to cry and beats her breast. Juno tells her this is no time for tears. She must hurry and save her brother from death if she can, or start the war again and break the treaty. Then Juno leaves her, confused and full of pain.

The kings come out. Latinus rides in a chariot with four horses. He wears a crown with twelve golden rays, a sign of the Sun, his ancestor. Turnus comes behind two white horses, holding two spears. On the other side, Aeneas, the father of the Roman people, comes out of his camp in his shining divine armour, with Ascanius, the second hope of Rome, beside him. A priest in white brings a young pig and a sheep to the altars. They face the rising sun, scatter salted grain, cut hair from the animals' heads, and pour wine.

Aeneas, called "good" by Virgil here, draws his sword and makes a solemn promise. He calls on the Sun, the Earth, Jupiter, and Juno, whom he hopes will now be kinder. He calls on Mars, on the springs and rivers, and on the gods of the sky and sea. If Turnus wins, the Trojans will go away to Evander's city, Iulus will leave this land, and they will never attack this kingdom again. But if Aeneas wins, which he thinks more likely, he will not make the Italians obey the Trojans, and he does not want this kingdom for himself. Both peoples will be undefeated and will live together forever under equal laws. Aeneas will bring his gods and religious rites. Latinus, his father-in-law, will keep his army and his royal power. The Trojans will build a city for Aeneas, and Lavinia will give it her name.

Latinus then swears by Earth, Sea, and Sky, by Apollo and Diana, by Janus, and by the gods of the underworld. He touches the altar and promises that this peace will never be broken, whatever happens. Nothing will change his mind, not even if the land melts into the sea and the sky falls into the underworld. As surely as his royal sceptre, cut long ago from a tree and decorated with bronze, will never again grow leaves, so surely will this peace hold. They sacrifice the animals and place the offerings on the altars.

But for a long time the Rutulians have thought this fight is unfair. Their feelings change back and forth, and now they feel it even more as they look at the two men. Turnus walks quietly to the altar, praying with his eyes down. His cheeks are thin and his young face is pale. Juturna sees the soldiers' doubt. She takes the form of Camertes, a famous and brave warrior, and walks among them spreading rumours. Are they not ashamed, she says, to let one man risk his life for all of them? They are not fewer or weaker than the enemy. The enemy has only Trojans, Arcadians, and the Etruscans, and there is hardly one enemy for every two of them. Turnus will gain eternal fame, while they will lose their country and become slaves to proud masters if they sit here doing nothing.

The soldiers begin to murmur. Even the Latins, who were hoping for an end to the war, now want to fight again, and they pity Turnus. Then Juturna sends a false omen. In the red sky, an eagle, Jupiter's bird, chases a flock of water birds. It dives down and catches a beautiful swan in its claws. But all the other birds turn around and attack the eagle together in a dark cloud of wings. In the end it drops the swan into the river and flies away. The Rutulians shout with joy. Tolumnius, their prophet, cries that this is the sign he has prayed for. The Trojan stranger is the eagle who frightens them like weak birds. If they all fight together, he will run away across the sea. Tolumnius throws his spear at the enemy. It flies through the air and kills one of nine brothers, the handsome sons of an Arcadian man and an Etruscan mother.

The other brothers rush forward in anger with their swords. The Latins charge against them. The Trojans, Etruscans, and Arcadians charge too. Everyone is now eager to decide the war by the sword. They tear apart the altars. Spears fly like a storm of iron. Latinus runs away, carrying the images of the gods whose treaty has been broken. Messapus, eager to destroy the treaty, rides at the Etruscan king Aulestes. Aulestes trips over the altars behind him, falls, and begs for mercy, but Messapus kills him. He says that this is a better victim for the gods. Corynaeus takes a burning stick from the altar and sets an enemy's beard on fire. The fighting spreads everywhere.

But Aeneas stands without a helmet, holding up his empty hand and shouting to his men. Where are they running? Why this fighting? They must control their anger. The treaty is made and its laws are fixed. He alone has the right to fight. They must leave the fight to him, and his hand will make the treaty good. The altars have already made Turnus his. While he is still speaking, an arrow flies through the air and hits him. No one knows who shot it, or what god or chance gave this honour to the Rutulians. Nobody ever boasted of wounding Aeneas.

When Turnus sees Aeneas leaving the battle wounded and the Trojan leaders worried, he is filled with sudden hope. He calls for his chariot and armour and jumps in. He drives through the battle killing many men, like Mars, the god of war, in his chariot with Terror and Anger around him. His horses' hooves scatter blood and sand. He kills many Trojans. He kills Eumedes, the grandson of Dolon, the Trojan spy in Homer's Iliad. He puts his foot on Eumedes's neck and stabs him with his own sword. He mocks him: here are the western lands he came to win by war; this is how the Trojans found their city. He kills many others, and the Trojans run before him like waves before the north wind. A brave Trojan named Phegeus grabs the reins of his horses, but he is dragged along, and Turnus kills him.

Meanwhile Mnestheus, Achates, and Ascanius bring Aeneas back to the camp. He is bleeding and leaning on his long spear. He is angry and tries to pull out the broken arrow himself. He asks them to cut the wound open so he can go back to battle quickly. The old healer Iapyx comes. Long ago, Apollo loved Iapyx and offered him his gifts of prophecy, music, and archery. But Iapyx chose to learn the art of healing instead, to save his dying father, a quiet art without fame. Aeneas stands leaning on his spear, angry, while Iulus and many men cry around him. Iapyx tries hard with his hands and with healing herbs, but nothing works. He cannot pull out the arrowhead. Meanwhile the noise of battle comes closer, and spears begin to fall inside the camp.

Then Venus, hurt by her son's pain, picks a healing plant called dittany from Mount Ida in Crete, a plant that wild goats eat when they are wounded by arrows. Hidden in a cloud, she secretly mixes it into the water Iapyx is using, with sweet-smelling medicine and ambrosia, the food of the gods. Without knowing it, Iapyx washes the wound with this water. At once all the pain leaves Aeneas's body. The bleeding stops, the arrowhead falls out by itself, and his strength returns. Iapyx shouts for his armour. This is not human medicine, he says, and it is not his hand that has saved Aeneas. A god is at work and is sending him back to greater deeds.

Aeneas quickly puts on his golden leg guards, his breastplate, and his shield. He holds Ascanius in his armoured arms and kisses him lightly through his helmet. He says: "Learn courage and hard work from me, my son; learn good luck from others." Now his hand will protect Ascanius in war and lead him to great rewards. When Ascanius grows up, he must remember the example of his family, his father Aeneas and his uncle Hector. This short speech is very important. It shows how Aeneas sees his own life: full of duty and effort, but not of happiness.

Aeneas rushes out of the gates with his spear, followed by his army. Dust covers the field, and the ground shakes. Turnus and the Italians see them coming and feel a cold fear. Juturna is the first to hear the sound, and she runs away in terror. Aeneas drives his army forward like a storm cloud coming from the sea, which makes farmers tremble for their crops. His men kill many Italians. Tolumnius, the prophet who threw the first spear and broke the treaty, is killed. The Rutulians run. But Aeneas does not bother to kill those who run away or those who fight him. He is looking only for Turnus. He searches for him in the dust and calls him alone to fight.

Juturna is afraid for her brother. She throws Turnus's driver, Metiscus, out of the chariot and takes his place, looking and sounding just like him. She drives the chariot everywhere through the battle, like a swallow flying around a rich man's house looking for food for her babies. She shows her brother here and there but never lets him come close to Aeneas. Aeneas follows them through the confusion and calls out to Turnus. But every time he gets near, Juturna turns the horses away. Aeneas does not know what to do. Then Messapus throws a spear at him. Aeneas drops to one knee behind his shield, and the spear knocks off the top of his helmet. Now his anger rises. Forced by their trickery, he calls on Jupiter and the altars of the broken treaty to witness. Then he charges into the enemy and kills everyone in his way, letting his anger run free.

Virgil asks what god can help him tell all these deaths. He asks Jupiter if it was right for two peoples who would live together in peace forever to fight such a terrible war. Aeneas and Turnus both kill many men, like two fires in a dry forest or two rivers rushing down from the mountains. Aeneas kills Sucro and others. Turnus kills two brothers and hangs their heads on his chariot. Aeneas kills Murranus, a proud man who boasts of his royal ancestors, with a huge stone, and his own horses trample him. Turnus kills Hyllus and Cretheus. Aeneas kills Cupencus. Aeolus, a Trojan who survived the Greeks and Achilles, dies on the Laurentine plain. His great house stood near Mount Ida; his grave is in Latium. All the armies fight with all their strength.

Then Venus puts a new idea into her son's mind. Aeneas looks around for Turnus and sees that the city of Latinus is calm and untouched by the war. He decides to attack it. He calls his captains to a small hill. He tells them that Jupiter is with them. This city, the cause of the war, the royal home of Latinus, will be burned to the ground today unless its people accept defeat and obey. Should he wait until Turnus decides to fight him again? This city is the source of this evil war. He tells them to bring fire and take back the broken treaty in flames. The Trojans form a wedge and attack the walls with ladders and fire. Aeneas stands in front, reaching toward the walls. He shouts at Latinus and calls the gods to witness that the Italians have broken two treaties and forced him to fight again.

Inside the city there is panic and argument. Some want to open the gates to the Trojans and drag the king to the walls. Others take weapons to defend the city. The people are like bees in a rock when a shepherd fills their hive with smoke. Queen Amata sees from her roof that the enemy is climbing the walls and throwing fire onto the houses. She sees no Rutulian soldiers and no sign of Turnus. She believes that Turnus has been killed in battle. Overcome by grief, she cries out that she is the cause of all this evil. She tears her purple robe and hangs herself from a high beam. When the women hear of this, Lavinia tears her beautiful hair and scratches her rosy cheeks, and the women around her cry loudly. The news spreads through the city. Latinus tears his clothes and pours dust on his white hair, in despair at his wife's death and the fall of his city.

On the far edge of the field, Turnus is chasing a few scattered enemies, but he is losing his joy in fighting. The wind brings him the confused sound of screaming from the city. He stops his horses and asks what this terrible noise is. Juturna, still disguised as his driver, tries to keep him away. Let them chase the Trojans here, she says. Others can defend the city. Turnus will not be outdone in killing or glory. But Turnus answers that he recognized her long ago, when she first broke the treaty. She cannot hide that she is a goddess. Why did she come down from heaven? To watch her poor brother die? He saw his dear friend Murranus die before his eyes, calling his name. Ufens is dead too. Now his city is about to be destroyed. Should he allow it? Should he prove Drances right by running away? Is death really so bitter? He asks the spirits of the dead to welcome him, since the gods of heaven have turned against him. He will come down to them with a pure soul, never unworthy of his great ancestors.

Just then a man named Saces rides up on a foaming horse, with an arrow wound in his face. He begs Turnus to pity his people. He is their last hope. Aeneas is threatening to destroy the city, and fire is already flying onto the roofs. Everyone is looking to Turnus. Latinus does not know whom to choose as his son-in-law. And the queen, Turnus's strongest supporter, has killed herself. Only Messapus and Atinas are still fighting at the gates, while Turnus drives around in an empty field.

Turnus stands frozen. Shame, grief, love, madness, and courage fill his heart together. When his mind clears, he turns his burning eyes to the city. He sees a tower he built himself catch fire, with flames rising into the sky. Now he says to his sister that fate has won. She must stop holding him back. He will follow where the gods and hard fortune call. He has decided to face Aeneas and accept whatever bitterness death brings. She will not see him disgraced anymore. He asks her to let him be mad in this way before the end. He jumps from the chariot and runs through the enemy, leaving his sad sister behind, like a huge rock falling down a mountain and carrying trees and animals with it. He reaches the walls, where the ground is wet with blood. He raises his hand and shouts to the Rutulians and Latins to stop fighting. Whatever fortune is left belongs to him. He alone must pay for the broken treaty and decide the war with the sword. Everyone steps back and makes room.

When Aeneas hears Turnus's name, he leaves the walls and stops all the fighting, full of joy. He clashes his armour, huge as a great mountain. All the Rutulians, Trojans, and Italians turn to watch, even the soldiers on the walls and those attacking the gates. Latinus himself is amazed to see these two great men, born in distant parts of the world, meeting to decide things with the sword. The two heroes throw their spears from far away and then crash together with their shields. The earth groans, and they strike blow after blow. They are like two bulls fighting for the leadership of the herd, while the cows wait silently to see which one they will follow. Jupiter himself holds up a pair of scales and puts the fates of the two men in them, to see which one will sink toward death.

Turnus jumps forward, raises his sword high, and strikes with all his strength. Both armies cry out. But his sword breaks in the middle of the blow. He sees a strange handle in his empty hand and runs. Virgil explains the story people tell. In his hurry that morning, Turnus forgot his father's sword, made by Vulcan, and took the sword of his driver Metiscus. That ordinary sword was good enough against fleeing Trojans. But when it struck the divine armour made by Vulcan, it broke like ice, and its pieces now shine on the sand. Turnus runs in circles across the plain. The Trojans surround him on all sides, and a marsh and the city walls block his way.

Aeneas chases him, though his wounded knee slows him down. He is like a hunting dog chasing a deer trapped by a river and the hunters' nets. The dog almost catches it, and its jaws snap on empty air. As Turnus runs, he shouts at the Rutulians by name and begs them for his own sword. But Aeneas threatens to kill anyone who comes near, and to destroy their city. Five times they run around the field and back again. It is no small prize they are running for: it is Turnus's life.

Near by stands the stump of a wild olive tree, sacred to the god Faunus. Sailors who were saved from the sea used to hang gifts there. But the Trojans had cut down the tree to clear the field. Aeneas's spear, thrown earlier, is stuck deep in its root. Aeneas bends to pull it out, so he can throw it at the man he cannot catch. Turnus, terrified, prays to Faunus and to the Earth to hold the spear tight, because he has always honoured them, and the Trojans have dishonoured the tree with war. His prayer is heard. Aeneas pulls with all his strength, but he cannot free the spear. While he struggles, Juturna, again disguised as Metiscus, runs forward and gives her brother his own sword. Venus is angry that the nymph dares to do this. She comes and pulls Aeneas's spear free from the root. Now both men are armed again, Turnus with his trusted sword and Aeneas with his tall spear. They face each other, breathing hard.

Meanwhile in heaven, Jupiter speaks to Juno, who is watching from a bright cloud. What will be the end? She knows that Aeneas is destined to become a god of his country and rise to the stars. What does she hope for, sitting there in the cold clouds? Was it right for a god-to-be to be wounded by a mortal? Was it right to give Turnus back his sword? Juturna could do nothing without Juno's help. Now she must stop. She has chased the Trojans over land and sea, started a terrible war, brought mourning to a royal house, and mixed grief with a wedding. He forbids her to go further.

Juno looks down and answers. Because she knows this is his will, she has left Turnus alone on earth, against her wishes. Otherwise she would be standing in the battle line surrounded by flames. She admits that she told Juturna to help her brother, but she swears by the river Styx that she did not tell her to shoot the arrow at Aeneas. Now she will leave the battle, though she hates to. But she asks one thing, which fate does not forbid. When the two peoples make peace with happy marriages and laws, let the native Latins keep their old name. Do not make them become Trojans or change their language or their clothes. Let Latium remain. Let the kings of Alba last for ages, and let Rome be strong with Italian courage. Troy has fallen. Let it stay fallen, and its name with it.

Jupiter smiles. She is truly his sister, he says, a daughter of Saturn, since such waves of anger rise in her heart. But she should calm this useless rage. He gives her what she wants. The Italians will keep their language and customs, and their name will stay as it is. The Trojans will only mix their blood with them and disappear into them. Jupiter will add the Trojan religious rites, and he will make them all Latins with one language. From them will come a people of mixed Italian blood that will be greater than all men and even gods in "pietas", in duty and devotion, and no nation will honour Juno more. Juno agrees and is glad. She leaves her cloud and goes away. This is how Juno's anger, the cause of the whole story since Book 1, finally ends. It also explains why the Romans were called Latins and not Trojans.

Then Jupiter decides to separate Juturna from her brother. There are two monsters called the Dirae, sisters of the Fury Megaera, born of Night, with snakes and wings like the wind. They stand by Jupiter's throne and bring terror to people when he sends disease, death, or war. Jupiter sends one of them down to earth as a sign for Juturna. She flies down like a poisoned arrow. When she reaches the battle, she shrinks into the shape of a small owl, the kind that cries at night on graves and ruined roofs. She flies back and forth in front of Turnus's face and beats her wings against his shield. A strange fear takes away Turnus's strength. His hair stands up, and he cannot speak.

Juturna recognizes the sound of the Fury's wings. She tears her hair, scratches her face, and beats her breast. How can his sister help him now? What can she do against this monster? She leaves the battle. She knows these wings and this sound of death, and she understands Jupiter's proud command. Is this how he repays her for taking her virginity? Why did he give her eternal life? If she were mortal, she could end her pain and go with her brother into the darkness. What will be sweet to her without him? She wishes the earth would open and swallow her. She covers her head with her grey veil and sinks, groaning, into the river.

Aeneas comes forward, shaking his huge spear like a tree, and speaks fiercely. Why does Turnus delay? Why does he still run? They must fight now, hand to hand, not race each other. Turnus may change into any shape, use all his skill and courage, fly to the stars, or hide in the earth. Turnus shakes his head. He says that Aeneas's fierce words do not frighten him. Only the gods frighten him, and Jupiter's hatred. He says no more. He sees a huge old stone lying on the plain, a boundary stone between fields. Twelve strong men of today could hardly lift it onto their shoulders. Turnus picks it up with a shaking hand, runs, and throws it at Aeneas. But he does not feel like himself. He does not know that he is running or lifting or throwing. His knees shake, and his blood is cold. The stone flies through empty air but does not reach Aeneas.

Virgil compares this to a bad dream. At night, in sleep, we try to run, but we cannot. Our legs fail, our tongue does not work, and no words come out. In the same way, the terrible goddess takes away all of Turnus's strength. He looks at his Rutulians and at the city. He hesitates and trembles at the spear coming toward him. He sees no way to escape and no way to attack. He cannot see his chariot or his sister anywhere.

While he hesitates, Aeneas aims his deadly spear, waits for the right moment, and throws it with all his strength. It roars louder than a stone from a war machine or a crash of thunder. It flies like a dark whirlwind, breaks through the edge of Turnus's armour and the seven layers of his shield, and goes through his thigh. Turnus falls to the ground with his knee bent under him. The Rutulians jump up with a groan, and the hills and forests echo their cry.

Turnus lifts his eyes humbly and stretches out his hand, asking for mercy. He says he deserves this, and he does not ask to be spared. Aeneas should use his good fortune. But if a father's grief can move him, he asks Aeneas to remember that Anchises was such a father to him. He asks him to pity the old age of Turnus's father, Daunus, and to give Turnus back to his family, alive or dead. Aeneas has won. The Italians have seen Turnus defeated and begging. Lavinia is his wife. He asks Aeneas not to carry his hatred further.

Aeneas stands over him in his armour, his eyes moving, and he holds back his sword hand. More and more, Turnus's words begin to move him, and he starts to hesitate. Then he sees, high on Turnus's shoulder, a sword belt he knows well, with its shining studs. It is the belt of young Pallas, whom Turnus killed and whose belt he took and wore as a prize. When Aeneas sees this reminder of his terrible grief, he burns with fury. He shouts in terrible anger. Should Turnus escape, dressed in the spoils of one Aeneas loved? It is Pallas who strikes this blow, he says. Pallas sacrifices him and takes revenge with his guilty blood. With these words, Aeneas drives his sword deep into Turnus's chest. Turnus's body goes cold, and his life, with a groan, flies angrily down into the shadows.

That is the end of the poem. There is no wedding, no funeral, no founding of the city, and no word about what Aeneas felt afterwards. Virgil uses for Turnus's last breath the same words he used for the death of Camilla in Book 11. This sudden ending has puzzled readers for two thousand years. Virgil died before he finished revising the Aeneid, but most scholars think the ending is where he meant to stop. Some readers see Aeneas's act as justice and loyalty to Pallas and Evander. Others see it as a failure of the mercy that Anchises asked of the Romans in Book 6, when he told them to spare the defeated and crush the proud. The poem leaves this question open. It ends not in celebration, but with the anger of its hero and the death of his enemy, a final reminder of how much suffering the founding of Rome required.

# The Iliad

Homer

## Before You Begin

The Iliad is one of the oldest stories in European literature. It is a long poem about war, anger, honor, and grief. People say that a poet called Homer made it, perhaps around the eighth century before Christ. But we know almost nothing about him. Some scholars think he was one real man. Others think the poem grew over many generations of singers, who learned it by heart and performed it aloud. So when this summary says "Homer," it simply means the poet of the Iliad, whoever he was.

The poem is set during the Trojan War. Troy was a rich city near the sea, in what is now Turkey. Its king was an old man named Priam. One of his sons, Paris, visited Sparta in Greece. There he took Helen, the beautiful wife of King Menelaus, and carried her back to Troy, together with much treasure. To get her back, the Greek kings joined together and sailed to Troy with a huge army. Their leader was Agamemnon, the brother of Menelaus. When the poem begins, the Greeks have been camped on the beach outside Troy for nine years. They have attacked many smaller towns nearby, but they have not taken the city itself.

The Iliad does not tell the whole war. It does not tell how the war started, and it does not tell how Troy finally fell. It covers only a few weeks in the tenth year. Its real subject is the anger of one man, Achilles, the greatest Greek warrior. The poem shows what his anger costs his friends, his enemies, and himself.

Here are the main people on the Greek side. Achilles is the strongest fighter. His mother is a sea goddess, and he knows his life will be short. Agamemnon is the proud commander of the whole army. Menelaus is Helen's husband. Odysseus is clever and good with words. Nestor is a very old king who loves to give advice and tell stories of his youth. Ajax is a giant of a man who fights like a wall. There are two men called Ajax, but the big one, the son of Telamon, is the one most often meant. Diomedes is a young and fearless fighter. Patroclus is the closest friend of Achilles. In the poem, the Greeks are also called Achaeans, Danaans, or Argives. These names all mean the same army.

On the Trojan side, Hector is the eldest son of Priam and the greatest Trojan warrior. He fights to protect his city and his family. Priam is the old king. Paris is the handsome prince who started the war. Andromache is Hector's loving wife, and they have a baby son. Helen lives in Troy with Paris and often feels shame about the war. Aeneas is a brave Trojan prince whose mother is a goddess.

The gods also play a large part. They watch the war from Mount Olympus, and they often come down to help or harm. Zeus is the king of the gods. Hera is his wife, and she hates Troy. Athena is a warrior goddess who helps the Greeks. Apollo, the archer god, helps the Trojans. Aphrodite, the goddess of love, protects Paris and Helen. Poseidon, the god of the sea, helps the Greeks. Thetis is the sea goddess who is the mother of Achilles. Hephaestus is the god of fire and the smith who makes things from metal. Ares is the god of war, and he fights for the Trojans.

This summary follows the free English prose translation by Samuel Butler, which you can read in [Project Gutenberg's edition of Samuel Butler's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2199). Butler uses the Latin names for most of the gods. He writes Jove for Zeus, Juno for Hera, Minerva for Athena, Venus for Aphrodite, Neptune for Poseidon, Vulcan for Hephaestus, and Mars for Ares. He also writes Ulysses for Odysseus. This summary uses the Greek names, which are more common today. The poem is divided into twenty-four parts, called books. Each section below tells one book in order.

## Book 1: The Quarrel

The poem begins by asking a goddess to sing about the anger of Achilles. This anger, the poet says, brought endless pain to the Greeks and sent many brave men down to Hades, the world of the dead. It all started with a quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army.

The trouble begins with a priest of the god Apollo, an old man called Chryses. The Greeks have captured his daughter, Chryseis, in a raid, and she has been given to Agamemnon as his prize. Chryses comes to the Greek camp with a large ransom, which means a payment to free a prisoner. He carries the holy staff of Apollo and asks politely for his daughter back. All the Greeks want to respect the priest and accept the gifts. But Agamemnon speaks to him roughly. He says the girl will grow old in his house in Greece, and he tells the old man to go away and never return. Chryses is afraid and leaves. Alone on the beach, he prays to Apollo for revenge.

Apollo hears him. Angry, he comes down from Mount Olympus with his silver bow and shoots arrows of disease into the Greek camp. First the animals die, then the men. For nine days the fires that burn the dead never stop. On the tenth day, Achilles calls the army together. The goddess Hera, who loves the Greeks, has put this idea into his mind. Achilles asks for a prophet to explain why Apollo is angry.

The prophet Calchas stands up. He is afraid, because the truth will offend a powerful king. He asks Achilles to protect him, and Achilles promises that no Greek will touch him, not even Agamemnon. Then Calchas explains that Apollo is angry because Agamemnon insulted his priest. The plague will stop only when the girl goes back to her father, without any ransom, together with a large sacrifice for the god.

Agamemnon is furious. He says he wants to keep the girl, because he likes her even more than his own wife, Clytemnestra. Still, he agrees to give her up to save his people. But he demands another prize at once, so that he is not the only leader without one. Achilles answers that there is no shared store of prizes left. Everything has already been divided. He asks Agamemnon to wait, and promises that he will be paid back three or four times over when Troy falls. Agamemnon refuses. He threatens to take the prize of Achilles, or of Ajax, or of Odysseus.

Now Achilles loses his temper. He says he has no quarrel of his own with the Trojans. He came to Troy only to help Agamemnon and his brother Menelaus. He does most of the fighting, but Agamemnon always takes the biggest share of the plunder, which means the goods taken from defeated enemies. Achilles says he will sail home to Phthia. Agamemnon replies coldly that he can go. Then, to show his power, Agamemnon says he will take Briseis, the young woman who was given to Achilles as his prize.

Achilles begins to draw his sword to kill Agamemnon. At that moment, the goddess Athena comes down from heaven, sent by Hera, who cares for both men. Only Achilles can see her. She takes him by his yellow hair and tells him not to fight with his sword. She promises that one day he will receive gifts three times as rich because of this insult. Achilles obeys and puts his sword back. But he goes on insulting Agamemnon with words. He calls him a coward who never fights in the front line and only robs his own men. Then he swears a solemn oath on the speaker's staff. One day, he says, the Greeks will miss Achilles badly, when they are dying at the hands of Hector, and Agamemnon will regret insulting the best of the Greeks. He throws the staff to the ground.

Old Nestor, the wise king of Pylos, tries to make peace. He reminds them that he has advised greater men than they are. He asks Agamemnon not to take the girl, and he asks Achilles to respect the king. But neither man will listen. Achilles says he will not fight over the girl, but he warns Agamemnon not to touch anything else of his.

The meeting ends. Agamemnon sends Chryseis home by ship with a sacrifice, and Odysseus, the clever Greek leader, goes as captain. Then Agamemnon sends two heralds, or official messengers, to take Briseis. Achilles does not blame the heralds. He tells his friend Patroclus to bring her out. Briseis does not want to go.

Then Achilles walks alone by the sea and weeps. He calls to his mother, the sea goddess Thetis. He reminds her that he is fated to have a short life, and says that Zeus should at least give him honor. Thetis rises from the sea like a mist and comforts him. Achilles tells her the whole story. He asks her to go to Zeus and beg him to help the Trojans. He wants the Greeks to be pushed back to their ships and killed, so that Agamemnon will learn how foolish he was to insult him. Thetis weeps. She is sad that her son's life will be both short and full of sorrow. But she promises to go to Zeus when he returns from a feast in twelve days. Until then, Achilles must stay away from the fighting.

Meanwhile, Odysseus arrives at Chryse and gives the girl back to her father. Chryses prays to Apollo again, this time to stop the plague, and Apollo listens. The Greeks make their sacrifice, feast, and sing to the god all day, then sail back. Achilles stays by his ships. He does not go to meetings or to battle, though his heart longs for the fighting.

After twelve days, Thetis goes up to Olympus. She kneels before Zeus, holds his knees, and asks him to give victory to the Trojans until the Greeks honor her son. Zeus is silent at first. He knows his wife Hera will be angry, because she hates the Trojans. But at last he nods his head, which is his most solemn promise, and great Olympus shakes.

Hera guesses at once that Thetis has been with Zeus. She accuses him of making secret plans. Zeus tells her sharply to sit down and be quiet, or he will hurt her. Hera is frightened and stays silent. The other gods are upset. Then Hephaestus, the lame smith god and son of Hera, makes peace. He gives his mother a cup of nectar, the drink of the gods. He reminds her that Zeus once threw him off Olympus when he tried to help her, and he fell all day before landing on the island of Lemnos. Hera smiles, and the gods laugh as Hephaestus hurries around serving drinks. They feast until sunset, and then go to sleep.

This first book sets up everything that follows. A quarrel about honor and prizes splits the Greek army. Achilles feels that his honor has been stolen, and his anger will now bring death to many of his own friends.

## Book 2: The Test and the Two Armies

That night, while gods and men sleep, Zeus lies awake. He is thinking about his promise to Thetis, to give honor to Achilles by letting many Greeks die. He decides to send a false dream to Agamemnon. The dream takes the shape of old Nestor, the advisor whom Agamemnon respects most. It tells Agamemnon that Zeus wants him to arm the Greeks at once, because today he will take Troy. The gods, it says, are no longer divided, since Hera has won them all to her side. Agamemnon believes it completely. He does not know that Zeus is planning many more hard battles.

In the morning, Agamemnon first meets with the leading kings beside Nestor's ship. He tells them about the dream. Then he explains a strange plan. Before the battle, he wants to test the spirit of the army. He will tell the soldiers to give up and sail home. The other leaders must then go among the men and stop them. Nestor agrees that they should trust the dream, since the supreme commander saw it.

The whole army gathers, buzzing like bees. Agamemnon stands up, holding his royal staff, which was made by the god Hephaestus and passed down through his family. He makes a gloomy speech. He says Zeus has broken his promise. Nine years have passed, the wood of the ships is rotting, and wives and children are waiting at home. The Greeks are many more than the Trojans, but the Trojans have allies from other lands. So, he says, let us sail home, because we will never take Troy.

The test goes badly wrong. The men do not stay to argue. They rush to the ships with joyful shouts, like waves in a storm or corn bending in the wind. They begin to drag the ships into the sea. The Greeks might have gone home, against what fate had planned. But Hera sees it and sends Athena down. Athena finds Odysseus, who is standing sadly by his ship. She tells him to stop the men, so that the Trojans will not keep Helen after so many Greeks have died for her.

Odysseus takes Agamemnon's staff and runs through the camp. To kings and leaders he speaks politely. He tells them that Agamemnon was only testing the army and may be angry. But when he finds a common soldier shouting, he hits him with the staff. He tells him to be quiet and obey his betters, because one king must rule. Slowly the men return to the meeting.

Only one man keeps shouting. His name is Thersites. He is the ugliest man in the army, with bent legs and a pointed head, and he loves to insult the leaders. He attacks Agamemnon. He says Agamemnon's tents are already full of bronze and women, and that the soldiers should sail home and leave him alone at Troy. He adds that Agamemnon has insulted Achilles, a far better man. Odysseus answers him harshly. He threatens to strip him naked and beat him out of the meeting. Then he hits Thersites on the back with the golden staff. Thersites sits down crying in pain. The soldiers feel a little sorry for him, but they laugh. They say this is the best thing Odysseus has ever done. This scene shows how ordinary men are treated in the poem. A common man who speaks against kings is silenced, even when some of his complaints sound true.

Now Odysseus speaks to the whole army, while Athena, disguised as a herald, keeps them quiet. He says he understands that the men are tired, after nine long years away from home. But it would be shameful to go home with nothing. He reminds them of a sign they saw at Aulis, the harbor where the fleet gathered before sailing to Troy. During a sacrifice, a snake came out from under an altar, climbed a tree, and ate eight baby sparrows and their mother. Then Zeus turned the snake to stone. The prophet Calchas explained the sign: they would fight nine years at Troy, and take the city in the tenth. Now, Odysseus says, it is all coming true.

The men cheer. Then Nestor speaks. He scolds them for talking like children. He reminds them of their oaths and of the lightning Zeus sent as a good sign when they sailed. He gives practical advice: Agamemnon should arrange the army by tribes and clans, so that each group helps its own people, and it becomes clear who is brave and who is a coward. Agamemnon praises Nestor. He even admits that he was the first to give offense in the quarrel with Achilles. He orders the men to eat, prepare their weapons, and get ready to fight all day. Anyone who hides by the ships, he says, will be left for dogs and vultures.

The Greeks eat and make sacrifices. Agamemnon sacrifices a bull and prays that Troy will fall before the sun sets, and that he will kill Hector. Zeus accepts the sacrifice, but he does not grant the prayer. Then Athena moves through the army with her aegis, a magic goat-skin shield with golden tassels. She fills every man with courage, so that war now seems sweeter to them than going home. The army marches onto the plain like a forest fire, like great flocks of birds, like countless flies.

Here the poet asks the Muses, the goddesses of song, to help him remember all the leaders. This long passage is known as the Catalogue of Ships. It lists each Greek group, its home towns, its leaders, and the number of its ships. Agamemnon brings the most ships, one hundred. Nestor brings ninety, and Diomedes eighty. Menelaus leads the men of Sparta. Odysseus brings twelve ships from Ithaca, and the great Ajax brings twelve from Salamis. Idomeneus leads the Cretans. The catalogue also mentions men who are absent. Achilles has fifty ships, but his men, the Myrmidons, are not fighting, because he is still angry about Briseis. They pass the time on the beach throwing discs and spears. The archer Philoctetes was left behind on the island of Lemnos after a snake bit him. Protesilaus, the first Greek to jump onto Trojan soil, is already dead. The poet says that, while Achilles stays away, the best Greek warrior is Ajax, son of Telamon, and the best horses belong to Eumelus.

Then Zeus sends Iris, the messenger goddess, to the Trojans. She takes the shape of Polites, one of Priam's sons, who is a lookout. She warns that a huge army is coming, and tells Hector to organize the allies by their peoples. Hector recognizes the goddess and calls everyone to arms. The Trojans pour out of the gates.

The book ends with a shorter list of the Trojans and their allies. Hector leads the Trojans. Aeneas, son of the goddess Aphrodite, leads the Dardanians. Pandarus, an archer taught by Apollo, leads another group. Many allies come from far away and speak different languages, such as the Thracians, the Paeonians, and the Carians. Sarpedon and Glaucus lead the Lycians. The two great armies now face each other on the plain.

## Book 3: Paris and Menelaus

The two armies move toward each other. The Trojans advance with loud cries, like flocks of birds. The Greeks march in silence, ready to stand by one another. Dust rises from their feet like a mist.

Paris, the Trojan prince who took Helen, steps out in front of his army. In Butler's translation he is often called Alexandrus, his other name. He wears a panther skin and waves two spears, challenging the best Greek to fight him. Menelaus, Helen's husband, sees him and is glad, like a hungry lion that finds food. He jumps down from his chariot. But when Paris sees Menelaus coming, he is afraid. He pulls back into the crowd of Trojans, like a man who suddenly sees a snake.

Hector, the eldest son of King Priam, is ashamed of his brother. He tells Paris that he is beautiful to look at but has no courage. He says Paris should never have been born. Paris brought a woman from a far country and caused sorrow for his father, his city, and his whole people. Now he will not even face the man whose wife he stole. Hector says the Trojans should have stoned him long ago.

Paris accepts that the criticism is fair. But he says Hector should not mock his good looks, because they are gifts from the goddess Aphrodite, and gifts from the gods must be respected. Then he makes an offer. He will fight Menelaus alone. The winner will take Helen and her treasure. Everyone else will swear a peace treaty, so that the Trojans can stay in Troy and the Greeks can sail home. Hector is glad. He holds back the Trojans, and Agamemnon stops the Greeks from throwing stones at him. Hector announces the offer. Menelaus accepts. He says both sides have suffered enough because of this quarrel. He asks for animals to sacrifice and asks that King Priam himself come to swear the oath, because Priam's sons cannot be trusted. Both armies are happy. They think the war is about to end. They take off their armor and sit down.

Meanwhile, the messenger goddess Iris visits Helen in the shape of one of Priam's daughters. Helen is weaving a large purple cloth that shows the battles fought for her sake. Iris tells her that the two men are going to fight for her. Helen suddenly feels a strong longing for her first husband, her city, and her parents. She covers her head and hurries, in tears, to the Scaean Gates, the main gate of Troy.

There, on the wall, sit Priam and the old men of Troy. They are too old to fight, but they love to talk. When they see Helen, they say quietly to each other that it is no surprise men fight for such a beautiful woman. Still, they wish she would go away, before she brings more sorrow to their children.

Priam, however, is kind. He calls Helen to sit beside him. He says he does not blame her; the gods caused this war. This famous scene is sometimes called the view from the wall. Priam asks Helen to name the Greek leaders he can see. Helen says she wishes she had died before coming to Troy and leaving her daughter and friends. Then she points out Agamemnon, a good king and a strong fighter, who was once her brother-in-law. Next Priam asks about a shorter, broad-chested man who walks among the ranks like a ram among sheep. Helen says it is Odysseus, a man full of clever tricks. The old Trojan Antenor adds a memory. Odysseus and Menelaus once came to Troy as envoys, or official messengers, to ask for Helen back. Menelaus spoke briefly and clearly. Odysseus at first looked awkward and silent, but when he began to speak, his words came like winter snow, and nobody could match him. Then Helen names the giant Ajax and the Cretan king Idomeneus. She looks for her two brothers, Castor and Pollux, but cannot find them. She wonders if they stayed home in shame because of her. She does not know that they are already dead and buried in their own land.

Now the heralds bring the animals for the sacrifice, and Priam drives out onto the plain. Agamemnon cuts wool from the lambs' heads and prays to Zeus, the Sun, the Earth, and the Rivers to witness the oath. If Paris kills Menelaus, the Greeks will go home. If Menelaus kills Paris, the Trojans must return Helen and her treasure and pay a fine. Agamemnon kills the lambs. Both armies pour wine on the ground and pray that anyone who breaks the oath will have his brains spilled like this wine. But the poet tells us that Zeus does not yet grant this prayer. Priam says he cannot bear to watch his son fight, and he drives back to the city.

Hector and Odysseus measure the ground. They shake lots in a helmet to decide who throws first, and Paris wins. Paris puts on his armor. He throws first, but his spear does not go through Menelaus's shield. Menelaus prays to Zeus for revenge, so that in future no man will wrong the host who welcomes him into his home. His spear goes through Paris's shield and armor and tears his shirt, but Paris twists aside and survives. Menelaus strikes Paris's helmet with his sword, but the sword breaks into pieces. Menelaus cries out that Zeus is the cruelest of the gods. Then he grabs Paris by the horsehair crest of his helmet and drags him toward the Greek lines. The helmet strap chokes Paris.

At this moment Aphrodite saves him. She breaks the leather strap, so that Menelaus is left holding an empty helmet. When Menelaus rushes back with his spear, Aphrodite hides Paris in a thick mist and carries him away to his own bedroom.

Then Aphrodite goes to fetch Helen, disguised as an old woman who used to work wool for her in Sparta. She tells Helen that Paris is waiting in his room, looking so fine that he seems ready for a dance. Helen recognizes the goddess by her beauty and is angry. She asks whether Aphrodite wants to give her to yet another man in another country. She tells the goddess to go and sit with Paris herself. She says she will not go to his bed, because the women of Troy would speak badly of her. Aphrodite becomes furious. She threatens to stop loving Helen and to make both armies hate her, so that she will die badly. Helen is frightened, and she obeys in silence.

In the bedroom, Helen sits facing Paris and turns her eyes away. She says she wishes he had died at the hands of her first husband, the better man. She mocks him for his old boasting. Paris answers that Menelaus won this time with Athena's help, and next time he may win himself. Then he tells Helen that he has never desired her so much, not even when he first carried her away. He leads her to bed, and she goes with him.

Meanwhile, Menelaus searches the battlefield like a wild animal, but nobody can find Paris. The Trojans would not hide him even if they could, because they hate him like death. Agamemnon announces that Menelaus has clearly won. He demands that the Trojans give back Helen and her treasure and pay the fine. The Greeks shout their approval. The book shows how small personal choices, like the cowardice of Paris and the will of a goddess, keep the great war going.

## Book 4: The Broken Truce

On Mount Olympus, the gods sit together drinking nectar and looking down at Troy. Zeus decides to tease his wife Hera. He points out that Menelaus has two goddesses on his side, Hera and Athena, but they only watch, while Aphrodite has just saved Paris. Menelaus clearly won the duel. So, Zeus asks, why not make peace now? Menelaus could take Helen home, and Troy could survive.

Athena is furious but says nothing. Hera cannot stay quiet. She says she has worked very hard to gather the Greek army against Priam, and she will not let all her effort be wasted. Zeus is angry. He asks what Priam and his people have done to her, that she wants to eat them alive. He says that of all cities under the sky, he loved Troy most, because its people always gave him good sacrifices. Still, he does not want a long fight with his wife, so he agrees to let Troy be destroyed. But he warns her: one day he may want to destroy a city she loves, and she must not stop him. Hera answers that her favorite cities are Argos, Sparta, and Mycenae, and he may destroy them whenever he likes. She asks only that Athena be sent down, to make the Trojans break the oath first. Zeus agrees. This scene shows how the gods trade human cities like pieces in a game.

Athena flies down like a shooting star. Both armies see the light and wonder whether it means war or peace. She takes the shape of a Trojan soldier and finds Pandarus, the archer who leads men from the land near Mount Ida. She tells him that if he shoots Menelaus with an arrow, all the Trojans will thank him, and Paris will give him rich gifts. Pandarus is foolish enough to agree. He takes out his famous bow, made from the horns of a wild goat. His men hide him behind their shields. He prays to Apollo, the archer god, and shoots.

But the gods have not forgotten Menelaus. Athena herself turns the arrow aside, the way a mother brushes a fly away from her sleeping child. She guides it to where Menelaus's belt and armor are thickest. The arrow goes through the belt, the armor, and an inner guard, and only scratches the skin. Still, blood runs down his legs.

Agamemnon is terrified. He holds his brother's hand and blames himself for letting him fight. He says the Trojans have broken their oaths, and Zeus will surely punish them one day: Troy will fall, with Priam and his people. But if Menelaus dies now, the Greeks will go home in shame, and some Trojan will jump on his tomb and laugh. Menelaus calms him. The wound is not deep, he says. Agamemnon sends the herald Talthybius to bring Machaon, a healer and the son of the famous doctor Asclepius. Machaon pulls out the arrow, cleans the wound, and puts on healing medicine.

While they are busy, the Trojans put their armor back on and come forward. The war starts again. Now Agamemnon is not lazy or afraid. He walks on foot through the army, encouraging the brave and scolding the slow. He tells his men that Zeus will not help liars, and that the Trojans who broke the oath will be eaten by vultures.

He visits his leaders one by one. He praises Idomeneus, the king of Crete, who promises to fight hard. He praises the two men called Ajax, whose troops look like a dark storm cloud. He finds old Nestor arranging his men with great skill. Nestor puts the chariots in front, the best foot soldiers at the back, and the cowards in the middle, so they must fight. He tells the chariot drivers to stay together and not rush ahead alone. Agamemnon wishes Nestor were still young. Nestor agrees that age has weakened him, but says old men can still give advice.

Then Agamemnon finds Menestheus, the leader of the Athenians, and Odysseus standing still. They have not yet heard the call to battle. Agamemnon insults them. He says they are always first at feasts but last in battle. Odysseus is angry and tells him he is talking nonsense. He promises Agamemnon will see him fighting in the front line. Agamemnon smiles and takes back his words.

Next he finds Diomedes, the young son of Tydeus, standing by his chariot with his friend Sthenelus. Agamemnon scolds him too. He tells a long story about Diomedes's father, Tydeus. Once Tydeus went alone as a messenger to the city of Thebes. There he beat all the men in contests. They sent fifty men to ambush him on his way home, and he killed all but one. Agamemnon says the son talks better than he fights. Diomedes says nothing, out of respect. But Sthenelus answers angrily. He says they are better than their fathers, because they actually captured Thebes, where their fathers failed. Diomedes tells his friend to be quiet. He says it is right for the commander to push the men forward, because Agamemnon will get the glory if they win and the shame if they lose. Then he jumps down, and his armor rings loudly. Diomedes's calm answer prepares us for his great day of fighting in the next book.

The armies meet. The Greeks march in total silence, obeying their leaders. The Trojans make a noise like thousands of bleating sheep, because their allies speak many different languages. Ares, the war god, drives the Trojans. Athena drives the Greeks. With them come Panic, Fear, and Strife, who starts small but grows until her head touches the sky.

The fighting is terrible. Shields crash together, and the earth runs red with blood, like the roar of flooding rivers. Antilochus, the young son of Nestor, kills the first man, a Trojan named Echepolus. A Greek leader who tries to strip the body of its armor is killed in turn. Ajax kills a young Trojan named Simoeisius, who was born beside the river Simois. The poet notes sadly that he never lived to repay his parents for raising him. He falls like a tall tree cut down for a chariot wheel. When a friend of Odysseus is killed, Odysseus is furious and kills one of Priam's sons. The Trojans start to fall back.

Then Apollo shouts from the citadel of Troy. He tells the Trojans not to give up. The Greeks are not made of stone or iron, he says, and Achilles is not fighting. On the other side, Athena urges the Greeks on. A Thracian leader crushes a Greek captain's leg with a stone and then kills him. He is killed himself a moment later. The book ends with many Trojans and Greeks lying face down in the dust side by side. The poet says that only a man protected by Athena could have walked safely through that battle.

## Book 5: Diomedes Fights the Gods

This book belongs to Diomedes, the young son of Tydeus. Athena fills him with courage and makes fire shine from his helmet and shield like a bright star. She sends him into the thickest part of the fighting.

Diomedes begins by killing a Trojan named Phegeus. His brother runs away, and the god Hephaestus hides him in darkness, so that their old father, a priest of Hephaestus, will not lose both sons. Then Athena takes Ares, the war god, by the hand. She suggests that they both leave the battle and let Zeus decide who wins. She leads him to the bank of the river Scamander. Without Ares, the Trojans are driven back, and each Greek leader kills a man. Agamemnon, Idomeneus, Menelaus, and others all strike down Trojans. Meriones kills the man who built the ships that carried Paris to Greece, the ships that started all the trouble.

Diomedes rushes across the plain like a river in flood that breaks through walls and fields. Then Pandarus, the archer who broke the truce in Book 4, hits him in the shoulder with an arrow. Pandarus shouts that the bravest Greek is wounded. But Diomedes's friend Sthenelus pulls out the arrow. Diomedes prays to Athena, and she comes to him. She gives him new strength. She also removes a mist from his eyes, so that he can now tell gods from men on the battlefield. She warns him not to fight any god, except one: if Aphrodite comes into battle, he should wound her.

Diomedes returns to the fight three times fiercer, like a wounded lion among sheep. He kills many Trojans, including two sons of Priam. Now Aeneas, the Trojan prince whose mother is Aphrodite, finds Pandarus. He asks him to shoot this man, who might even be a god. Pandarus explains that he has already hit Diomedes, and Menelaus before him, and both are still alive. Some god must be protecting Diomedes. He regrets that he left his horses and chariots at home. He swears to break his bow and burn it if he ever returns home. So Aeneas takes him in his own chariot, pulled by wonderful horses descended from a gift of Zeus, and they drive at Diomedes together.

Sthenelus begs Diomedes to retreat, but he refuses. He says he comes from a family that does not run. He tells Sthenelus to steal Aeneas's famous horses if they win. Pandarus throws his spear and boasts that he has hit Diomedes through the body. But he has only struck the shield. Diomedes throws, and Athena guides the spear into Pandarus's face. Pandarus falls dead. Aeneas jumps down to protect the body like a lion. Diomedes picks up a huge stone, one that two men of today could not lift, and smashes Aeneas's hip. Aeneas falls to his knees, and darkness covers his eyes.

Aeneas would have died, but his mother Aphrodite throws her white arms around him and covers him with her robe. As she carries him away, Sthenelus takes Aeneas's horses as Diomedes ordered. Diomedes chases the goddess. He knows she is not a warrior goddess. He stabs her hand near the wrist, and her divine blood, called ichor, flows out. She screams and drops her son. Apollo catches Aeneas and hides him in a cloud. Diomedes shouts after the goddess that she should leave war alone and go back to tricking weak women.

The messenger goddess Iris leads Aphrodite away. Aphrodite borrows the chariot of her brother Ares and flies to Olympus. There her mother Dione comforts her. Dione tells stories of other gods who suffered at the hands of mortals. She also warns that a man who fights the gods will not live long or see his children again. She heals the wound. Hera and Athena laugh. Athena jokes that Aphrodite must have scratched her hand on a brooch pin while persuading some Greek woman to run away with a Trojan. Zeus smiles and tells Aphrodite gently that war is not her work. She should leave fighting to Ares and Athena and keep to matters of love.

Back on earth, Diomedes attacks Aeneas again, even though Apollo is protecting him. Three times he charges, and three times Apollo pushes back his shield. On the fourth charge, Apollo shouts at him in a terrible voice. Men, he says, cannot match themselves against the gods. Diomedes steps back. Apollo carries Aeneas to his temple in Troy, where goddesses heal him. He leaves on the battlefield a phantom, a false image that looks like Aeneas, and men fight over it.

Apollo then asks Ares to return and drive Diomedes out of the battle. Ares, disguised as a Thracian leader, encourages the Trojans. Sarpedon, the leader of the Lycians and a son of Zeus, scolds Hector. He says the Trojan princes are hiding while the allies, who have come from far away and have nothing to gain, do the hard fighting. Hector feels the sting of these words. He leaps down and rallies his men. Apollo sends Aeneas back, fully healed, and his friends are amazed to see him.

The battle swings back and forth. Aeneas kills two Greek brothers, twins. Menelaus comes forward to fight him. Ares secretly hopes Menelaus will be killed. But Antilochus, the young son of Nestor, stands beside Menelaus, and Aeneas pulls back. Then Hector charges, with Ares himself fighting beside him. Diomedes sees the god and is afraid. He tells the Greeks to retreat slowly, facing the enemy, because they cannot fight a god. Hector kills several Greeks.

In another part of the field, Tlepolemus, a son of the hero Heracles, meets Sarpedon. Both are grandsons or sons of Zeus. Tlepolemus mocks Sarpedon and boasts about his father, who once destroyed Troy. Sarpedon answers that Heracles destroyed Troy only because its king cheated him. They throw their spears at the same moment. Sarpedon's spear goes through the throat of Tlepolemus and kills him. Tlepolemus's spear wounds Sarpedon badly in the thigh, but Zeus saves his son for now. Odysseus wants to chase Sarpedon, but fate does not allow it, so he kills many Lycians instead. Sarpedon begs Hector not to let him fall into Greek hands. He wants at least to die inside Troy. His friends pull the spear from his leg, and he faints, but the cool north wind revives him.

Hera now sees the Greeks dying under Ares and Hector. She and Athena prepare for war. Hera harnesses her golden chariot. Athena puts on her armor and her aegis, the terrible shield-cloak decorated with Fear, Strife, and the head of the monster Gorgon. They drive out of the gates of heaven. Hera asks Zeus for permission to drive Ares out of the battle. Zeus tells her to send Athena against him, since she has punished him often before.

The goddesses land near Troy. Hera, in the shape of a man with a voice as loud as fifty men, shouts at the Greeks and calls them cowards. Athena finds Diomedes resting and wiping blood from his wound. She teases him and says he is not the man his father was. Diomedes explains that he is only obeying her order not to fight the gods. Athena now tells him to attack Ares directly. She pushes Sthenelus out of the chariot, takes the reins herself, and puts on the helmet of Hades, which makes her invisible. Ares throws his spear at Diomedes, but Athena turns it aside. Then Diomedes throws, and Athena drives his spear into Ares's belly.

Ares roars like ten thousand men. Both armies are terrified. He flies up to Olympus like a dark cloud and complains to Zeus. He shows his wound and says Zeus lets his daughter Athena do whatever she likes. Zeus has no sympathy. He says Ares is the most hateful of all the gods, because he loves only war and fighting, just like his mother Hera. But because Ares is his own son, he has him healed. The healer of the gods cures him at once. Then Hera and Athena return to Olympus, having stopped the killing done by Ares.

This book shows a mortal man so filled with courage that he wounds two gods. It is a day of great glory for Diomedes. But the warnings from Dione and Apollo also remind us that human beings have limits.

## Book 6: Hector and Andromache

The gods have left the battlefield, and the fighting goes on across the plain between the two rivers of Troy. The Greeks are winning. Ajax kills the best of the Thracian warriors. Diomedes kills a rich and generous man named Axylus, who used to welcome every traveler into his house by the road. Now none of his guests is there to save him. Many other Trojans fall.

Then comes a hard moment. Menelaus catches a Trojan named Adrestus, whose horses have crashed into a bush. Adrestus holds Menelaus's knees and begs for his life. He promises that his rich father will pay a large ransom. Menelaus is ready to spare him and send him to the ships. But Agamemnon runs up and stops him. He says no Trojan should be spared, not even a baby still inside its mother. Menelaus pushes the man away, and Agamemnon kills him. Old Nestor shouts to the Greeks not to stop and collect armor from the dead, but to keep killing. This scene shows how cruel the war has become.

The Trojans are close to running back into the city. Then Helenus, a son of Priam who can read signs from the gods, gives advice to Hector and Aeneas. He tells them to hold the army in front of the gates. He also tells Hector to go into the city and ask their mother, Queen Hecuba, to gather the older women at the temple of Athena. There she should place her most beautiful robe on the knees of the goddess's statue. She should promise twelve young cows as a sacrifice, if Athena will keep Diomedes away from Troy. Helenus says the Trojans fear Diomedes even more than they ever feared Achilles. Hector rallies the men so well that the Greeks think a god has come down to help the Trojans. Then he leaves for the city.

While Hector is gone, two warriors meet between the armies: Diomedes and Glaucus, a leader of the Lycians, who are allies of Troy. Diomedes asks who Glaucus is. If he is a god, Diomedes will not fight him, because men who fight the gods do not live long. Glaucus answers with a famous image. The generations of men, he says, are like the leaves on trees. The wind blows the old leaves to the ground, and in spring new leaves grow. In the same way, one generation of people is born as another passes away.

Then Glaucus tells his family history. His grandfather was the hero Bellerophon. A queen fell in love with Bellerophon, and when he refused her, she lied to her husband and said he had attacked her. The king did not want to kill a guest himself, so he sent Bellerophon to Lycia with a secret letter asking the king of Lycia to kill him. The king of Lycia gave Bellerophon dangerous tasks. He had to kill the Chimera, a fire-breathing monster that was part lion, part goat, and part snake. He also had to fight fierce tribes and the Amazons, a race of warrior women. Then the king set a trap with his best warriors, but Bellerophon killed them all. At last the king saw that Bellerophon was protected by the gods, so he gave him his daughter and half his kingdom. Later Bellerophon fell out of favor with the gods and wandered alone. His son was the father of Glaucus, and he told Glaucus always to be the best and not shame his ancestors.

Diomedes is delighted. He plants his spear in the ground. He explains that his grandfather once welcomed Bellerophon as a guest, and the two men exchanged gifts. This makes Diomedes and Glaucus guest-friends, bound by an old tie of friendship and hospitality. They agree not to fight each other, since there are many other enemies to kill. They shake hands and exchange armor as a sign of friendship. But the poet adds a small joke: Zeus takes away Glaucus's good sense, because he gives golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze armor worth only nine. The scene shows that, even in war, old bonds of friendship can be stronger than the sides people fight on.

Meanwhile, Hector reaches the Scaean Gates. Women crowd around him, asking about their husbands, sons, and brothers. He tells them to pray. At Priam's great palace he meets his mother, Hecuba. She offers him wine to give him strength. He refuses. Wine might weaken him, he says, and he cannot pray to Zeus with hands dirty with blood. He gives her Helenus's instructions. Then he says he will go to find Paris. He wishes the earth would swallow his brother, who has brought so much harm to Troy.

Hecuba chooses her finest robe, one that glitters like a star, and takes it to Athena's temple with the other women. The priestess places it on the knees of the goddess and prays that Athena will break the spear of Diomedes. But Athena refuses the prayer.

Hector goes to the house of Paris. He finds his brother polishing his armor, while Helen sits with her women. Hector scolds him: the people are dying around the city, while Paris sits at home. Paris accepts the criticism. He says he was not angry with the Trojans, only sad, and that Helen was just now urging him to fight. He promises to follow soon. Helen speaks gently to Hector. She calls herself hateful. She wishes a storm had carried her away on the day she was born. Since the gods planned these evils, she wishes at least that she had a better husband, a man who would feel shame. She invites Hector to sit and rest. She says that she and Paris will be the subject of songs for future generations. Hector thanks her but will not sit. He says he must first go home to see his wife and his little son, because he does not know if he will ever come back to them.

At home, Hector does not find his wife. A servant tells him that Andromache has gone to the wall, because she heard the Trojans were losing. Hector hurries back toward the Scaean Gates. There Andromache comes running to meet him. With her is a nurse carrying their baby son. Hector calls the boy Scamandrius, but the people call him Astyanax, which means "lord of the city," because his father alone protects Troy.

Hector smiles at his son in silence. Andromache takes his hand and weeps. She tells him his courage will kill him. She has no family left. Achilles killed her father when he destroyed her home city of Thebe, and on the same day he killed her seven brothers. Her mother died soon after. So now, she says, Hector is her father, mother, brother, and husband. She begs him to stay on the wall and defend the weakest part, near a fig tree, and not make their son an orphan.

Hector answers that he has thought of all this. But he would feel deep shame before the Trojans if he hid from battle. He has learned to fight in the front line, to win glory for his father and himself. Then he says something very sad. He knows in his heart that one day Troy will fall, with Priam and all its people. But what hurts him most is not the death of his parents or his brothers. It is the thought of Andromache being carried away as a slave by some Greek. He imagines her weaving or carrying water in Greece, and someone pointing at her and saying, there is the wife of Hector. He says he would rather be dead and buried than hear her cry as she is taken away.

Hector reaches out for his son. But the baby screams and hides in his nurse's arms, frightened by his father's shining helmet and its horsehair crest. His mother and father laugh. Hector takes off the helmet and puts it on the ground. He kisses his son, lifts him up, and prays to Zeus. He asks that the boy will grow up to be a great warrior and king of Troy, and that one day people will say, "The son is far better than the father."

He gives the child back to Andromache, who smiles through her tears. Hector feels pity for her and touches her gently. He tells her not to grieve too much. Nobody can send him to death before his fated time, he says, but no one, brave or coward, can escape his fate once born. He tells her to go home and look after her work, because war is the business of men. Andromache walks home, turning back again and again to look at him. At home, she and her servants mourn for Hector as if he were already dead, because they believe he will never return.

This is one of the most famous scenes in the poem. It shows the war from the side of a family, and the price that wives and children pay. It also shows Hector's tragic choice between love for his family and his sense of honor.

As Hector leaves, Paris catches up with him. Paris runs through the city in his shining armor, laughing, like a horse that has broken free and gallops to the river. He apologizes for being late. Hector tells him kindly that he is a good fighter, but careless and lazy, and it hurts Hector to hear what the Trojans say about him. Together, the two brothers go out to battle.

## Book 7: Hector and Ajax

Hector and Paris come out of the gates together, and the Trojans are as glad to see them as tired sailors are to feel a good wind. Paris, Hector, and Glaucus, the Lycian leader, each kill a Greek.

Athena sees the Greeks dying and flies down from Olympus. Apollo, who wants the Trojans to win, comes out to meet her near a great oak tree. He suggests that they stop the general fighting for today. The Greeks can destroy Troy later, since the goddesses have decided it must fall. Athena agrees and asks how. Apollo says they should make Hector challenge one of the Greeks to a single fight. The Greeks will be too ashamed to refuse.

Helenus, the Trojan prophet, understands the plan of the gods. He tells Hector to make the challenge, and he adds that Hector is not fated to die today. Hector is glad. He makes both armies sit down. Athena and Apollo sit on the oak tree in the shape of vultures, watching. Hector speaks. He says Zeus did not let the oath work, so the war will go on until Troy falls or the Greeks are defeated at their ships. He invites the best Greek to fight him. He sets the terms. If he dies, the winner can take his armor but must send his body home for a proper funeral. If he wins, he will hang the Greek's armor in Apollo's temple, but he will return the body so the Greeks can bury it and build a mound by the sea. Then sailors in the future will see the mound and say a hero killed by Hector lies there. In this way, Hector says, his fame will never die.

The Greeks are silent. They are ashamed to refuse but afraid to accept. At last Menelaus jumps up. He insults the others and calls them women, and he starts to put on his armor. But Agamemnon holds him back. Hector is much stronger, he says, and even Achilles was careful about facing him. Menelaus listens and sits down.

Then old Nestor scolds the Greeks. He says that Peleus, the father of Achilles, would weep and wish to die if he heard that the Greek leaders were afraid of Hector. Nestor tells a story of his youth. Once, when he was the youngest man in the army, a giant warrior named Ereuthalion challenged everyone. All the others were afraid, but Nestor fought him and killed him with Athena's help. If only he were young now, he says, Hector would quickly find an opponent.

His words work. Nine men stand up. They are Agamemnon, Diomedes, the two men called Ajax, Idomeneus and his comrade Meriones, Eurypylus, Thoas, and Odysseus. They each mark a lot, a small token, and put it in Agamemnon's helmet. The soldiers pray that the lot of Ajax, Diomedes, or Agamemnon will come out. Nestor shakes the helmet, and the lot of the great Ajax jumps out. Ajax is happy. He is sure he will win.

Ajax puts on his armor and walks forward like the war god himself, with a grim smile. He carries his famous shield, as big as a wall. It is made of seven layers of ox-hide and one layer of bronze. The Trojans tremble, and even Hector's heart beats fast, but he cannot retreat now. Ajax tells Hector that the Greeks have other great fighters besides Achilles. Hector replies that he is no child. He knows all the skills of battle, and he will fight openly, not by tricks.

Hector throws first. His spear goes through six layers of the shield and stops in the seventh. Ajax's spear goes through Hector's shield and armor, but Hector twists and saves himself. They pull their spears free and attack each other like lions. Ajax wounds Hector in the neck. Hector picks up a stone and hits Ajax's shield. Ajax throws an even bigger stone, which crushes Hector's shield and knocks him flat on his back. Apollo lifts him up again. They are about to fight with swords when the heralds of both sides step between them. Night is falling, the heralds say, and both men are brave and loved by Zeus.

Ajax says that Hector, as the challenger, should be the one to agree. Hector praises Ajax's size, strength, and good sense. He agrees to stop for today. He suggests they exchange gifts, so people will say they fought hard but parted as friends. Hector gives Ajax a silver-studded sword, and Ajax gives Hector a purple belt. The Trojans are amazed and happy to see Hector come back alive. The Greeks lead Ajax proudly to Agamemnon, who gives a feast and honors Ajax with the best piece of meat. This duel shows respect between enemies, much like the meeting of Glaucus and Diomedes in Book 6.

After the meal, Nestor gives advice. Many Greeks have died, he says. They should stop fighting for a day to collect and burn the dead, so they can later take the bones home to the children. Near the funeral fire, they should build one large mound for all. Next to it they should build a high wall with strong gates to protect the ships and camp, and dig a deep ditch, or trench, in front of the wall. The leaders agree.

Inside Troy, the Trojans also meet, and there is much disagreement. Wise old Antenor says they should give Helen and her treasure back. The Trojans broke their oath, he says, and they will not succeed until they put this right. Paris refuses. He says he will never give up Helen, but he will return the treasure he took with her and add more of his own. King Priam does not decide between them. He tells everyone to eat and keep watch. In the morning the herald Idaeus should take Paris's offer to the Greeks, and also ask for a truce, which means a short stop in the fighting, so both sides can burn their dead.

In the morning, Idaeus delivers the message. He even says that he wishes Paris had died before all this. The Greeks are silent. Then Diomedes speaks. They should take nothing, he says, neither the treasure nor Helen herself, because even a child can see that Troy is about to fall. The Greeks shout in agreement. Agamemnon tells Idaeus that this is their answer. But he agrees to the truce for the dead, because no one should refuse the dead their funeral fire.

The next day, both sides gather their dead. The bodies are so damaged that they are hard to recognize. The men wash away the blood and weep. Priam forbids loud crying, so the Trojans burn their dead in silence. The Greeks do the same. Then, before dawn, the Greeks build the common mound, the wall with gates, and the ditch with sharp stakes in it.

The gods watch the work. Poseidon, the god of the sea and earthquakes, complains to Zeus. The Greeks have built this wall without making sacrifices to the gods. Its fame will spread, he says, and people will forget the wall of Troy that he and Apollo built long ago. Zeus tells him not to worry. When the Greeks have gone home, Poseidon can break the wall, throw it into the sea, and cover the beach with sand again.

That evening, ships arrive from the island of Lemnos with wine. The Greeks buy it with bronze, iron, hides, cattle, and captives, and they feast all night. The Trojans feast in their city too. But all night Zeus thunders as a sign of evil to come. The men are afraid. They pour their wine on the ground as an offering to Zeus before they dare to drink. Then they go to sleep.

## Book 8: Zeus Turns the Battle

At dawn, Zeus calls all the gods to a meeting on the highest peak of Olympus. He gives a strict order. No god or goddess may help either the Trojans or the Greeks. Anyone who disobeys will be beaten, or thrown down into Tartarus, a dark pit far below the world of the dead. To show his power, he describes a contest. If all the gods together hung on a golden chain from heaven and pulled, they could not drag him down. But he alone could pull them all up, together with the earth and the sea. The gods are frightened and silent. Only Athena speaks. She accepts his order, but she says she feels sorry for the Greeks and will still give them advice. Zeus smiles and tells her kindly that he does not really mean to be harsh with her.

Zeus then drives his golden chariot to Mount Ida, a mountain near Troy. From there he sits and watches the city and the Greek ships. Both armies arm themselves. The Trojans are fewer, but they fight for their wives and children. All morning the battle is equal. At midday, Zeus takes out his golden scales. He puts the fate of death for the Trojans in one pan, and the fate of death for the Greeks in the other. The Greek side sinks down to the ground. This means that today belongs to the Trojans. Zeus thunders and sends lightning onto the Greeks, and they are seized with fear.

The Greek leaders run, even Agamemnon, Idomeneus, and the two men called Ajax. Only old Nestor stays, and not by choice. Paris has shot one of his horses with an arrow in the head, and the dying animal has tangled his chariot. Nestor is cutting the horse free when Hector charges toward him. Diomedes sees the danger. He shouts to Odysseus to help, but Odysseus does not listen and runs on to the ships. So Diomedes goes alone to save the old man. He takes Nestor into his own chariot, which is pulled by the fast horses he captured from Aeneas. Nestor drives and Diomedes throws his spear at Hector. He misses Hector but kills his chariot driver.

Then Zeus throws a thunderbolt right in front of Diomedes's horses. Nestor drops the reins in fear. He tells Diomedes that Zeus is against them today, and no man can fight the will of Zeus. Diomedes agrees, but he is deeply hurt to think that Hector will boast that Diomedes ran from him. Nestor tells him that nobody would believe Hector, least of all the widows of the men Diomedes has killed. They turn and flee. Hector shouts insults after them. He calls Diomedes a girl and a coward. Three times Diomedes thinks of turning back to fight, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida.

Hector feels that Zeus is with him. He tells the Trojans that the new Greek wall is weak and his horses will jump the ditch easily. He asks for fire to burn the ships. He calls to his four horses and reminds them that Andromache has fed them well. He hopes to capture Nestor's golden shield and the armor Hephaestus made for Diomedes.

Hera is shaking with anger on her throne. She tries to persuade Poseidon to join her in helping the Greeks against Zeus's wishes. Poseidon refuses. He says no god should fight Zeus, who is far stronger.

Hector drives the Greeks back inside their wall, and they crowd between the ditch and the ships. He might have burned the ships then, but Hera puts courage into Agamemnon. Agamemnon climbs onto the ship of Odysseus, which is in the middle of the line, so that his voice can be heard from one end of the camp to the other. He shouts at the Greeks for their empty boasts. Then, in tears, he prays to Zeus. He reminds Zeus of all the sacrifices he made and asks at least to save the Greeks' lives. Zeus pities him and sends an eagle carrying a young deer. The eagle drops the deer beside Zeus's altar. The Greeks see this good sign and fight back with new courage.

Diomedes is the first to cross the ditch and kill a Trojan. Many leaders follow him. Among them is Teucer, the half-brother of the great Ajax and a skilled archer. He hides behind Ajax's huge shield. When Ajax lifts the shield, Teucer looks out and shoots. Then he ducks back down, like a child running to its mother. He kills eight Trojans. Agamemnon praises him and promises him a great prize when Troy falls. Teucer says he does not need encouragement. He has hit eight men, but he cannot hit Hector, whom he calls a mad dog.

He shoots again at Hector, but misses, and kills Gorgythion, a son of Priam. The young man's head falls to the side like a poppy heavy with spring rain. Teucer shoots once more at Hector, but Apollo turns the arrow aside, and it kills Hector's new chariot driver. Hector gives the reins to his brother Cebriones. Then he jumps down, picks up a jagged stone, and throws it at Teucer just as he is drawing his bow. The stone hits him near the collarbone and breaks the string of his arm, and he drops the bow. Ajax runs to cover his brother with his shield, and friends carry Teucer to the ships.

Zeus again fills the Trojans with courage. Hector drives the Greeks back across the ditch to their ships, killing the men at the back as they run. His eyes burn like those of the war god.

On Olympus, Hera and Athena can bear it no longer. Athena is very angry at her father. She reminds Hera how often she saved Zeus's son Heracles, and complains that Zeus now listens to Thetis instead. The two goddesses arm themselves and drive out of the gates of heaven. But Zeus sees them from Ida. He sends Iris, his messenger, with a terrible warning. If they continue, he will lame their horses, throw them from their chariot, break it, and wound them with lightning so badly that they will need ten years to heal. Hera decides they must obey. They turn back and sit on their thrones, very angry.

When Zeus returns to Olympus, he mocks the two goddesses for sitting silently apart. Hera repeats that they only pity the Greeks. Then Zeus tells them what is coming. Tomorrow, he says, Hector will kill even more Greeks. He will not stop until Achilles rises up again, when they are fighting at the ships over the body of Patroclus. This is the first time the poem tells us clearly that Patroclus will die and that his death will bring Achilles back to battle. Zeus adds that he does not care how angry Hera is.

The sun sets. The Trojans are sorry to see night come, and the Greeks are very glad. Hector holds an assembly on the plain. He says that only darkness saved the Greek ships. He orders the Trojans to camp on the plain instead of going back into the city. They must bring food and wine, and light many fires all night, so the Greeks cannot escape by sea in secret. In the city, boys and old men should guard the walls, and the women should keep fires burning in their houses. Hector says that tomorrow he will meet Diomedes and see who is stronger. He is as sure of victory as he would be of becoming a god.

The Trojans cheer. They make sacrifices, but the gods do not accept them, because they hate Troy. That night a thousand campfires shine on the plain between the ships and the river, like stars on a clear night. Around each fire sit fifty men, and their horses wait beside the chariots for the dawn. The Greeks are now trapped behind their wall, and their situation is desperate.

## Book 9: The Embassy to Achilles

While the Trojans watch by their fires, the Greeks are full of fear. Agamemnon calls the army together quietly. He weeps like a stream running down a cliff. He repeats the words he used to test the army in Book 2, but this time he means them. Zeus has deceived him, he says, so they should sail home, because they will never take Troy.

For a long time nobody speaks. Then Diomedes answers boldly. He reminds Agamemnon that he called him a coward in front of everyone. Now he says that Zeus gave Agamemnon power and a royal staff, but not courage, which is the greatest power. If Agamemnon wants to go home, he can go. The rest of the Greeks will stay. Even if everyone leaves, he and his friend Sthenelus will fight on alone until Troy falls, because the gods brought them here. The Greeks cheer.

Nestor praises Diomedes as wise for his age, but says there is more to discuss. He tells the young men to post guards along the ditch, and he advises Agamemnon to hold a dinner for the senior leaders. Seven captains take a hundred young men each to guard the wall. After dinner, Nestor speaks openly. He says that Agamemnon insulted Achilles against Nestor's advice by taking Briseis, and dishonored a hero whom the gods honor. Now they must find a way to win him back, with gifts and kind words.

Agamemnon admits his mistake. He says he was blinded by passion. He offers a huge list of gifts. He will give seven new tripods, which are three-legged metal pots used as valuable prizes, and ten talents of gold, a talent being a large weight of precious metal. He will give twenty cauldrons, twelve prize-winning horses, and seven skilled women from the island of Lesbos. He will return Briseis, and swear that he has never slept with her. When Troy falls, Achilles may fill his ship with gold and bronze and choose twenty Trojan women. Back in Greece, Achilles may marry one of Agamemnon's daughters without paying a bride price, and receive seven rich cities. But Agamemnon ends with a proud demand. Achilles must yield, because Agamemnon is older and more royal.

Nestor chooses the messengers. Phoenix, an old man who helped raise Achilles, will lead. With him go Ajax, who is close to Achilles, and Odysseus, the best speaker, together with two heralds. Nestor looks especially at Odysseus as he urges them to succeed.

They walk along the shore and pray to Poseidon. They find Achilles in his tent, playing a beautiful lyre and singing about the famous deeds of heroes. Patroclus sits silently opposite him. Achilles jumps up and welcomes them warmly. Even in his anger, he says, they are the Greeks he loves most. He tells Patroclus to mix stronger wine, and together they prepare a meal of roasted meat.

After they eat, Odysseus speaks first. He describes the danger: the Trojans are camped by the ships, Zeus sends lightning on their side, and Hector rages like a madman and promises to burn the ships at dawn. He reminds Achilles of his father Peleus's advice to control his proud temper. Then he repeats Agamemnon's list of gifts, word for word. But he wisely leaves out the last part, where Agamemnon demanded that Achilles obey him. He adds that even if Achilles hates Agamemnon, he should pity the other Greeks, who would honor him like a god. And he could kill Hector, who now thinks no Greek can stand against him.

Achilles gives a long and powerful answer. He says he hates a man who says one thing and hides another in his heart, so he will speak plainly. He will not be persuaded. He says that fighting brings no reward: the coward and the brave man receive equal honor, and both die the same. He has captured twelve cities by sea and eleven by land, and given almost all the treasure to Agamemnon, who stayed behind at the ships. Other leaders kept their prizes. Only from him did Agamemnon take the woman he loved. He asks why the Greeks are fighting at all. Is it not for Helen? Are the sons of Atreus the only men who love their wives? He says he loved Briseis with all his heart, though she was a captive won by his spear.

Achilles hates Agamemnon's gifts. Even if Agamemnon offered twenty times more, all the wealth of Egyptian Thebes, or gifts as many as grains of sand, it would not change his mind until he had full revenge for the insult. He will not marry Agamemnon's daughter. His father will find him a wife at home. Then he says something deeper. Nothing is worth as much as his life. Cattle and horses can be taken in raids, but a man's life, once it leaves him, can never be won back. His mother Thetis has told him that he has two possible fates. If he stays and fights at Troy, he will die young, but his glory will last forever. If he goes home, his glory will be lost, but he will live a long life. He says he will sail home in the morning, and he advises the others to do the same, because Zeus is protecting Troy. Phoenix may stay and sail with him if he wishes.

Everyone is shocked into silence. Then old Phoenix begins to cry. He reminds Achilles that Peleus sent him to teach the boy how to speak well and how to act. He tells his own sad story. As a young man, he quarreled with his own father over a woman, and his father cursed him never to have children. Phoenix fled to Peleus, who welcomed him like a son. Since he had no children of his own, Phoenix made Achilles his son. He remembers holding him on his knees as a baby, cutting his food, and how the child spilled wine on his shirt.

Phoenix tells Achilles to conquer his pride. Even the gods can be moved by prayers. He describes Prayers as the lame, wrinkled daughters of Zeus. They follow behind Ruin, which runs fast and harms people. A man who respects these Prayers is helped by them, but if he rejects them, they ask Zeus to send ruin upon him. Then Phoenix tells an old story about a hero called Meleager. Meleager was angry with his mother and refused to fight to defend his city, even when the elders offered him rich land. He did not move until enemies were climbing the walls and setting fire to the city, and his wife described what happens to people when a city falls. Then he fought and saved the city, but by then he received none of the gifts. Phoenix warns Achilles not to make the same mistake. Take the gifts now, he says, and you will be honored like a god.

Achilles answers gently but firmly. He calls Phoenix father, but says he does not need that kind of honor. He already has honor from Zeus. He tells Phoenix not to weep for Agamemnon's sake, or Achilles might stop loving him. Phoenix should stay the night, and they will decide in the morning whether to leave.

Then Ajax speaks, not to Achilles at first but to Odysseus. He says they should go, because Achilles is cruel and cares nothing for his friends' love. Even a man whose brother or son was killed will accept a payment from the killer and make peace. But Achilles keeps his anger, all for one girl, when they offer him seven women and much more. Ajax asks him to respect his own home, since they are his guests and his friends.

Achilles admits that Ajax speaks well. But his heart still swells with anger when he remembers how Agamemnon treated him like a worthless outsider. Then he changes his answer a little. He will not fight until Hector reaches the tents and ships of the Myrmidons, his own men, and sets them on fire. There, he says, he will stop Hector. This is a small step back from his threat to sail home.

The messengers return. Odysseus reports that Achilles refused the gifts and threatens to sail home in the morning. He does not mention Achilles's later words to Ajax. Everyone sits in silence. Then Diomedes says Agamemnon should never have begged Achilles. He has only made him prouder. Achilles will fight when he feels like it, or when a god moves him. Now they should rest, and in the morning Agamemnon should lead the army bravely in the front line. The leaders agree and go to sleep.

This book is the heart of the poem's debate about honor. Agamemnon tries to buy back Achilles's friendship with things. But Achilles begins to question the whole system of prizes and glory, and even the value of fighting itself.

## Book 10: The Night Raid

That same night, the other Greek leaders sleep, but Agamemnon cannot. He sighs again and again. When he looks toward the plain, he sees the many Trojan fires and hears their flutes and voices. When he looks at his own camp, he tears his hair in despair. He decides to visit old Nestor to find some plan. He puts on a lion skin and takes his spear.

Menelaus cannot sleep either. He feels responsible, because the Greeks came all this way for his sake. He goes to his brother, who is already dressing. Agamemnon says Zeus now favors Hector. He has never seen one man do so much damage in a single day, and Hector is not even the son of a god. He sends Menelaus to wake Ajax and Idomeneus. He tells him to be polite to everyone and not proud, because they must all share the work.

Agamemnon finds Nestor sleeping beside his armor. The old man wakes and asks who is walking around in the dark. Agamemnon explains his fear. Nestor comforts him. He says Zeus will not give Hector everything he hopes for, especially if Achilles lets go of his anger. Nestor criticizes Menelaus for sleeping, but Agamemnon defends his brother: Menelaus is often slow only because he waits for Agamemnon to lead, but tonight he was awake first.

Nestor wakes Odysseus, then finds Diomedes sleeping on an ox hide outside his tent, with his men around him. Nestor nudges him with his foot. Diomedes jumps up and jokes that the old man never rests. Nestor answers that the Greeks are standing on the edge of a razor, between life and death. Diomedes goes to wake the others. The leaders then check the guards and find them all awake and alert, like sheepdogs that hear a wild animal in the forest.

The leaders cross the ditch and hold a meeting in an open space on the plain. Nestor asks if anyone is brave enough to go into the Trojan camp as a spy, to learn whether the Trojans will stay by the ships or go back to the city. Such a man would win great fame and many gifts. Diomedes offers to go, but he wants a companion. Two men together, he says, see more than one. Many volunteers stand up. Agamemnon tells Diomedes to choose the best man, not the man of highest birth. He says this because he is afraid for his brother Menelaus. Diomedes chooses Odysseus, who is clever, brave, and loved by Athena. Odysseus modestly tells him not to praise him, and notes that most of the night is already gone.

They borrow weapons. Odysseus wears a famous leather helmet covered with boar's teeth, which has passed through the hands of many owners. Athena sends a heron, a water bird, flying on their right as a good sign. They cannot see it in the dark, but they hear its cry. Both men pray to Athena, and she hears them. They move forward like two lions in the night, among the dead bodies and weapons.

Meanwhile, Hector also cannot rest. He asks for a volunteer to spy on the Greek ships, to learn whether they are still guarded or planning to flee. He promises the best chariot and horses in the Greek camp as a reward. A Trojan named Dolon volunteers. He is not handsome, but he is a fast runner and rich, the only son in a family with five sisters. He asks Hector to swear that he will receive the horses of Achilles himself. Hector swears, but the poet tells us that the oath is empty. Dolon puts on a wolf skin and sets out. He will never return to give his report.

Odysseus sees Dolon coming. He and Diomedes lie down among the dead and let him pass. Then they run after him and cut him off from the Trojan camp, like two hunting dogs chasing a deer. When Dolon is near the Greek guards, Diomedes throws his spear just over his shoulder, on purpose, and orders him to stop. Dolon stands still, shaking and pale, his teeth chattering. He begs them to take him alive, promising that his father will pay a rich ransom.

Odysseus tells him not to think about death, and asks why he is out at night. Dolon admits that Hector sent him and promised him the horses of Achilles. Odysseus smiles and says those horses can only be controlled by Achilles, the son of a goddess. Then he questions Dolon about the Trojan camp. Dolon tells everything. Hector and the leaders are meeting by an old tomb. The Trojans keep watch, but the allies sleep, because their wives and children are not in danger. He describes where each group of allies is camped. Then he points out the best target. The Thracians have just arrived and sleep at the far end of the camp. Their king, Rhesus, has the most beautiful horses Dolon has ever seen, whiter than snow and faster than the wind, and golden armor fit for a god.

Dolon asks to be tied up or taken to the ships until they check his story. But Diomedes says that if they let him go, he will only come back to spy again. As Dolon reaches up to beg him, Diomedes cuts off his head with his sword. The head falls in the dust while Dolon is still speaking. Odysseus offers Dolon's wolf skin, cap, bow, and spear to Athena, and hangs them on a bush, marking the place so they can find it later.

The two men reach the Thracian camp. The soldiers are asleep, exhausted, with their armor neatly laid out and their horses beside them. Rhesus sleeps in the middle. Odysseus tells Diomedes to kill the men while he takes the horses. Athena fills Diomedes with strength, and he moves through the sleeping soldiers like a lion among sheep with no shepherd. He kills twelve men. Odysseus drags each body aside, so that the horses will not be frightened when they walk over the dead. Then Diomedes kills King Rhesus himself, the thirteenth, who is having a bad dream sent by Athena. Odysseus unties the horses and drives them out, hitting them with his bow because he forgot to take the whip. He whistles to Diomedes.

Diomedes wonders whether to do more. Should he take the chariot with the golden armor, or kill more Thracians? But Athena warns him to go back now, before another god wakes the Trojans. He jumps onto one of the horses, and they ride to the ships. Just then Apollo, angry at Athena's help, wakes a Thracian leader, a relative of Rhesus. The man sees the empty space where the horses were and his friends dying. He cries out, and the whole Trojan camp wakes in confusion.

On the way back, Odysseus and Diomedes stop to pick up Dolon's gear. Nestor hears the sound of hooves and hopes it is them, but fears the worst. When they arrive, everyone greets them with joy. Nestor admires the horses, which shine like sunbeams, and wonders if a god gave them. Odysseus laughs and tells the story: Diomedes killed the Thracian king and twelve of his men, and they also killed a Trojan spy. They tie the horses in Diomedes's stable and hang Dolon's gear on Odysseus's ship as an offering to Athena. Then they wash in the sea, take a bath, and sit down to eat and pour wine for Athena.

This book is different from the rest of the poem. It is a night adventure of spying and tricks, not an open battle. It gives the Greeks a small victory after the terrible day before, and it shows the cunning of Odysseus and the hard, ruthless side of war.

## Book 11: The Greek Leaders Are Wounded

At dawn, Zeus sends Strife, the goddess of conflict, to the Greek ships. She stands on the ship of Odysseus in the middle of the camp and gives a loud, sharp cry. It fills the Greeks with such courage that they would rather fight than sail home.

Agamemnon puts on his splendid armor. The poet describes it in detail. His breastplate, a gift from the king of Cyprus, is decorated with blue snakes that shine like rainbows. His great shield shows the face of the Gorgon, the monster whose look causes terror. Hera and Athena thunder to honor him. That morning, Zeus sends a shower of bloody dew as a sign that many men will die.

The Trojans gather on the high ground around Hector, Aeneas, a wise Trojan named Polydamas, and the sons of old Antenor. Hector appears now in the front, now at the back, like a dangerous star that shines through the clouds. The two armies meet like reapers cutting a field. Strife is happy, because she is the only god on the battlefield. The other gods stay at home, unhappy that Zeus wants the Trojans to win. Zeus sits apart and watches.

Around midday, the Greeks break through, led by Agamemnon. This is his greatest moment in the poem. He kills many Trojans. He kills two sons of Priam whom Achilles had once captured and released for ransom. Then two brothers fall from their chariot and beg Agamemnon to take them alive for a rich ransom. But their father is the Trojan who was bribed by Paris to keep Helen in Troy, and who once proposed killing Menelaus and Odysseus when they came as messengers. So Agamemnon shows no mercy. He kills one and cuts off the other's head and hands. He drives the Trojans across the plain like a forest fire, all the way to the Scaean Gates. Many horses run with empty chariots, because their drivers lie dead, more useful to vultures than to their wives.

Zeus now sends Iris to Hector with a message. Hector must stay back while Agamemnon is fighting. But when Agamemnon is wounded and leaves the battle, Zeus will give Hector strength to kill until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down.

A young Trojan named Iphidamas, a son of Antenor, faces Agamemnon. He had just married and left his wife to come to the war. His spear strikes Agamemnon's belt but bends on the silver. Agamemnon kills him with his sword. The poet feels pity for him, dying far from the wife he never had time to enjoy. His elder brother Coon, full of grief, stabs Agamemnon through the arm. Agamemnon kills Coon too. He keeps fighting while the wound is warm, but when the blood stops, the pain becomes as sharp as a woman's labor pains. He climbs into his chariot and is driven back to the ships, telling the other leaders to defend them.

Now Hector attacks, as Zeus promised. He kills nine Greek leaders and many ordinary soldiers, like a storm wind beating the sea. The Greeks are about to run, but Odysseus calls to Diomedes to stand with him. Diomedes agrees, though he says Zeus is against them. Together they fight like two wild boars and give the Greeks time to breathe. When Hector charges them, Diomedes throws his spear and hits Hector's helmet. The helmet, a gift from Apollo, saves him, but Hector falls to his knees and his eyes go dark. He recovers and escapes in his chariot. Diomedes calls him a dog and says Apollo has saved him again.

Then Paris, hiding behind a stone monument on the plain, shoots an arrow through Diomedes's foot and pins it to the ground. Paris laughs and boasts. Diomedes scorns him. He says that without his bow Paris is nothing, and his arrow was no worse than a scratch from a child. Odysseus stands over Diomedes while he pulls out the arrow, but the pain is great. Diomedes drives back to the ships.

Odysseus is now alone. He speaks to himself. It would be bad to run, but worse to be caught alone. Then he decides that a true warrior must stand firm, whether he wounds or is wounded. The Trojans surround him like hunters around a wild boar. He kills several of them. Then a Trojan named Socus wounds him in the side, but Athena keeps the spear from going deep. Odysseus kills Socus as he turns to run and boasts that his parents will not even close his eyes; vultures will eat him. When the Trojans see Odysseus bleeding, they rush at him. He shouts three times for help.

Menelaus hears him and brings Ajax. The Trojans are around Odysseus like jackals around a wounded deer, until a lion arrives. Ajax comes with his great shield, and the Trojans scatter. Menelaus leads Odysseus away to his chariot. Ajax charges across the plain like a flooding river, killing many Trojans.

On the other side of the field, Paris wounds Machaon, the healer, with an arrow. Idomeneus tells Nestor to carry Machaon to the ships quickly, because a doctor is worth many other men. Hector's driver, Cebriones, sees Ajax causing chaos and drives Hector there. But Hector avoids Ajax himself. Then Zeus fills Ajax with fear. Ajax retreats slowly, turning sometimes to fight, like a lion driven from a farm, or like a stubborn donkey that boys beat with sticks while it eats their grain. Spears stick in his shield. Eurypylus, another Greek leader, comes to help him, but Paris shoots Eurypylus in the thigh. Eurypylus calls the Greeks to protect Ajax, and they form a wall of shields around him.

Meanwhile, Achilles is standing on the stern of his ship, watching the battle. He sees Nestor's chariot carrying a wounded man. He calls Patroclus out of the tent. The poet says this is the beginning of the evil that will come to Patroclus. Achilles says that now the Greeks will soon come begging at his knees. He sends Patroclus to find out who the wounded man is. He thinks it is Machaon, but the horses went by too fast to see.

In Nestor's tent, a servant woman prepares a drink of wine, cheese, and barley for Nestor and Machaon in Nestor's large golden cup. Patroclus arrives. He refuses to sit down. He says he must quickly report to Achilles, who is a difficult man and quick to blame. Nestor answers bitterly. Why does Achilles care about one wounded man? Diomedes, Odysseus, Agamemnon, Eurypylus, and Machaon are all wounded, but Achilles feels no pity. Will he wait until the ships are burning?

Then Nestor tells a long story from his youth. When he was a young man, the people of Pylos fought their neighbors, the Epeans of Elis, over stolen cattle. Nestor led a raid and brought back huge herds. When the Epeans attacked in revenge, his father hid Nestor's horses because he thought him too young. But Nestor fought on foot, killed the enemy's best horseman, took his chariot, and won a great victory. He was honored by all. That, he says, is the kind of man he was, but Achilles keeps his courage only for himself.

Finally, Nestor reminds Patroclus of the day he and Odysseus came to recruit Achilles and Patroclus for the war. Patroclus's father told him that Achilles was nobler and stronger, but Patroclus was older, so he should give Achilles wise advice. Now, Nestor says, Patroclus must use that advice. If Achilles still refuses to fight because of some prophecy, he should at least send Patroclus with the Myrmidons, wearing Achilles's own armor. The Trojans might think Achilles has returned and pull back, giving the tired Greeks time to rest. This idea will shape the rest of the story.

Patroclus is deeply moved and runs back toward Achilles. But on the way he meets Eurypylus, limping, sweating, and bleeding from the arrow in his thigh. Eurypylus says the Greeks have no hope left. He begs Patroclus to treat his wound, since the doctors are wounded or busy. Patroclus feels pity. Although he is carrying an urgent message, he takes Eurypylus to his tent. He cuts out the arrow, washes the wound with warm water, and puts on a bitter herb that stops the pain and the bleeding. Patroclus's kindness here contrasts with Achilles's hardness.

## Book 12: The Battle at the Wall

While Patroclus is caring for the wounded Eurypylus, the battle rages at the Greek wall. The poet first looks into the future. The Greeks built the wall without making sacrifices to the gods, so it will not last. After Troy has fallen and the Greeks have sailed home, Poseidon and Apollo will turn all the rivers of Mount Ida against it. For nine days the rivers will flood it while Zeus sends rain. Poseidon will break the stones and timbers with his trident, wash them into the sea, and cover the place with sand. No trace will remain. This short look ahead reminds us that even the great works of heroes do not last.

But for now, the wall still stands, and the Greeks are trapped behind it. Hector moves among the Trojans like a lion or wild boar surrounded by hunters. He urges them to cross the ditch. But the horses stop at the edge and neigh with fear. The ditch is wide, its sides are steep, and sharp stakes stand inside it.

Polydamas, a wise Trojan friend of Hector, gives advice. It would be madness, he says, to drive chariots into the ditch. It is narrow and full of stakes. If the Greeks fight back, the Trojans would be trapped and no one would get home alive. He suggests leaving the horses with the drivers and attacking on foot. Hector likes this plan. He jumps down, and the Trojans form five groups. Hector and Polydamas lead the bravest. Paris leads another group. Two sons of Priam lead a third, together with a warrior named Asius. Aeneas leads the fourth. Sarpedon, the son of Zeus, leads the allies, with Glaucus at his side.

Only Asius refuses to leave his chariot. He drives toward an open gate on the left, where Greeks running from the plain are being let in. His men follow, shouting, sure that the Greeks will break. But two strong Greek warriors stand in front of the gate like two tall oak trees on a mountain. They fight like wild boars, while men on the wall throw stones down as thick as snow. Asius cries out that Zeus is a liar, because these two men will not move, like wasps defending their nest. But Zeus wants to give the glory to Hector, not to Asius. The two Greeks kill many of the attackers. The poet tells us that Asius himself will die soon, killed by Idomeneus.

Meanwhile, Hector's men stop at the ditch, because they see a sign from the gods. An eagle flies along the left side of the army, carrying a huge red snake that is still alive. The snake twists and bites the eagle on the neck. In pain, the eagle drops the snake among the Trojans and flies away with a cry.

Polydamas explains the sign. He says the eagle did not manage to bring its prey home to its young. In the same way, even if the Trojans break the wall, they will not return home in good order. Many will die at the ships. He complains that Hector always criticizes him, even when he speaks wisely.

Hector is angry. He says Polydamas wants him to forget the promise Zeus made him, and to trust birds instead. He does not care whether birds fly to the right or the left. Then he says a famous line: there is only one good sign, to fight for your country. He accuses Polydamas of cowardice and threatens to kill him if he holds others back from the fight. Then he leads the charge. Hector's choice here shows his courage, but also his growing overconfidence. Polydamas will turn out to be right.

Zeus sends a strong wind from Mount Ida that blows dust into the eyes of the Greeks. The Trojans tear down the defenses on top of the wall and pull out the supports. But the Greeks hold the wall with their shields and throw weapons down. The two men called Ajax move along the wall, encouraging everyone. They praise some men and scold others. They say that not all men are equal in battle, but now there is work for everyone. Stones fly from both sides as thick as snow in winter.

Then Zeus sends his son Sarpedon against the wall like a hungry lion that attacks a well-guarded farm. Before attacking, Sarpedon speaks to his friend Glaucus. This is one of the most famous speeches in the poem about the duty of a hero. Why, he asks, are they honored in their home of Lycia, with the best seats at feasts, the best food, full cups, and large lands? It is because they must fight in the front line. Then their people will say that their princes deserve their wealth because they fight bravely. He continues: if they could escape old age and death forever by avoiding this battle, he would not fight, and he would not send Glaucus to fight. But death waits for all men in ten thousand forms, and no one can escape it. So let them go forward, and either win glory or give glory to another. Here Sarpedon explains the hero's code: because life is short and death is certain, glory is the only thing that lasts.

Sarpedon and Glaucus lead the Lycians against the part of the wall defended by Menestheus, the leader of the Athenians. Menestheus is frightened. The noise is so loud that he cannot shout for help, so he sends a messenger to ask for Ajax and Teucer, the archer. The great Ajax tells the other Ajax to hold their position, and goes with Teucer.

They arrive just in time. Ajax throws a huge stone, which a young man of today could hardly lift with both hands, and crushes the head of one of Sarpedon's companions. Teucer shoots Glaucus in the arm. Glaucus quietly jumps down from the wall so that the Greeks will not see he is wounded and laugh at him. Sarpedon is sad to see his friend go but keeps fighting. He kills a Greek, then grabs the top of the wall with his hands and pulls a whole section down, making an opening. Teucer's arrow hits the strap of his shield, and Ajax's spear pushes him back, but Zeus saves his son for now. Sarpedon calls to the Lycians to follow him. He cannot break through alone, he says. The more men there are, the better.

The battle becomes perfectly balanced. Neither side can push the other back, like two farmers arguing over a strip of land at the edge of a field. Blood covers the wall. The poet compares the battle to an honest working woman who weighs wool carefully on her scales to earn a little money for her children. The two sides are balanced as exactly as her scales.

Finally, Zeus gives the glory to Hector. Hector shouts to the Trojans to break the wall and throw fire on the ships. He picks up a huge stone, sharp at one end, that two strong men could hardly lift onto a cart. Zeus makes it light for him, and he carries it as easily as a shepherd carries a fleece. He walks up to the great double gate, plants his feet, and throws the stone into the middle. The hinges break, the bars give way, and the doors fly open. Hector leaps through the gate with two spears in his hands. His face is as dark as night, his armor flashes, and his eyes burn like fire. Only a god could stop him now. He calls to the Trojans. Some climb over the wall, others pour through the gate. The Greeks run in panic toward their ships.

## Book 13: Poseidon Helps the Greeks

Zeus has brought Hector and the Trojans to the Greek ships. Now he turns his eyes away from Troy and looks at faraway peoples in the north. He does not think any god will dare to disobey his order and join the battle.

But Poseidon, the god of the sea, is watching from the top of a mountain on the island of Samothrace. He feels sorry for the Greeks and is very angry with Zeus. He comes down the mountain in four great steps, and the hills shake under his feet. He drives his golden chariot over the waves. Sea creatures play around him, and the sea opens a path for him. He leaves his horses in an underwater cave and goes secretly to the Greek army.

Poseidon takes the shape of Calchas, the Greek prophet. He speaks first to the two men called Ajax. He tells them they can save the Greeks if they stand firm against Hector, who fights like a pillar of fire. He touches them with his staff and fills them with strength. Then he flies off like a hawk. The smaller Ajax, the son of Oileus, realizes that it was a god, because he recognized him from behind by his legs and feet as he left. Both men feel new power in their hands and legs.

Poseidon then goes to the Greeks resting at the back, who are weeping because the Trojans have crossed the wall. He scolds them. He admits that Agamemnon caused this disaster by insulting Achilles, but he says that is no reason to stop fighting. The hearts of brave men heal quickly. The Greeks form a solid wall around the two Ajaxes, shield against shield, helmet against helmet, man against man.

Hector charges like a huge rock rolling down a mountain. But when he reaches the tight Greek line, he is stopped and pushed back. He calls to the Trojans to stand firm. The fighting continues. Teucer kills a Trojan who is married to a daughter of Priam. Hector throws at Teucer but kills a grandson of Poseidon instead. In anger, the smaller Ajax cuts off the head of the dead Trojan and throws it like a ball into the dust at Hector's feet.

Poseidon is furious that his grandson has died. Now in the shape of another Greek leader, he finds Idomeneus, the king of Crete, and urges him into battle. Idomeneus arms himself and meets his comrade Meriones, who is coming back to fetch a new spear because he broke his own. The two men talk. Idomeneus says he has many spears taken from Trojans he has killed. Meriones says he too has fought bravely. Idomeneus agrees. He describes how you can tell a brave man from a coward in an ambush: the coward changes color, cannot sit still, and his teeth chatter, but the brave man stays calm and is eager to fight. Meriones, he says, would never be wounded in the back. They decide to go to the left side of the battle, because the center is well defended by the two Ajaxes and Teucer.

The poet now explains what the two great gods are doing. Zeus wants the Trojans to win for now, to honor Achilles and Thetis, but he does not want to destroy the Greek army completely. Poseidon wants to help the Greeks, but he is younger than Zeus and less powerful, so he helps them in secret, in human form. Together, the two brothers have tied a knot of war that no one can untie.

Idomeneus, though his hair is already gray, fights wonderfully. He kills Othryoneus, a newcomer who had been promised Priam's daughter Cassandra if he could drive the Greeks away. Idomeneus mocks the dead man. He jokes that the Greeks will give him one of Agamemnon's daughters instead, if he helps them destroy Troy. Then he kills Asius, the Trojan who drove his chariot to the wall in Book 12. Asius falls like a tree cut for a ship. Antilochus, the son of Nestor, kills Asius's driver and takes his horses.

Deiphobus, a son of Priam, throws at Idomeneus but kills another Greek. Then Idomeneus kills a Trojan named Alcathous, the brother-in-law of Aeneas, while Poseidon holds the man still. Idomeneus boasts. He is descended from Zeus, he says, through King Minos of Crete. Deiphobus goes to get help from Aeneas. Aeneas is standing at the back. The poet tells us he is unhappy because Priam does not give him the honor he deserves. Deiphobus reminds him that Alcathous raised him as a child. Aeneas comes to fight.

Idomeneus does not run. He calls his friends to help him. He admits he is afraid of Aeneas, who is young and strong, while he himself is older. A fierce fight breaks out over the body. Aeneas misses Idomeneus. Idomeneus kills another Trojan, but he is tired now and retreats slowly. Deiphobus throws at him again and kills Ascalaphus, a son of the war god Ares. Ares does not know his son has died, because he is sitting on Olympus, obeying Zeus's order. Meriones wounds Deiphobus in the arm, and his brother carries him back to the city.

The fighting goes on. Antilochus fights bravely, and Poseidon protects him. Meriones kills the son of Asius. Helenus, the Trojan prophet, kills a Greek. Menelaus attacks Helenus. Helenus's arrow bounces off Menelaus's armor, and Menelaus's spear goes through Helenus's hand into his bow. Then a Trojan named Pisander attacks Menelaus with an axe. Menelaus kills him with his sword.

Standing over the body, Menelaus gives an angry speech. He says the Trojans stole his wife and his treasure while they were his guests. They did not fear Zeus, who protects guests and hosts. One day Zeus will destroy their city. Then he wonders why Zeus favors such proud people. Men grow tired of everything, he says, even sleep, love, sweet songs, and dancing. But the Trojans never grow tired of war.

More men fall. Meriones kills a young Paphlagonian prince, and his father follows the body back to Troy, weeping. Paris kills a Greek named Euchenor to avenge his friend. Euchenor's father, a prophet, had told him he would either die of a terrible disease at home or be killed at Troy. He chose to die in battle.

Hector does not know that the Trojans are losing on the left. He is still fighting where he broke through the wall. There the two men called Ajax stand side by side like two oxen pulling a plow. Behind them, the Locrians, the lightly armed men of the smaller Ajax, shoot arrows and stones, and the Trojans begin to lose courage.

Polydamas, Hector's wise friend, speaks to him again. He says that Hector is a great fighter, but the gods give different gifts to different people: to one, war; to another, music or dancing; and to another, good judgment. He advises Hector to call the leaders together and decide whether to attack the ships or retreat while they still can. He also warns that a man who never tires of battle, Achilles, is waiting at the ships and will not stay out much longer. Hector agrees this time.

Hector looks for his brothers and allies, but many are dead or wounded. He finds Paris and scolds him, asking where the others are. Paris answers calmly that he has been fighting the whole time. The others are dead or wounded. He promises to follow Hector as far as his strength allows. Together they lead the Trojans forward like a great storm wave.

Ajax challenges Hector. He says the Greeks are good fighters, only Zeus is against them, and soon Hector will be running back to his city. A bird flies on Ajax's right, a good sign, and the Greeks cheer. Hector answers with a proud boast. He is as sure that this day will destroy the Greeks as he would be sure of being a god, and Ajax will feed the dogs and birds of Troy. Both armies shout, and the noise rises to heaven.

## Book 14: Hera Tricks Zeus

Old Nestor is still in his tent with the wounded healer Machaon. He hears the battle noise growing louder. He tells Machaon to rest and have a warm bath, and goes out to see what is happening. He sees the Greek wall broken and the Greeks running in panic. For a moment he cannot decide, like the sea waiting for the wind, whether to join the fighting or look for Agamemnon. He decides to find Agamemnon.

He meets the three wounded kings, Agamemnon, Diomedes, and Odysseus, who are walking from their ships to look at the battle, leaning on their spears. Their ships are pulled up on the shore far from the fighting. Agamemnon is frightened. He fears Hector's boast will come true, that he will burn the ships and kill them all. Nestor agrees that things are very bad. The wall has fallen, and the Greeks are dying everywhere. But he says wounded men cannot fight.

Agamemnon now proposes, for the third time, that they escape. They should drag the ships nearest the sea into the water and anchor them, and at night launch the rest. It is better, he says, to run and live than to be caught and killed.

Odysseus looks at him angrily. He says Agamemnon should command some worthless army, not men like them, whom Zeus has made to fight from youth to old age until they die. He tells him to be quiet before the soldiers hear. If the men see the ships being pulled into the sea during the battle, they will stop fighting and only look back at the ships. Agamemnon's plan would destroy them. Agamemnon is hurt by these words. He says he is not forcing anyone, and he would welcome a better idea.

Diomedes offers one. He is the youngest, but he reminds them that he comes from a noble family, so they should not ignore him. He says the wounded kings should go to the battle and stay just out of reach of the spears, so they will not be wounded again. From there, they can encourage the others. They agree. On the way, Poseidon comes to them in the shape of an old man. He takes Agamemnon's hand. He says Achilles must be happy to see the Greeks dying, because he has no pity, but the gods are not so angry with Agamemnon. Soon, he says, the Trojans will run back to their city. Then Poseidon gives a great battle cry, as loud as ten thousand men, and the Greeks are filled with courage.

On Olympus, Hera sees her brother Poseidon helping the Greeks and is glad. Then she looks at Zeus sitting on Mount Ida and hates him. She thinks of a plan to deceive him. She will make herself beautiful and go to Ida. If Zeus desires her and makes love to her, she can make him fall into a deep sleep.

She goes to her private room, which her son Hephaestus built with a secret lock. She washes with ambrosia, the food and perfume of the gods, rubs her skin with sweet oil, and braids her shining hair. She puts on a beautiful robe made by Athena, golden pins, a belt with a hundred tassels, earrings, and a veil.

Then she calls Aphrodite and asks for help, though they are on opposite sides of the war. Hera tells a lie. She says she wants to visit two old gods, Ocean and his wife Tethys, who raised her. They have been fighting for a long time, and she wants to make peace between them. So she asks Aphrodite for her powers of love and desire. Aphrodite says she cannot refuse the wife of Zeus. She gives Hera her special embroidered band, which holds all her charms: love, desire, and sweet words that steal the wisdom even of wise people. Hera smiles and hides it in her dress.

Next Hera flies to the island of Lemnos to find Sleep, the brother of Death. She asks him to close Zeus's eyes after she lies with him. She offers him a golden chair made by Hephaestus. Sleep refuses. He is afraid. Once before, he helped Hera put Zeus to sleep so that she could cause trouble for Heracles, Zeus's son. When Zeus woke, he was furious and threw the gods around his house looking for Sleep. Sleep only escaped by running to Night, whom even Zeus respects. Hera answers that Zeus will not care as much about the Trojans as he did about his own son. Then she offers something better. She promises Sleep that he can marry Pasithea, one of the Graces, whom he has always loved. Sleep accepts, but makes her swear by the river Styx, the most serious oath of the gods. Hera swears.

They fly together to Mount Ida. Sleep hides in a tall pine tree in the shape of a bird. Hera goes up to the highest peak. As soon as Zeus sees her, he is filled with desire, as strong as when they first loved each other in secret, long ago. He asks why she has come without her chariot. She repeats her lie about visiting Ocean and Tethys, and says she did not want to leave without telling him.

Zeus says she can go another time. He says he has never desired any goddess or woman so much. Then, foolishly, he lists the many women and goddesses he has loved before, saying he never loved any of them as much as he loves her now. Hera pretends to be shy. She says that someone might see them on the open mountain, and suggests going to her room on Olympus. Zeus says he will cover them with a golden cloud so thick that even the sun cannot see through it. He takes her in his arms. The earth makes a soft bed of fresh grass and flowers under them, and a golden cloud covers them, dropping shining dew. Then Zeus falls asleep in her arms, overcome by love and sleep.

Sleep hurries to Poseidon and tells him that now is his chance. Zeus is asleep, and the Greeks can win for a short time. Poseidon leads the Greeks himself. He tells them to arm in the best way: the bravest men should take the biggest shields and helmets, and give their smaller ones to weaker men. The wounded kings help to organize the exchange. Then Poseidon marches in front, holding a terrible sword that flashes like lightning. Hector arranges the Trojans on the other side. The armies meet with a noise louder than the sea in a storm, a forest fire, or a howling wind.

Hector throws his spear at the great Ajax. It hits the place where the straps of his shield and sword cross on his chest, and does not wound him. As Hector moves back, Ajax picks up one of the large stones used to hold the ships in place. He hits Hector in the chest, near the neck. Hector spins like a top and falls like an oak tree struck by lightning. His spear drops from his hand.

The Greeks rush to drag him away, but the Trojan leaders, Polydamas, Aeneas, Sarpedon, Glaucus, and others, cover him with their shields. His friends carry him to his chariot and drive him toward the city. At the river they pour water over him. He opens his eyes, kneels, and vomits blood, then falls back unconscious.

With Hector gone, the Greeks attack even more fiercely. The fighting becomes a cycle of killing and boasting. Polydamas kills a Greek and mocks him. Ajax throws at Polydamas but kills a son of Antenor, and mocks the Trojans in return. The dead man's brother kills a Greek in revenge and says that a man should be glad to leave a relative behind to avenge him. Then a Greek leader named Peneleos kills the only son of a rich Trojan. He cuts off the head and holds it up on his spear like a flower, telling the Trojans to let the young man's parents weep. The Trojans are seized with fear and look for a way to escape.

Many more Trojans fall to Ajax, Antilochus, Meriones, Teucer, and Menelaus. The smaller Ajax, the son of Oileus, kills the most, because no one is faster at chasing men who run in fear.

This book is partly comic. The great king of the gods is tricked by his wife with beauty, love, and a lie. But the result is serious. While Zeus sleeps, many men die, and the course of the war changes for a while.

## Book 15: Fighting at the Ships

The Trojans have been driven back across the ditch and are standing pale with fear by their chariots. At this moment Zeus wakes up on Mount Ida beside Hera. He sees the Trojans running, Poseidon leading the Greeks, and Hector lying on the ground, gasping and vomiting blood.

Zeus pities Hector and looks angrily at Hera. He calls her a trickster and says he would like to beat her. He reminds her of the time he hung her in the sky with heavy anvils tied to her feet and golden chains on her hands, as punishment for her cruelty to his son Heracles. Hera trembles. She swears a great oath that Poseidon is helping the Greeks by his own choice, not because of her. This is not completely true, but it is clever. Zeus smiles. He says if she really agrees with him, she should go to Olympus and send Iris and Apollo to him.

Then Zeus tells Hera his whole plan for the future. Apollo will heal Hector and send him back to fight. Hector will drive the Greeks back to the ships of Achilles. Achilles will then send Patroclus into battle. Patroclus will kill many Trojans, including Zeus's own son Sarpedon, and then Hector will kill Patroclus. Achilles will kill Hector to avenge his friend. From that moment the Greeks will push the Trojans back until they take Troy, as Athena wishes. But until Achilles is honored, as Zeus promised Thetis, no god may help the Greeks. Here the poem tells us the main events of the rest of the story in advance. The suspense is not about what will happen, but about how.

Hera flies to Olympus as fast as a thought. The gods greet her. She smiles with her lips, but her forehead is worried. She tells them that Zeus is proud and cruel, and that they must accept whatever he sends. To hurt Ares, she tells him that his son Ascalaphus has been killed. Ares hits his thighs in grief. He says he will go down and avenge his son, even if Zeus strikes him with lightning. He orders his horses, Panic and Fear, to be harnessed. But Athena, afraid that Zeus will punish all the gods, runs to him. She pulls off his helmet and shield and takes his spear. She calls him a madman. Better men than his son have died, she says, and will die. Nobody can protect everyone's family. She makes him sit down.

Hera sends Iris and Apollo to Zeus on Ida. Zeus sends Iris to Poseidon with an order: stop fighting, and go back to the gods or to the sea. If not, Zeus, who is older and stronger, will come and fight him. Poseidon is very angry. He says that he, Zeus, and Hades are three brothers and equal. When the world was divided by lots, Zeus got the sky, Poseidon the sea, and Hades the world of the dead. The earth and Olympus belong to all three. Zeus should give orders to his own children, not to him. Iris asks politely if he really wants her to take such a harsh answer to Zeus, reminding him that the spirits of revenge always support the older brother. Poseidon admits that she is wise. He agrees to leave, but he warns that if Zeus saves Troy against the wishes of Athena, Hera, and the other gods, there will be lasting anger. Then he goes down into the sea, and the Greeks miss him badly.

Zeus sends Apollo to Hector. He gives Apollo his aegis, the terrifying shield-cloak, to frighten the Greeks. Apollo finds Hector sitting up and recovering. Hector, still weak, asks which god is speaking to him. Apollo tells him not to be afraid. Zeus has sent him, Apollo, to protect him. Apollo fills Hector with strength. Hector runs back to battle like a horse that has broken free from its stable. The Greeks are shocked to see him alive, like hunters who suddenly meet a lion. An Aetolian leader named Thoas says some god has saved Hector again. He advises that the best fighters stand firm in front, while the main army retreats to the ships.

The Trojans charge, with Apollo in front, wrapped in a cloud and holding the aegis. As long as he holds it still, men fall on both sides. But when he shakes it in the faces of the Greeks and shouts, they lose all courage, like cattle attacked by wild animals at night. Hector, Aeneas, Polydamas, Paris, and others each kill Greeks. The Greeks flee back over the ditch and inside the wall. Hector orders the Trojans to forget collecting armor and to go straight for the ships. Anyone who stays behind, he says, will be killed and left for the dogs.

Apollo kicks down the sides of the ditch, making a wide bridge for the chariots. Then he knocks down the Greek wall as easily as a child knocks down a sand castle on the beach. The Greeks are trapped at their ships, praying with raised hands. Nestor prays to Zeus to remember their sacrifices. Zeus thunders, but the Trojans take this as a sign for them and attack even harder. They climb over the wall like a wave over a ship's side. The Greeks climb onto their ships and fight with long poles meant for sea battles.

Meanwhile, Patroclus has been sitting with the wounded Eurypylus, talking to him and treating his wound. When he sees the Trojans pouring through the wall, he hits his thighs in dismay. He says he must leave and go to Achilles, to try to persuade him to fight. A friend's advice, he says, can be powerful.

The battle at the ships is perfectly balanced, as straight as a carpenter's line. Hector and Ajax fight over the same ship. Ajax kills a cousin of Hector who is bringing fire. Hector misses Ajax and kills a friend of Ajax. Ajax calls his brother Teucer to bring his bow. Teucer shoots a Trojan driver. Then he aims at Hector, which could have ended the battle. But Zeus is watching and breaks Teucer's bowstring, so the arrow goes wild. Teucer complains that a god is against them. Ajax tells him to leave the bow and fight with a spear and shield.

Hector sees this and tells the Trojans that Zeus is clearly on their side. Then he says that a man who dies fighting for his country dies with honor, and his wife, children, home, and land will be safe if the Greeks are driven away. On the other side, Ajax tells the Greeks that if Hector burns the ships, they cannot walk home across the sea. Hector, he says, is not inviting them to a dance but to a battle. Better to risk life or death now than to be slowly crushed by weaker men.

The fighting goes on. Menelaus kills a Trojan from behind. Hector scolds a cousin for not defending a dead relative. Ajax tells the Greeks that men who respect each other's opinion are less likely to die, and that there is no glory in running. Menelaus encourages Antilochus, the young son of Nestor, who kills a Trojan. When Hector comes running, Antilochus flees like an animal that knows it has done wrong.

The poet explains Zeus's plan again. Zeus is waiting to see a Greek ship burning. That will complete Thetis's prayer. After that, he will give the victory back to the Greeks. So he fills Hector with fury. Hector foams at the mouth, and his eyes blaze. But the poet also reminds us that Hector will die young, and that Athena is already bringing his death closer. Hector attacks the Greeks again and again, but they stand like a cliff against the sea. He kills one good Greek warrior who trips over the rim of his own shield.

The Greeks are pushed back from the first row of ships to their tents. Nestor begs each man, in the name of his parents, wife, and children, to stand firm. Athena removes a mist from their eyes, so they can see the whole battle. Ajax refuses to retreat. He jumps from ship to ship with a huge pole, like a rider leaping from horse to horse, shouting orders.

Hector grabs the stern of a ship, the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy, the first Greek to die in the war. Around it the fighting is close and terrible, with swords, axes, and spears. Hector holds on and shouts for fire. He says the old Trojan leaders held him back before, but now Zeus himself is urging him on. Ajax, hit by many weapons, steps back to the rowers' bench. There he stands with his long spear and kills anyone who brings fire to the ships. He shouts that they have no city or wall to protect them now, only the sea behind them and their own strong hands. In this way he kills twelve men in front of the ships.

## Book 16: The Death of Patroclus

While the Greeks fight around the ship of Protesilaus, Patroclus comes to Achilles in tears, like water running down a high rock. Achilles gently teases him. He asks why Patroclus is crying like a little girl who pulls at her mother's dress to be picked up. Has bad news come from home, or is he weeping for the Greeks, who are dying because of their own pride?

Patroclus answers with a heavy heart. All the best Greek fighters are wounded: Diomedes, Odysseus, Agamemnon, and Eurypylus. He asks Achilles how he can still be so hard. He says Achilles cannot really be the son of Peleus and Thetis; the gray sea and the steep cliffs must have given birth to him, he is so cruel. Then, using Nestor's idea from Book 11, he makes a request. If some prophecy is keeping Achilles out of battle, let him at least send Patroclus with the Myrmidons. And let Patroclus wear Achilles's armor, so the Trojans will think Achilles has returned and pull back. The poet tells us sadly that Patroclus does not know he is asking for his own death.

Achilles is deeply moved. He says no prophecy is holding him back. It is the insult that still hurts him, the way Agamemnon took Briseis from him as if he were a worthless outsider. But, he says, no man can stay angry forever. He agrees. Patroclus can wear his armor and lead the Myrmidons to save the ships. But Achilles gives him strict instructions. Patroclus must only drive the Trojans away from the ships and then come back. He must not chase them to the city, for two reasons. First, it would take away glory that belongs to Achilles. Second, some god might attack him, because Apollo loves the Trojans. Achilles then makes a strange wish: he wishes that all the Trojans and all the Greeks would die, so that only he and Patroclus would be left to take Troy together.

Meanwhile, Ajax can no longer hold out at the ship. Spears and stones keep hitting his helmet. His arm is tired from holding the shield, and he can hardly breathe. Then Hector cuts off the head of Ajax's spear with his sword. Ajax understands that Zeus is against him and steps back. The Trojans throw fire onto the ship, and it bursts into flames.

Achilles sees the fire. He tells Patroclus to arm quickly. Patroclus puts on the armor of Achilles piece by piece. But he does not take Achilles's great spear, made from an ash tree on Mount Pelion, because only Achilles can use it. Automedon, the chariot driver, harnesses Achilles's two immortal horses, Xanthus and Balius, and a third, mortal horse named Pedasus.

Achilles gathers the Myrmidons. They are as fierce as hungry wolves after a kill. He has fifty ships and five captains, including old Phoenix. He reminds the Myrmidons that they complained about his anger for keeping them out of battle. Now their chance has come. They form a tight line, shield against shield, like stones in a well-built wall.

Then Achilles goes into his tent. From a special chest given to him by his mother, he takes out a precious cup that he uses only to make offerings to Zeus. He cleans it, fills it with wine, and prays. He asks Zeus to give Patroclus victory and courage, so that Hector will learn that Patroclus can fight well even without Achilles. And he asks Zeus to bring Patroclus back safely, with his armor and his men. The poet says Zeus hears the prayer and grants half of it. Patroclus will drive the Trojans from the ships, but he will not return alive.

The Myrmidons pour out like angry wasps whose nest has been disturbed. Patroclus shouts that they must win glory for Achilles and show Agamemnon his mistake. When the Trojans see Patroclus in the shining armor of Achilles, they panic. They think Achilles has made peace with Agamemnon and returned. Patroclus kills the leader of the Paeonians near the burning ship, and puts out the fire. The Trojans are driven back, though not without hard fighting.

Many Greek leaders kill Trojans. The two sons of Nestor kill two brothers who were companions of Sarpedon. Idomeneus and Meriones kill others. The Trojans begin to flee in disorder, like lambs attacked by wolves. Hector knows the battle has turned, but he stays to protect his men for a while. Then his horses carry him away. Many Trojan chariots crash in the ditch. Patroclus's immortal horses jump over it in one leap. He tries to reach Hector, but Hector escapes. Patroclus cuts off the fleeing Trojans and drives them back toward the ships, killing many. He even pulls one man out of his chariot with his spear, like a fisherman pulling a fish out of the sea.

Then Sarpedon, the son of Zeus and leader of the Lycians, sees his men dying. He scolds them and says he will face this warrior himself. He jumps from his chariot, and so does Patroclus. They rush at each other like two screaming vultures.

On Olympus, Zeus looks down with pity. He tells Hera that he loves Sarpedon dearly and is thinking of saving him from death and carrying him home to Lycia. Hera objects. Sarpedon is a mortal, and his death has been fated for a long time. If Zeus saves him, other gods will want to save their own sons, and they will all be angry. But she suggests that, after his death, Zeus could send Death and Sleep to carry his body home, so his family can bury him with honor. Zeus agrees. But in grief he sends a rain of blood upon the earth in honor of his son. This scene shows that even the king of the gods accepts fate, and that he suffers too.

The fight begins. Patroclus kills Sarpedon's driver. Sarpedon misses Patroclus but kills the mortal horse Pedasus. Automedon quickly cuts the dead horse free. Sarpedon misses again. Then Patroclus's spear strikes Sarpedon near the heart. He falls like a tall tree. Dying, he calls to his friend Glaucus. He asks him to gather the Lycians and protect his body and armor, because it would be a lasting shame if the Greeks took them. Then he dies. Patroclus puts his foot on the body and pulls out the spear.

Glaucus is still wounded in the arm from Teucer's arrow. He prays to Apollo, who heals him. He gathers the Lycians and goes to Hector. He tells Hector that he has forgotten the allies who are dying for Troy far from home. Now Sarpedon, the strength of Lycia, is dead. The Trojans are filled with grief and anger, and Hector leads them back. Patroclus tells the two Ajaxes that they must take Sarpedon's body and armor.

A terrible fight breaks out over the body. Zeus spreads darkness over it. Hector kills a Myrmidon with a stone. Patroclus kills a Trojan in revenge. Glaucus kills a rich Myrmidon. Aeneas throws at Meriones and misses. Aeneas mocks Meriones, calling him a good dancer who would be dead if he had been hit. Meriones answers with his own boast. Patroclus stops him. Words belong in meetings, he says; in battle there should be blows, not talk.

The body of Sarpedon is now so covered with blood, dust, and weapons that no one can recognize him. Men swarm around it like flies around milk pails. Zeus watches and decides that Patroclus will first drive the Trojans back toward the city. He makes Hector lose courage. Hector flees in his chariot, seeing that Zeus's scales have turned against him. The Lycians also run. The Greeks strip Sarpedon's armor. Then Zeus tells Apollo to take Sarpedon's body away. Apollo washes it in the river, puts ambrosia on it, and dresses it in heavenly clothes. Then Death and Sleep, two brothers, carry it home to Lycia.

Now Patroclus forgets Achilles's warning. In pride and foolishness, he chases the Trojans toward the city. The poet says that if he had obeyed Achilles, he would have escaped death. He kills many more men. The Greeks might have taken Troy that day. But Apollo stands on the city wall. Three times Patroclus tries to climb the wall, and three times Apollo pushes him back with his own hands. The fourth time, Apollo shouts at him. It is not his fate to take Troy, nor even the fate of Achilles, who is a far better man. Patroclus steps back.

Hector is waiting in his chariot inside the Scaean Gates, unsure whether to fight or bring the army inside. Apollo comes to him in the shape of his uncle and tells him to attack Patroclus. Hector drives straight at Patroclus, ignoring all the other Greeks. Patroclus jumps down and throws a sharp stone. It hits Cebriones, Hector's driver and half-brother, in the forehead. Cebriones falls from the chariot. Patroclus jokes cruelly that the man dives so well he should look for oysters in the sea. Hector and Patroclus fight over the body like two lions over a deer. Hector holds the head, Patroclus holds the feet. The battle rages around them until evening, when the Greeks finally drag the body away and take the armor.

Then Patroclus charges three times, and each time he kills nine men. But on the fourth charge, his end comes. Apollo, hidden in a thick mist, comes behind him. He strikes Patroclus on the back with the flat of his hand, and Patroclus becomes dizzy. Apollo knocks the helmet of Achilles off his head. It rolls in the dust under the horses' feet, the first time that helmet has ever been dirtied. Patroclus's spear breaks in his hand, his shield falls, and Apollo opens his armor. Patroclus stands helpless and confused.

A young Trojan named Euphorbus, a fast runner and skilled fighter, stabs him in the back with a spear and runs away into the crowd. Wounded, Patroclus tries to retreat to his friends. Then Hector pushes through the ranks and drives his spear into Patroclus's belly. Patroclus falls, and the Greeks are filled with grief.

Hector boasts over him. He says Patroclus thought he would destroy Troy and carry away the Trojan women, but Hector was there to protect them. Now vultures will eat him. Achilles did not help him. Hector imagines Achilles told Patroclus not to come back until he had killed Hector.

With his last breath, Patroclus answers. Hector may boast, but it was Zeus and Apollo who defeated him and took his armor. If twenty men like Hector had attacked him, they would all have died. Fate and Apollo killed him first, then a mortal, Euphorbus, and Hector was only third. Then he makes a prophecy. Hector will not live long either. Death is near him, and he will die at the hands of Achilles.

Patroclus dies. His soul flies to the house of Hades, sad to leave its youth and strength behind. Hector speaks to the dead man. Why does he predict Hector's death? Who knows, maybe Hector will kill Achilles first. He pulls out his spear and goes after Automedon, but the immortal horses carry the driver away. The death of Patroclus is the turning point of the poem. Achilles's anger against Agamemnon has led to the death of the person he loves most.

## Book 17: The Fight for the Body

Menelaus learns that Patroclus has fallen. He pushes to the front and stands over the body, like a mother cow standing over her first calf. Euphorbus, the young Trojan who first wounded Patroclus, comes up and tells Menelaus to leave the body and its armor, because the glory belongs to him. Menelaus warns him to go away, reminding him that he has already killed his brother. Euphorbus answers that now Menelaus will pay for that death, and that he will bring Menelaus's head to his grieving parents. He throws, but his spear does not go through Menelaus's shield. Menelaus prays to Zeus and drives his spear through the young man's throat. Euphorbus falls, his beautiful hair, tied with gold and silver, covered in blood. The poet compares him to a young olive tree full of flowers, torn down by a sudden storm.

Apollo, in the shape of an ally of Troy, goes to Hector. He tells Hector to stop chasing the horses of Achilles, which no mortal except Achilles can control, because Menelaus has just killed Euphorbus. Hector is filled with grief and charges forward. Menelaus thinks hard. If he leaves the body, the Greeks will blame him. But if he fights Hector alone, with a god on Hector's side, he will die. He decides to retreat slowly, like a lion driven away from a farm, and look for Ajax.

He finds the great Ajax and tells him that Hector already has the armor of Achilles, but they must save the body. Meanwhile Hector has stripped the armor from Patroclus and is dragging the body away. He wants to cut off its head and throw the rest to the dogs of Troy. But Ajax arrives with his huge shield, and Hector pulls back. Ajax stands over the body like a lion protecting its cubs, with Menelaus beside him.

Glaucus, the Lycian leader, angrily scolds Hector. He says Hector looks brave but runs away in battle. He left the body of Sarpedon, his guest and friend, to the Greeks. If the Lycians listen to Glaucus, they will go home. He adds a practical point. If the Trojans could bring the body of Patroclus into Troy, the Greeks would trade Sarpedon's armor and body for it. But Hector did not dare to face Ajax, a better man. Hector replies that he fears no one, but the will of Zeus is stronger than any man. He tells Glaucus to watch him fight.

Then Hector does something important. He runs after the men who are carrying Achilles's armor to the city. He takes off his own armor and puts on the armor of Achilles, which the gods gave to Peleus, Achilles's father. The poet notes that Achilles himself will not grow old in his father's armor. Zeus watches and shakes his head. He pities Hector, who does not know that death is close. Zeus says it was not right for Hector to take the armor from the head and shoulders of such a hero. He gives Hector great strength for now, but he decides that Hector will never come home from battle to give that armor to Andromache. Ares enters Hector, and he shines in the armor so that he looks like Achilles. He promises the allies that whoever brings the body of Patroclus to the Trojans will get half the spoils and equal glory with himself.

The Trojans charge. Ajax tells Menelaus that he now fears less for the body than for their own lives. Menelaus calls for help, and several Greek leaders come. Zeus covers the fighting around the body in thick mist, because he liked Patroclus and does not want his body to be eaten by Trojan dogs. At first the Trojans pull the body away. Then Ajax charges like a wild boar and kills a Trojan who has tied a strap around the body's ankle and is dragging it. Hector throws at Ajax but kills a Greek leader. Ajax kills another Trojan.

The Trojans are about to run, but Apollo, in disguise, scolds Aeneas. Aeneas recognizes the god and tells Hector that Zeus is still with them. The Trojans turn around and fight again. Ajax orders the Greeks to stay close together around the body and not move forward or back. Because they protect one another, fewer Greeks die than Trojans.

The poet describes a strange scene. Over the body of Patroclus, it is dark, and the fighters suffer in mist. But in the rest of the battlefield, the sun is shining, and men fight at ease or rest. The two sons of Nestor, Antilochus and Thrasymedes, do not even know Patroclus is dead. All day the two sides pull the body back and forth, like workers stretching an ox hide. Each side says they would rather die than let the other have it.

Achilles still does not know. The battle is far away, near the walls of Troy. His mother has told him that Troy will not fall in his lifetime, but she has not told him that his dearest friend would die.

Then comes one of the most moving scenes in the poem. The immortal horses of Achilles stand away from the battle and weep. They have learned that their driver, Patroclus, has been killed. Automedon whips them and speaks to them kindly and harshly, but they will not move. They stand still like a stone on a grave, with their heads bowed and hot tears falling from their eyes. Zeus sees them and pities them. He asks why the gods gave these immortal horses to a mortal man. Was it so that they could share human sorrow? For of all creatures on earth, he says, none is more miserable than man. But he will not let Hector capture them. He gives them new strength, and they run back into the battle.

Automedon drives alone and cannot fight and drive at the same time. A friend named Alcimedon takes the reins, and Automedon fights. Hector and Aeneas see this and try to capture the horses. Automedon prays to Zeus, throws his spear, and kills one of the Trojans with them. The two Ajaxes come to help, and Hector and Aeneas pull back.

Now Zeus changes his mind and sends Athena to encourage the Greeks. In the shape of old Phoenix, she tells Menelaus it would be a shame if dogs tore apart the friend of Achilles. Menelaus answers that he wishes Athena would give him strength. Athena is pleased that he named her first, and makes him as bold as a fly that keeps coming back to bite. He kills a close friend of Hector. Apollo, in disguise, mocks Hector for running from Menelaus, who was never a great fighter. Hector is filled with grief and returns.

Then Zeus shakes his aegis, covers Mount Ida with cloud, and sends thunder and lightning. The Greeks panic. Several Greek leaders are wounded. Hector's spear misses Idomeneus but kills the driver of Meriones, who had come to rescue him. Meriones tells Idomeneus to flee to the ships, and he does.

Ajax sees that Zeus is helping the Trojans. Every Trojan weapon hits its target, while Greek weapons fail. He wishes someone could tell Achilles, but the darkness is so thick that he cannot even see who is nearby. He prays to Zeus: if the Greeks must die, at least let them die in daylight. Zeus pities his tears and clears away the mist. The sun shines on the battle.

Ajax asks Menelaus to find Antilochus, the son of Nestor, and send him to tell Achilles the terrible news. Menelaus leaves the body unwillingly, like a lion leaving a farm. He tells the two Ajaxes and Meriones to remember how kind and gentle Patroclus always was. He searches with eyes as sharp as an eagle's and finds Antilochus. He tells him that Patroclus has fallen and that Hector has the armor. Antilochus is speechless with shock. His eyes fill with tears. He gives his armor to a friend and runs, weeping, to Achilles.

Menelaus returns to the body. He tells the Ajaxes that even if Achilles is angry, he cannot fight, because he has no armor. Ajax gives the plan. Menelaus and Meriones will lift the body and carry it, while the two men called Ajax hold back the Trojans. Menelaus and Meriones lift the body high. The Trojans run after them like dogs chasing a wounded boar, but whenever the two Ajaxes turn to face them, they lose courage. Menelaus and Meriones sweat and pant like mules pulling a heavy beam down a mountain path. The two Ajaxes stand like a ridge of hills that holds back a flooding river. But Hector and Aeneas keep pressing, and the young Greeks flee before them like small birds before a hawk. Much fine armor lies scattered around the ditch, and the fighting does not stop.

## Book 18: The Shield of Achilles

Achilles is sitting by his ships. He sees the Greeks running back across the plain and he begins to fear the worst. His mother once told him that, while he was still alive, the best of the Myrmidons would be killed by the Trojans. He remembers that he told Patroclus to come back as soon as he had saved the ships, and not to fight Hector.

At that moment Antilochus arrives in tears. He says that Patroclus has fallen, that the two armies are fighting over his naked body, and that Hector has his armor. A dark cloud of grief falls over Achilles. He pours dust over his head and his handsome face. He throws himself on the ground and tears his hair. The captive women cry out and beat their breasts. Antilochus holds Achilles's hands, because he is afraid Achilles might cut his own throat.

Achilles gives a terrible cry, and his mother Thetis hears him at the bottom of the sea. Her sisters, the sea nymphs, gather around her, and she leads them in a song of mourning. She says she gave birth to the best of sons. She raised him like a young plant in a garden and sent him to Troy, but she will never welcome him home again. While he lives, he is full of sorrow, and she cannot help him.

The sea nymphs rise from the water and come to the shore. Thetis takes her son's head in her hands and asks why he is crying. Zeus has granted his wish, she says. The Greeks have been trapped at their ships. Achilles answers that yes, Zeus has granted his prayer, but what good is it now? His dear friend Patroclus, whom he loved as much as his own life, is dead, and Hector has taken the armor the gods gave to Peleus. He wishes Thetis had never married a mortal man. He says he does not want to live unless he kills Hector to pay for Patroclus's death.

Thetis weeps. She tells him that if he kills Hector, his own death will come soon after. Achilles answers that he would die right now, because he failed to protect his friend. He was not there when Patroclus needed him. He says he has been sitting by his ships, a useless weight on the earth, the best fighter among the Greeks but no help to anyone. Then he curses strife and anger, which rises in a man like smoke and tastes sweeter than honey, yet hardens even a good man's heart. That is what Agamemnon's insult did to him. But now he will put it behind him. He will go and find Hector, and he will accept his own death whenever the gods send it. Even Heracles, the strongest of heroes, could not escape death. Until then, he will win glory and make the Trojan women weep. This is the moment when Achilles chooses a short, glorious life over a long one, the choice he described in Book 9.

Thetis agrees that it is right to save his friends. But his armor is now in Hector's hands. She asks him not to go into battle until tomorrow morning, when she will bring him new armor made by Hephaestus. She sends her sisters back to the sea and goes to Olympus.

Meanwhile the battle for the body of Patroclus continues. Three times Hector grabs the body by the feet, and three times the two men called Ajax push him away. Hector would have taken it, but Hera secretly sends Iris to Achilles, without Zeus knowing. Iris tells him that Hector wants to cut off the head and put it on a stake on the wall of Troy. Achilles says he has no armor. Iris tells him to go to the ditch just as he is and show himself, so the Trojans will be frightened.

Achilles rises. Athena puts her aegis around his shoulders and surrounds his head with a golden cloud, from which fire shines like signal fires rising from a besieged island city. He stands at the ditch, without joining the fight, because he respects his mother's request. He gives a great shout, and Athena shouts with him. His voice is like a trumpet. Three times he shouts, and three times the Trojans fall into confusion. Their horses turn back in fear, and twelve of their best warriors die, crushed by their own chariots and spears. The Greeks pull the body of Patroclus away and lay it on a stretcher. Achilles weeps over his friend. He sent him into battle with horses and chariot, but did not welcome him back.

Hera makes the sun set early, and the fighting stops. The Trojans hold a meeting, standing up, because they are too frightened to sit. Polydamas, Hector's wise friend, who was born on the same night as Hector, speaks. He advises them to go back into the city now. While Achilles was angry with Agamemnon, he says, the Greeks were easier to fight. But now Achilles will not stay on the plain. He will try to take the city. If they are still on the plain in the morning, many will die. Behind the walls, they will be safe.

Hector is angry. He says the Trojans have already been trapped inside their walls too long, and the city's wealth has been spent on the war. Now that Zeus has given him glory, he will not hide. They will camp on the plain and attack the ships at dawn. If Achilles comes, Hector will face him. Ares, he says, is fair to all, and the killer may also be killed. The Trojans cheer. The poet calls them fools, because Athena has taken away their good sense. They follow Hector's bad advice and ignore the wise advice of Polydamas. This is one of Hector's biggest mistakes.

All night, the Greeks mourn Patroclus. Achilles leads the mourning. He lays his hands, which have killed so many, on his friend's chest, and groans like a lion whose cubs have been stolen by a hunter. He remembers promising Patroclus's father that he would bring his son home with his share of the plunder. But Zeus does not give men everything they want. Both he and Patroclus will die in the same land. Then he makes a promise to the dead man. He will not bury Patroclus until he brings him Hector's head and armor. And he will kill twelve Trojan young men at his funeral fire. Until then, captured Trojan women will weep over him day and night. The Myrmidons wash the body, put oil and ointment on its wounds, and wrap it in fine cloth.

On Olympus, Zeus says to Hera that she has finally got what she wanted: Achilles is back in the war. Hera answers that she has every right to plan evil for the Trojans if she is angry with them.

Thetis arrives at the bronze house of Hephaestus. He is busy making twenty tripods on golden wheels that can roll by themselves to the meetings of the gods and back. His wife welcomes Thetis. Hephaestus is very glad to see her. When he was born lame, his mother Hera threw him out of heaven, and Thetis and another sea goddess saved him. He lived with them secretly for nine years in a cave by the sea, making jewelry. So he owes her a great debt. He cleans himself and limps to meet her, supported by golden servant girls that he made himself. They can think, speak, and move like real women.

Thetis weeps and tells him her sorrows. Zeus forced her to marry a mortal man, who is now old. Her son will die young. She tells the story of Briseis, the embassy, and Patroclus's death. She begs Hephaestus to make new armor for her son, who does not have long to live. Hephaestus answers that he wishes he could hide Achilles from death as easily as he can make him armor that will amaze everyone.

He goes to his forge. Twenty bellows blow on the fire. He throws in bronze, tin, silver, and gold, and takes up his hammer and tongs. First he makes a great shield, with five layers. On it he creates a whole world.

In the center he puts the earth, the sky, and the sea, the sun, the full moon, and the stars. Then he makes two cities. In the first city, there is peace. People celebrate weddings, leading brides through the streets by torchlight, while young men dance to flutes and lyres and women watch from their doors. In the marketplace, a crowd gathers for a trial. Two men are arguing about blood money, a payment made to the family of a man who was killed. One says he has paid, the other says he has not. The people take sides, and old men sit on stone seats in a circle and give their judgments in turn.

The second city is at war. Two armies surround it and argue whether to destroy it or take half its wealth. The people of the city do not agree. They leave their wives, children, and old men on the walls and go out to set an ambush, led by Ares and Athena, shown in gold and larger than the men. They hide by a river where cattle come to drink. When two shepherds arrive, playing pipes and suspecting nothing, the hidden men kill them and take the herds. The armies hear the noise and fight by the river. Strife, Confusion, and Fate move among them. Fate drags the dead by their feet, and her robe is red with blood.

Hephaestus also makes scenes of country life. Men plow a large field. Each time they reach the end of a row, someone hands them a cup of wine. Though the field is made of gold, it looks dark like freshly turned earth. In another field, reapers cut grain, boys carry it, and men tie it into bundles, while the owner of the land watches happily. Under an oak tree, servants prepare a meal. There is a vineyard heavy with dark grapes, where young men and women carry the fruit in baskets while a boy plays the lyre and sings. There is a herd of cattle walking to the river. Two lions attack a bull, and the herdsmen and dogs are too afraid to stop them. There is a peaceful pasture with sheep, huts, and pens. And there is a dancing floor where young men and women dance, holding each other by the wrists, sometimes in a circle that turns like a potter's wheel and sometimes in lines, while a singer plays and acrobats perform. Around the edge of the shield flows the great river Ocean, which the Greeks believed circled the whole world.

The shield is one of the most famous passages in the poem. In the middle of a story about war and death, it shows the whole of human life: peace and war, justice, work, celebration, and nature. It reminds us of everything that Achilles is about to give up.

Then Hephaestus makes a breastplate brighter than fire, a strong helmet with a golden crest, and leg guards of tin. He gives all the armor to Thetis. She flies down from Olympus like a hawk, carrying the shining armor to her son.

## Book 19: The End of the Quarrel

At dawn, Thetis arrives at the ships with the new armor. She finds her son lying over the body of Patroclus, weeping, with his men weeping around him. She takes his hand. She tells him that they must let Patroclus lie, since he died by the will of the gods, and she gives him the armor made by Hephaestus. When she puts it down, it rings loudly. The Myrmidons are afraid to look at it. But Achilles's anger grows even stronger, and his eyes shine with a fierce light. He is glad to hold the god's gift.

He has one worry. He fears that flies will get into Patroclus's wounds and the body will rot while he is away fighting. Thetis promises to protect the body. Even if it lies for a whole year, it will stay fresh. She puts ambrosia and red nectar into the wounds to preserve it. She tells him to call the Greeks together, to give up his anger against Agamemnon, and to arm for battle.

Achilles walks along the shore and calls out with a loud voice. Everyone comes to the meeting, even the ship's pilots and the men who give out food, because Achilles has appeared again after so long. Odysseus and Diomedes come limping, still hurt by their wounds. Agamemnon, also wounded, comes last.

Achilles speaks first. He says that it would have been better for both of them if Briseis had died on the day he captured her. Then fewer Greeks would have died during his anger. The quarrel has helped only Hector and the Trojans. But now, he says, it is over. He puts his anger aside, because he cannot keep it forever. He asks Agamemnon to prepare the army for battle at once. The Greeks are overjoyed.

Agamemnon answers from his seat, without stepping into the middle of the crowd. He asks to be heard without interruption. He says the Greeks have often blamed him, but he was not really responsible. Zeus, Fate, and the Fury, a dark spirit of revenge, put madness into his mind on the day he took Briseis. He describes Ate, the goddess of blindness or folly, the oldest daughter of Zeus. She walks softly, not on the earth but on the heads of men, and makes them stumble into ruin.

He tells a story to show that even Zeus was once tricked by her. On the day the hero Heracles was to be born, Zeus announced that a boy born that day from his family would rule over all his neighbors. Hera tricked him into swearing an oath. Then she delayed the birth of Heracles and made another child of the same family, Eurystheus, be born early. So Eurystheus became the ruler, and Heracles had to serve him and do his terrible labors. Zeus was so angry that he grabbed Folly by the hair and threw her out of heaven down to earth. In the same way, Agamemnon says, he was blinded. Now he will make up for it. He offers all the gifts that Odysseus described in Book 9. He says Achilles can wait while the gifts are brought, if he wishes.

Achilles is not interested. He says Agamemnon can give the gifts or keep them, as he likes. Now they should not waste time on small matters. There is work to do. Everyone will see Achilles fighting in the front line again.

Odysseus gives practical advice. The men should not go to battle hungry. It will be a long fight, and no one can fight all day without food and wine. He suggests that the men eat first. Meanwhile, Agamemnon should bring out the gifts in front of everyone, so all can see them. He should swear an oath that he never slept with Briseis. And he should hold a feast to make peace with Achilles. Odysseus also gives Agamemnon some advice: even a king loses no honor by making amends when he was wrong. Agamemnon agrees. He sends young men to bring the gifts and orders a boar to be prepared for a sacrifice.

But Achilles refuses to eat. How can the men eat while the bodies of those Hector killed are still lying on the plain? He wants the army to fight without food until they have taken revenge, and to eat only at sunset. He himself can think of nothing but killing, blood, and the sound of dying men. Patroclus is lying in his tent with his feet toward the door, and his friends are weeping around him.

Odysseus answers firmly but respectfully. Achilles is much better in battle, he says, but Odysseus is older and wiser in advice. Men quickly grow tired of fighting. Men die every day in war; if they fasted for every death, they would never eat. They must bury the dead, mourn for a day, and then eat and drink so they can fight harder.

Odysseus and some young leaders bring out the gifts: seven tripods, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven skilled women, and Briseis. Odysseus weighs out ten talents of gold. Agamemnon cuts hairs from the boar's head and prays to Zeus, the Earth, the Sun, and the Furies, who punish people who swear false oaths. He swears that he never touched Briseis, not in his bed or in any other way. He kills the boar, and the herald throws it into the sea to feed the fish. Achilles then speaks. He says that Zeus truly blinds men and leads them to ruin; otherwise Agamemnon would never have angered him so much. Zeus must have wanted many Greeks to die. Then he ends the meeting and tells the men to eat. The Myrmidons take the gifts to his tents.

When Briseis sees the wounded body of Patroclus, she throws herself on it and cries out, tearing at her face and neck. She remembers that when she left, Patroclus was alive. Her life has been one sorrow after another. She saw her husband and her three brothers killed when her city was destroyed. But Patroclus told her not to cry. He promised he would make Achilles marry her and take her home to Phthia for a wedding feast. He was always kind to her, she says, and she will never stop mourning him. The other women weep too. They seem to weep for Patroclus, but each is really weeping for her own sorrows. This short scene gives a voice to the women who are captured and traded as prizes in this war.

The Greek leaders beg Achilles to eat, but he refuses. Only a few stay with him: Agamemnon, Menelaus, Odysseus, Nestor, Idomeneus, and Phoenix. Achilles remembers how Patroclus used to prepare his meals before battle. Now he will fast out of grief. He says that even the news of his father's death, or of the death of his young son Neoptolemus, who is growing up on the island of Scyros, would not hurt him more. He had hoped that he alone would die at Troy, and that Patroclus would go home, take his son back to Phthia, and show him his house and property. The old men around him sigh, each thinking of his own home.

Zeus pities Achilles. He tells Athena to put nectar and ambrosia into his chest so that he will not be weak from hunger. Athena, who already wants to do it, flies down like a hawk and does so secretly.

The Greek army pours out from the ships, their helmets, shields, and spears as thick as snowflakes. The whole earth shines with their bronze. In the middle of them, Achilles arms himself. He grinds his teeth, and his eyes blaze like fire, because his grief is too great to bear. He puts on the leg guards, the breastplate, and the sword. He takes up the great shield, which shines like the moon or like a fire seen by sailors far out at sea. He puts on the helmet, which shines like a star, with its golden crest. He tests the armor, and it feels so light that it seems to lift him like wings. He takes his father's great spear, which only he can use.

Automedon harnesses the horses, and Achilles climbs into the chariot, shining like the sun. He speaks to his immortal horses, Xanthus and Balius. He tells them to bring their driver safely back this time, and not leave him dead on the plain as they left Patroclus.

Then something wonderful happens. Hera gives the horse Xanthus a human voice. Xanthus bows his head until his mane touches the ground. He says they will save Achilles today. But the day of his death is near, and it will not be their fault. It was not their slowness that let Patroclus die. It was Apollo who killed him and gave the glory to Hector. Even though they can run as fast as the west wind, Achilles is fated to die at the hands of a god and a man. Then the Furies stop the horse from speaking further.

Achilles answers sadly. Why does Xanthus foretell his death? He knows well that he is fated to die here, far from his father and mother. But that will not stop him from giving the Trojans more war than they can bear. With a loud cry, he drives his horses to the front. Achilles goes into battle knowing he will die, which makes his choice both brave and tragic.

## Book 20: The Gods Go to War

The Greeks arm by their ships around Achilles, who is hungry for battle, and the Trojans arm on the high ground of the plain. On Olympus, Zeus asks Themis, the goddess of order, to call all the gods to a meeting. Every river and every nymph comes, except the great river Ocean. Poseidon asks why Zeus has called them.

Zeus explains. He cares about the Greeks and Trojans, even though they are dying. He himself will sit on Olympus and watch. But now the other gods may go down and help whichever side they like. The reason is surprising. If Achilles fights alone with no one to stop him, the Trojans will not stand for a moment. In his fury over Patroclus, he might even go beyond what fate allows and take Troy before its time.

So the gods take sides. Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes, and Hephaestus join the Greeks. Ares, Apollo, Artemis the archer goddess, her mother Leto, the river god Xanthus, and Aphrodite join the Trojans. While the gods are still away, the Greeks win easily, because the Trojans tremble at the sight of Achilles in his shining armor. But when the gods arrive, Strife rises up. Athena shouts by the ditch and on the shore. Ares shouts from the citadel of Troy, like a black storm cloud. Zeus thunders from the sky, and Poseidon shakes the earth until the mountains, the city, and the ships tremble. Even Hades, the king of the dead, jumps from his throne in fear, afraid that the ground will crack open and show his terrible kingdom to everyone.

The gods line up against each other. Apollo faces Poseidon. Athena faces Ares. Artemis faces Hera. Hermes faces Leto. The river god Xanthus, whom men call Scamander, faces Hephaestus.

Achilles wants only one thing: to find Hector. But Apollo, in the shape of a son of Priam, encourages Aeneas to fight Achilles. He reminds Aeneas that he once boasted over his wine that he would fight Achilles. Aeneas is unwilling. He remembers that Achilles once chased him from his cattle on Mount Ida, and he only escaped because Zeus helped him. He says no one can fight Achilles, because some god always protects him. Apollo answers that Aeneas's mother is Aphrodite, a daughter of Zeus, while Achilles's mother is only the daughter of an old sea god. He fills Aeneas with courage.

Hera sees this. She calls Poseidon and Athena and suggests they go down together to support Achilles, so that he will know the strongest gods are on his side. After today, she says, he can suffer whatever fate has spun for him on the day he was born. But Poseidon disagrees. He does not want to force the gods to fight each other, since their side is clearly stronger. He suggests that they sit and watch, and join the fight only if Ares or Apollo start fighting or hold Achilles back. So Poseidon and his group sit on an old earth wall built for Heracles, wrapped in mist. The gods who favor Troy sit on a hill called Callicolone. Neither side wants to begin.

On the plain, Aeneas and Achilles step out between the armies. Achilles comes at him like a wounded lion. He speaks first and mocks Aeneas. Does he hope to become king of Troy if he kills Achilles? Priam has sons of his own. Or have the Trojans promised him land? He reminds Aeneas how he ran away from him on Mount Ida, and warns him to go back before he regrets it.

Aeneas answers that he is not a child to be frightened by words. He knows Achilles's family, and Achilles knows his. Then he tells his own family history, back to Dardanus, a son of Zeus who founded the first city. He lists the kings of Troy, including Ganymede, the beautiful boy whom the gods took to serve wine to Zeus. He explains that Priam and Aeneas's father Anchises come from two branches of the same royal family. But courage, he says, is given by Zeus. Then he says that they should stop talking like women arguing in the street. There are enough insults for a hundred-oared ship. Let them fight with spears.

Aeneas throws first. His spear hits Achilles's shield. Achilles is afraid that it will go through, forgetting that the gift of a god cannot be broken by a man. The spear goes through two layers of the shield but stops in the gold layer. Then Achilles throws. His spear goes through the edge of Aeneas's shield and sticks in the ground behind him. Aeneas is shaking with fear. Achilles draws his sword and charges. Aeneas picks up a huge stone that two ordinary men could not lift.

At this moment, Poseidon speaks to the other gods. He feels sorry for Aeneas, who is about to die because he listened to Apollo. Aeneas is innocent and has always made good sacrifices. Also, it is fated that Aeneas will survive. Zeus now hates the family of Priam, but Aeneas and his descendants will one day rule the Trojans. Hera answers that she and Athena have sworn never to save any Trojan, so Poseidon can decide himself.

Poseidon goes into the battle. He puts a mist over Achilles's eyes, pulls the spear out of Aeneas's shield, and lays it at Achilles's feet. Then he lifts Aeneas high into the air and carries him over the armies to the edge of the battlefield. He scolds Aeneas for fighting a stronger man who is more loved by the gods. He tells him to stay away from Achilles. After Achilles is dead, Aeneas can fight in the front line without fear, because no other Greek will kill him.

The mist clears. Achilles sees his spear on the ground and no Aeneas. He is amazed and says that Aeneas must also be protected by the gods. He lets him go and turns to encourage the Greeks. He tells them to fight man against man, because even a god could not fight so many alone.

Hector tells the Trojans not to fear Achilles. He says he could fight the gods with words, but not with spears. Achilles, he says, will not do everything he promises. Hector will face him, even if his hands are like fire and his strength like iron. But Apollo warns Hector not to fight Achilles alone in the front line, but to stay in the crowd. Hector is afraid and obeys.

Achilles attacks the Trojans with a terrible cry. He kills a Trojan leader by splitting his head. He boasts over him that his family lands are far away by a lake. He kills a son of Antenor and another man who bellows like a bull as he dies.

Then he catches Polydorus, the youngest son of Priam. Priam had forbidden him to fight, because he was the youngest and the most loved, and the fastest runner. But the young man, foolishly showing off his speed, runs among the front lines. Achilles spears him in the back as he runs past. He falls to his knees, holding his insides in his hands.

When Hector sees his little brother dying, a mist comes over his eyes. He can no longer stay back. He rushes at Achilles like a flame. Achilles is glad. Here is the man who has wounded his heart most deeply by killing his friend. They speak. Achilles tells him to come closer and die sooner. Hector answers that he knows Achilles is much stronger, but the result is in the hands of the gods. His spear can be sharp too.

Hector throws, but Athena breathes on the spear and turns it back to land at his feet. Achilles charges to kill him, but Apollo snatches Hector away and hides him in a thick mist. Three times Achilles strikes at empty air. The fourth time, he shouts that Hector has escaped death again, saved by Apollo. But one day, if some god helps Achilles, he will finish him.

Achilles then kills many more Trojans. A young Trojan named Tros grabs his knees and begs for mercy, because they are the same age. The poet calls him a fool, because Achilles is not in a mood for pity. Achilles stabs him in the liver. He cuts off heads and pierces bodies one after another.

Achilles rages like a forest fire on a mountain in a dry season. His horses trample the shields and bodies of the dead the way oxen trample barley on a threshing floor, where grain is separated from straw. Blood splashes up from the hooves and wheels onto the axle and the chariot. Still, Achilles presses on to win more glory, his hands covered in blood. The book shows Achilles as a force of destruction. His grief has turned into a pitiless rage, and even begging for mercy cannot reach him now.

## Book 21: Achilles and the River

The Trojans flee to the ford of the river Xanthus, which men also call Scamander. Here Achilles splits their army in two. Half of them run across the plain toward the city, and Hera spreads a mist in front of them to slow them down. The other half are trapped against the deep river and fall into the water, with their horses, shouting in terror, like insects driven into a river by a grass fire.

Achilles leaves his spear leaning against a bush on the bank. He jumps into the river with only his sword and cuts down the Trojans on every side. The river turns red with blood. The Trojans hide under the banks like small fish fleeing from a dolphin. When his arms grow tired of killing, he pulls twelve young men out of the water alive. He ties their hands behind them with their own belts and sends them to the ships. He plans to kill them at the funeral of Patroclus, as he promised in Book 18.

Then he meets Lycaon, a young son of Priam, who is climbing out of the river. Achilles knows him. Some time before, he caught Lycaon at night in his father's orchard, cutting branches to make a chariot, and sold him as a slave on the island of Lemnos. A family friend paid a large sum to free him. Lycaon came home only eleven days ago, and on the twelfth day fate has put him into Achilles's hands again. Now he has no armor, no shield, and no spear; he threw them away to climb out of the river, and he is exhausted.

Achilles is surprised. He says that if this man can come back from being sold across the sea, perhaps the dead will start coming back too. He decides to see whether the earth can hold Lycaon. He thrusts his spear, but Lycaon ducks under it, grabs Achilles's knees with one hand and the spear with the other, and begs for his life. He reminds Achilles that he once ate bread in his tent as a prisoner. He was sold for the price of a hundred oxen and paid three times that to be free. Zeus must hate him, he says, to give him to Achilles a second time. He adds that he is not a full brother of Hector, who killed Patroclus. His mother is a different wife of Priam, and Achilles has already killed his only full brother, Polydorus.

Achilles answers him in a famous speech that is both cold and sad. He calls Lycaon a fool. Before Patroclus died, he says, he liked to spare Trojans and sell them as slaves. But now no Trojan will live, least of all the sons of Priam. Then he speaks almost gently. He calls Lycaon "friend" and tells him that he too must die. Why cry about it? Patroclus died, and he was a much better man. Even Achilles himself, handsome and strong, the son of a goddess, will die. One day, at dawn, at noon, or at evening, someone will kill him with a spear or an arrow. In this speech, Achilles sees that all men share the same fate, but this knowledge makes him merciless, not kind.

Lycaon lets go of the spear and spreads his arms. Achilles kills him with his sword and throws his body into the river. He tells the dead man that fish will eat him and his mother will never mourn over his body. He says the river, to which the Trojans sacrificed bulls and horses, will not save them.

The river god is growing angry. He fills a warrior named Asteropaeus with courage. Asteropaeus, the leader of the Paeonians, is the grandson of another river god and can throw with both hands. He tells Achilles that he arrived at Troy only eleven days ago. He throws two spears at once. One hits Achilles's shield and stops at the gold layer. The other scratches Achilles's elbow, and his blood flows, the only time Achilles is wounded in the poem. Achilles throws his great spear, but it misses and sticks deep in the riverbank. Asteropaeus tries three times to pull it out, and the fourth time tries to break it. Before he can, Achilles kills him with his sword. Achilles boasts that a grandson of a river cannot beat a descendant of Zeus, because even the great river Ocean fears Zeus's lightning. He leaves the body in the water, where eels and fish eat it, and kills many more Paeonians.

Now the river god himself rises in human form and speaks to Achilles. He says that Achilles is the strongest of men but also the most violent. His waters are so full of dead bodies that he cannot flow to the sea. He begs Achilles at least to kill the Trojans on land and not in his stream. Achilles says he will, but he will not stop killing until he has driven the Trojans into the city and faced Hector.

But Achilles jumps into the middle of the river again. The river becomes furious. He rises in a huge wave. He throws the dead bodies onto the land, roaring like a bull, and hides the living Trojans in his deep pools. The wave crashes on Achilles's shield, and he cannot stand. He grabs an elm tree, but it pulls out by the roots and falls across the river like a bridge. Achilles climbs out and runs across the plain in fear. The river chases him, rising behind him. However fast he runs, the water catches up, like water running down a garden ditch faster than the gardener can follow. Every time he stops to stand his ground, a wave crashes down on his shoulders and the water washes the ground from under his feet.

Achilles cries out to Zeus. He asks whether no god will pity him. He blames his mother, who told him he would die under the walls of Troy from Apollo's arrows. He would rather be killed by Hector, the best of the Trojans, so that a hero would die at the hands of a hero. Instead, he will die like a pig farmer's boy swept away while crossing a stream in a storm. This shows how much Achilles cares about dying with honor.

Poseidon and Athena come to him in human shape and take his hand. They tell him it is not his fate to drown in the river. The water will soon go down. They advise him to keep fighting until he has driven the Trojans inside their walls, and then to kill Hector and return to the ships. Athena gives him strength, and he pushes through the floodwater.

But the river is still angry. He calls his brother, the river Simois, to help him. He wants them to raise all their streams and throw rocks and tree trunks, to bury Achilles so deep in sand and mud that the Greeks will never find his bones and will not need to build him a tomb. He rises up, full of foam, blood, and bodies, to drown Achilles.

Hera is afraid Achilles will be swept away. She calls her son Hephaestus, the god of fire. She tells him to fight the river with fire, while she brings strong winds from the sea to spread the flames. Hephaestus lights a great fire. First it burns the dead bodies on the plain and dries the ground. Then he turns the fire on the river. It burns the trees and reeds along the banks. The fish and eels suffer, and the water begins to boil like a pot of fat on a fire. The river god begs Hephaestus to stop. He says he cannot fight against fire, and he does not care anymore about helping the Trojans. Then he begs Hera. He swears he will never again save the Trojans, not even when the whole city is burning. Hera tells Hephaestus to stop, saying it is not right to hurt a god so badly for the sake of mortals. The river returns to its bed.

Now the other gods start fighting each other. The earth groans and the sky rings like a trumpet. Zeus, sitting on Olympus, laughs with pleasure to see the gods fighting. Ares attacks Athena first. He reminds her that she helped Diomedes wound him in Book 5. He strikes her aegis with his spear. Athena picks up a huge black stone, an old boundary marker from a field, and hits Ares on the neck. He falls and covers a great area of ground. Athena laughs at him. Aphrodite leads the groaning Ares away by the hand. Hera tells Athena to go after her. Athena hits Aphrodite on the chest, and she falls too. Athena mocks them, saying that if all of Troy's helpers were as strong as Aphrodite, the war would have ended long ago.

Poseidon challenges Apollo. He reminds Apollo that they both once worked for Laomedon, an earlier king of Troy. Poseidon built the city's walls, and Apollo looked after the king's cattle. But Laomedon refused to pay them and threatened to cut off their ears. So why does Apollo help the Trojans now? Apollo answers that he will not fight his father's brother for the sake of miserable mortals. Humans, he says, are like leaves: they grow for a while and eat the fruit of the earth, and then they wither and die. He walks away.

His sister Artemis, the huntress, mocks him for running away. Hera hears her and grows angry. She grabs Artemis's wrists with one hand, takes the bow from her shoulder with the other, and laughing, beats her about the ears with it. Artemis's arrows fall out, and she runs away crying, like a dove fleeing a hawk. Hermes tells Leto, Artemis's mother, that he will not fight her. Leto picks up her daughter's bow and arrows. Artemis runs to Zeus and sits on his knees in tears. Zeus laughs and asks who has hurt her. She says it was his wife Hera. This battle of the gods is almost comic. While men suffer and die below, the gods fight with little real harm and quickly forget it.

Apollo goes into Troy, worried that the Greeks might take the city before its fated time. The other gods return to Olympus. Achilles continues to kill Trojans and their horses.

Old King Priam stands on a tower and sees Achilles chasing the Trojans with no one to stop him. He groans and orders the gatekeepers to open the gates to let the fleeing soldiers in, but to close them quickly so Achilles cannot enter. The Trojans run toward the city, thirsty and covered in dust, with Achilles behind them.

The Greeks would have taken the city then, but Apollo encourages Agenor, a son of Antenor. Agenor thinks to himself. If he runs with the others, Achilles will catch him and kill him as a coward. If he runs away into the hills of Ida, Achilles may see him and catch him on the plain. So he decides to stand and fight. Achilles, after all, is mortal, and his flesh can be pierced like any man's. Agenor throws his spear. It hits Achilles's leg below the knee, but the armor made by the god stops it. Achilles charges, but Apollo hides Agenor in mist and carries him away.

Then Apollo plays a trick. He takes the shape of Agenor and runs in front of Achilles, always just a little ahead, leading him away across the fields toward the river. While Achilles chases the god, the rest of the Trojans pour through the gates into the city. They do not even stop to see who has survived and who has died. Everyone who can still run crowds inside the walls.

## Book 22: The Death of Hector

The Trojans are now safe inside the walls. Frightened like young deer, they wipe away their sweat and drink water. But fate keeps Hector outside, alone in front of the Scaean Gates.

Out on the plain, Apollo stops running and reveals himself to Achilles. He asks why Achilles, a mortal, is chasing a god whom he can never kill. While Achilles was chasing him, the Trojans escaped into the city. Achilles is furious. He calls Apollo the cruelest of the gods for robbing him of glory, and says he would take revenge if he could. Then he turns and runs back toward the city as fast as a winning racehorse.

Old King Priam is the first to see him coming. Achilles's armor shines like the brightest star in the sky, the star called Orion's Dog, which appears at harvest time and brings fever and disease. Priam cries out and beats his head with his hands. He stretches out his arms and begs Hector to come inside. Achilles is stronger, he says. He has already killed or sold many of Priam's sons. Even now, two of them, Lycaon and Polydorus, are missing. Priam begs Hector to pity his old father. If Hector dies, Priam will live to see his sons killed, his daughters dragged away, and little children thrown to the ground. In the end, he says, his own dogs, which he fed at his table, will tear his body apart at his own gates. When a young man dies in battle, everything about his death is honorable. But nothing is more pitiful than an old man's body dishonored by dogs.

Hector's mother, Hecuba, also weeps. She uncovers her breast and shows him where she fed him as a baby. She begs him to come inside and fight from the wall. If Achilles kills him, she says, neither she nor his wife will be able to weep over his body, because the dogs will eat it near the Greek ships.

But nothing moves Hector. He leans his shield against the wall and waits, like a snake waiting in its hole. He speaks to himself. If he goes inside, Polydamas will be the first to blame him, because Polydamas told him to bring the army back into the city on the night Achilles returned, and he refused. Now his overconfidence has destroyed the army. He is ashamed to face the Trojan men and women. He would rather kill Achilles or die gloriously before the city. For a moment, he imagines another possibility. What if he put down his shield and helmet and went to Achilles unarmed? He could offer to give back Helen and all her treasure, and half of all the wealth in Troy. But he quickly sees that this is a dream. Achilles would show no mercy and would kill him like a woman, unarmed. This is no time for sweet talk, like young lovers chatting by a rock or a tree. It is better to fight and see whom Zeus favors.

Then Achilles arrives, like the war god himself, his spear shining like fire or the rising sun. When Hector sees him, fear takes hold of him. He cannot stand still. He turns and runs. Achilles chases him like a hawk chasing a dove.

They run along the road under the city wall, past a lookout point and a wild fig tree, to the two springs that feed the river Scamander. One spring is warm and steams like smoke; the other is cold as ice even in summer. Beside them are the stone basins where, in peaceful times before the Greeks came, the Trojan women used to wash their clothes. They run past these. The poet says that a good man is running ahead, but a much better man is chasing him. They are not running for a prize like an animal or an ox hide, as in a race, but for the life of Hector. Three times they run all the way around the city.

All the gods watch. Zeus speaks first. He pities Hector, who has given him many sacrifices. He asks the gods whether they should save him. Athena objects. Would Zeus save a mortal man whose death was decided long ago? Zeus tells her he did not mean it seriously. She may do what she wishes. Athena flies down.

Achilles keeps chasing Hector like a hunting dog after a young deer. Whenever Hector tries to run close to the city gates, so the Trojans can help him by throwing weapons from the wall, Achilles cuts him off and drives him back toward the plain. The poet compares it to a dream in which one person cannot escape and the other cannot catch him. Hector might still have escaped, but Apollo, who has been giving him strength, is about to leave him. Achilles signals to the Greek soldiers not to throw anything at Hector, because he wants the glory for himself.

On the fourth time around, as they reach the springs, Zeus takes out his golden scales. He puts the fate of Achilles in one pan and the fate of Hector in the other. Hector's side sinks down toward the house of Hades. At that moment, Apollo leaves Hector.

Athena goes to Achilles and tells him to stop and rest. She will persuade Hector to stand and fight. Then she takes the shape of Deiphobus, Hector's favorite brother, and goes to Hector. She says she has come out to help him, and that together they should face Achilles. Hector is deeply grateful. He says Deiphobus was always his dearest brother, and now he loves him even more, because he alone came out of the city when everyone else stayed inside. Athena says that their parents and friends begged her to stay inside, but she could not bear to leave him alone.

Hector turns to face Achilles. He says he will not run anymore. He has run three times around the city, but now he will kill or be killed. He proposes an agreement, with the gods as witnesses. Whoever wins will take the other's armor but return the body to his people for burial.

Achilles looks at him with hatred. He says there can be no agreements between lions and men, or between wolves and lambs. They hate each other forever. There will be no promises between them until one of them is dead. Hector must now pay for all the friends of Achilles he has killed.

Achilles throws his spear. Hector ducks, and it flies over his head. Athena secretly picks it up and gives it back to Achilles. Hector does not see this. He tells Achilles that he has missed, and that he will not be stabbed in the back while running. He throws his own spear. It strikes the center of Achilles's shield and bounces off. Hector has no second spear. He calls to Deiphobus for another, but no one is there.

Then Hector understands. The gods have tricked him. Deiphobus is inside the walls, and Athena has deceived him. Death is near, and there is no escape. Zeus and Apollo, who used to protect him, have decided his fate. But he will not die without a fight. He will do some great deed that people will talk about in the future. He draws his sword and charges like an eagle diving from the clouds.

Achilles charges at him with his shield in front of him and his helmet shining. The point of his spear gleams like the evening star. Achilles studies Hector's body, looking for a weak spot. Hector is wearing the armor of Achilles that he took from Patroclus, so Achilles knows it well. The only open place is the throat, where the collarbones meet the neck. Achilles drives his spear there. It goes through the neck but does not cut the windpipe, so Hector can still speak. He falls in the dust.

Achilles boasts over him. Hector thought he was safe when he killed Patroclus and took his armor, but Achilles, a far greater man, was waiting. Now the Greeks will bury Patroclus with honor, while dogs and vultures will tear Hector apart.

Dying, Hector begs Achilles, by his life, his knees, and his parents, not to let dogs eat him. He asks him to accept a rich ransom of gold and bronze from his father and mother and send his body home, so the Trojans can give him a funeral. Achilles refuses with terrible words. He calls Hector a dog. He says he wishes he could cut up Hector's flesh and eat it raw for what he has done. Even if Priam offered ten or twenty times the ransom, or Hector's weight in gold, his mother would never lay out his body and mourn for him. Dogs and birds will eat him.

With his last breath, Hector says he knew Achilles could not be moved, because his heart is made of iron. But he warns him to be careful, so that he does not bring the anger of the gods on himself. One day, Paris and Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean Gates, brave as he is. Then Hector dies. His soul flies down to Hades, sad to leave its youth and strength. Achilles speaks to the dead body. Let him die. Achilles will accept his own death whenever Zeus and the gods decide.

Achilles pulls out his spear and strips off the armor. The other Greeks run up and look at Hector's strong and handsome body. Each one stabs it again. They joke that Hector is much easier to handle now than when he was throwing fire on their ships.

Achilles speaks to the Greek leaders. For a moment he thinks of attacking the city right away, to see whether the Trojans will give up now that Hector is dead. Then he stops himself. Patroclus is still lying unburied at the ships. Even if the dead forget everything in the house of Hades, he will never forget his friend. They should sing a song of victory and take Hector's body back to the ships.

Then he does something shameful. He cuts holes behind the ankles of both of Hector's feet, between the heel and the ankle, and passes leather straps through them. He ties the body to his chariot, with the head dragging on the ground. He puts the armor in the chariot and drives off. Dust rises around Hector. His dark hair spreads out, and his head, once so handsome, is dragged through the dirt. Zeus has given him to his enemies to be shamed in his own land. This mistreatment of a dead body breaks the rules of respect that Greeks and Trojans normally share.

On the wall, Hecuba tears her hair and throws off her veil. Priam groans. The whole city weeps, as if Troy itself were already burning. The people can barely hold Priam back from running out of the gates. He rolls in the dirt and begs them to let him go alone to the Greek ships. He wants to beg Achilles to respect his old age. Achilles has a father too, he says, an old man like himself. Priam has lost many sons to Achilles, but he grieves for Hector more than all of them. He wishes Hector had died in his arms, so that his parents could at least have wept over him. This wish prepares us for the last book. Hecuba leads the women in mourning. She says that Hector was the pride of Troy, honored like a god, and now she has nothing left to live for.

Andromache, Hector's wife, has not heard anything yet. She is weaving a purple cloth with flowers in an inner room. She has told her servants to heat water for a warm bath for Hector when he comes back from battle. The poet says she does not know that he is far beyond the reach of baths. Then she hears cries from the wall. She trembles and drops her shuttle, the tool used to pass thread through the loom. She fears that Achilles has caught Hector alone, because he never stayed with the other men but always ran ahead.

She runs to the wall like a madwoman and looks out. She sees Hector's body being dragged behind the horses toward the Greek ships. Darkness covers her eyes and she faints. Her headdress falls off, including the veil that Aphrodite gave her on her wedding day. Her sisters-in-law hold her up.

When she wakes, she mourns. She says that she and Hector were born to share the same unhappy fate. Now he goes to Hades and leaves her a widow, with a baby son. She describes the hard life that awaits their son, Astyanax. Even if he survives the war, others will take his land. A child without a father is cut off from his friends. He will go begging among his father's friends, pulling at their clothes. Someone might let him take a sip from a cup, but not enough to drink. A boy whose parents are alive will push him away from the table and say, "Out with you, you have no father here." The child who once sat on his father's knee and ate the best food will come crying back to his widowed mother. Now worms will eat Hector by the ships after the dogs have finished. He lies naked, while fine clothes made by women lie at home. She says she will burn all those clothes, since he will never wear them, as an honor to him in the eyes of the Trojans. The women weep with her. Hector's death means the death of Troy and of the family we met in Book 6.

## Book 23: The Funeral Games

While the Trojans mourn in their city, the Greeks return to their ships. But Achilles does not let the Myrmidons unharness their horses yet. First they must honor Patroclus. Three times they drive their chariots around his body, weeping, and the sand and their armor are wet with tears. Achilles lays his hands, red with blood, on his friend's chest. He says goodbye to Patroclus, even in the house of Hades. He promises again to give Hector's body to the dogs and to kill twelve young Trojans at his funeral fire. Then he throws Hector's body face down in the dust beside the bed where Patroclus lies. The Myrmidons hold a large funeral feast, and they kill many oxen, sheep, goats, and pigs.

The Greek leaders bring Achilles to Agamemnon's tent. They have warm water ready so he can wash off the blood. But Achilles swears by Zeus that no water will touch him until he has burned Patroclus, built his tomb, and cut his own hair in mourning. He asks Agamemnon to send men at dawn to collect wood for the funeral pyre, the large pile of wood on which a body is burned.

That night, Achilles lies by the sea among his men and finally falls into a deep sleep, exhausted from chasing Hector around the city. Then the ghost of Patroclus comes to him. It looks exactly like him, with the same height, voice, eyes, and clothes. The ghost speaks. Achilles is sleeping and has forgotten him, he says. He loved him while he was alive, but now he does not care for him dead. He asks Achilles to bury him quickly. The other ghosts will not let him cross the river into the world of the dead, so he wanders alone at its gates. Once he is burned, he will never come back. They will never again sit together and talk as friends. He reminds Achilles that he too is fated to die under the walls of Troy.

He makes one more request. He asks that their bones be placed together in one urn, a container for the ashes of the dead. He reminds Achilles how they grew up together. As a boy, Patroclus accidentally killed another child in a quarrel over a game of dice. His father brought him to the house of Peleus, who welcomed him and made him Achilles's companion. Let them be together in death too, in the golden jar that Thetis gave her son.

Achilles promises to do everything. He asks Patroclus to come closer so they can embrace one more time. He reaches out his arms, but there is nothing to hold. The ghost disappears into the earth like smoke, with a thin cry. Achilles jumps up and claps his hands. He says that in the house of Hades there are ghosts and shadows, but no real life. All night, he says, the spirit of Patroclus stood over him, looking just like himself. His words make everyone weep again until dawn.

In the morning, Agamemnon sends men and mules to Mount Ida to cut wood, led by Meriones. They cut down tall oaks and bring the logs to the shore, to the place Achilles has chosen for the tomb of Patroclus and, later, for himself. The Myrmidons put on their armor and drive their chariots in a procession, with thousands of men on foot behind them. In the middle, Patroclus's friends carry the body. They cut off locks of their hair and cover him with them. Achilles walks behind, holding his friend's head.

At the pyre, Achilles cuts off a lock of his own golden hair. His father had promised to offer it to the river Spercheius at home, together with a sacrifice, when Achilles returned safely. But now Achilles knows he will never go home. So he places the lock in the hands of Patroclus instead.

The mourning goes on until Achilles asks Agamemnon to send the army away to eat. Only the closest friends stay. They build a huge pyre, a hundred feet on each side, and lay Patroclus on top. They kill many sheep and oxen. Achilles covers the body with fat and puts jars of honey and oil beside it. He throws four horses onto the pyre, and two of Patroclus's dogs. Then, in his fury, he kills the twelve young Trojan prisoners with his sword and puts them on the fire too. He calls to Patroclus that he has kept his promise, and that dogs, not fire, will eat Hector.

But the dogs do not touch Hector's body. Aphrodite keeps them away day and night, and puts oil of roses on the body so it will not be torn when Achilles drags it. Apollo covers the area with a dark cloud so the sun will not dry it out.

The pyre will not catch fire. Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind and promises them offerings. Iris goes to fetch them from their feast. The two winds come roaring across the sea and blow on the pyre all night. All night Achilles pours wine on the ground from a golden cup and calls the spirit of Patroclus, walking around the fire and groaning like a father burning the bones of his son who died just after his wedding. At dawn the fire dies down, and the winds go home. Achilles falls asleep, exhausted.

The leaders wake him. He asks them to put out the fire with wine and carefully collect the bones of Patroclus, which lie in the center, apart from the animals and the men. They place the bones in a golden urn with fat and cover it with linen. Achilles tells them to build only a modest tomb for now. Later, after Achilles himself has died, the Greeks who remain can build it wide and high for both of them. They raise a mound of earth.

Then Achilles tells the army to sit, and he brings out prizes from his ships: cauldrons, tripods, horses, mules, oxen, women, and iron. He announces funeral games, a series of sports contests held to honor a dead hero. This part of the book is lighter. It shows the Greek leaders competing, arguing, and making peace, and it shows Achilles acting as a generous and fair host.

The first and longest contest is the chariot race. Achilles does not compete, because his immortal horses are mourning their kind driver, who used to wash and care for them. Five men enter: Eumelus, Diomedes with the horses he took from Aeneas, Menelaus, Antilochus the son of Nestor, and Meriones. Before the race, old Nestor gives his son Antilochus long advice. His horses are slow, Nestor says, so he must win by skill, not speed. A woodcutter or a sailor does more by skill than by strength. He describes the turning point, an old tree stump between two white stones. Antilochus must turn around it as tightly as possible, leaning left, without hitting the stone. They draw lots for starting places. Phoenix is sent to watch the turning post and report fairly.

The chariots race off in a cloud of dust. On the way back, Eumelus is in front, and Diomedes is just behind him. Then Apollo, who dislikes Diomedes, knocks the whip out of his hand. Diomedes cries with anger. But Athena sees it, gives him back his whip, and breaks the yoke of Eumelus's chariot. Eumelus is thrown out and hurts his face and elbows. Diomedes takes the lead.

Behind him, Antilochus urges his horses to catch Menelaus. He sees a narrow place where rain has damaged the road. Menelaus slows down to be safe, but Antilochus drives alongside him dangerously. Menelaus shouts that the road is too narrow and they will both crash. Antilochus pretends not to hear. Menelaus pulls back to avoid an accident, and Antilochus gets ahead. Menelaus angrily calls him a cheat.

Meanwhile, the spectators argue. Idomeneus, standing on high ground, thinks he sees Diomedes leading. The smaller Ajax rudely tells him that he is old and his eyes are poor. Idomeneus offers to bet a tripod. Achilles stops their quarrel and tells them to just wait and watch.

Diomedes comes in first, his horses covered in sweat. Antilochus comes second, by a trick, with Menelaus right behind him. If the race had been longer, Menelaus would have passed him. Meriones is fourth. Eumelus comes last, dragging his broken chariot. Achilles feels sorry for him. He says the best man came last, and proposes to give Eumelus the second prize.

Antilochus objects. He says Eumelus should have prayed to the gods. If Achilles feels sorry for him, he can give him something else from his own rich store, but Antilochus will not give up the mare he has won. Achilles smiles, because Antilochus is one of his dearest friends. He gives Eumelus a fine bronze breastplate instead.

Then Menelaus stands up, angry. He accuses Antilochus of blocking his horses with a dirty trick. He asks Antilochus to swear by Poseidon that he did not do it on purpose. Antilochus wisely apologizes. He says he is young, and young men act too fast and think too little. He offers to give the mare to Menelaus, and more besides, rather than lose his friendship. Menelaus is soothed, like a field of grain refreshed by dew. He says that Antilochus, his father, and his brother have all worked hard for him, so he gives the mare back. He takes the cauldron instead. This small scene shows how a quarrel over a prize can be solved by respect and generosity, in contrast to the quarrel of Book 1.

One prize is left over, a two-handled jar. Achilles gives it to old Nestor as a memory of Patroclus, since Nestor is too old to compete. Nestor is delighted. He tells a long story about the funeral games of his youth, when he won in boxing, wrestling, running, and spear throwing, and lost only the chariot race, to twin brothers. He thanks Achilles for remembering him.

Next comes boxing. Epeus, a huge man, grabs the prize mule and boasts that no one can beat him. He admits he is not good in battle, but no man can be good at everything. Only Euryalus dares to fight him. Epeus soon knocks him out with a punch to the jaw. Euryalus jumps up like a fish leaping out of the water and falls. Epeus helps him up, and his friends carry him away, spitting blood.

In wrestling, the great Ajax faces clever Odysseus. They hold each other like the beams of a roof. Neither can throw the other. Finally Ajax lifts Odysseus off the ground, but Odysseus kicks the back of his knee, and they fall with Odysseus on top. Then Odysseus tries to lift Ajax, but can only move him a little. His knee gives way, and they fall side by side. As they get ready for a third round, Achilles stops them and declares a tie. Each gets an equal prize.

In the foot race, the smaller Ajax, Odysseus, and Antilochus compete. Ajax leads, with Odysseus right behind him. Near the end, Odysseus prays to Athena, and she makes him lighter. Then Ajax slips in the dung of the cattle killed for the funeral and falls with his mouth and nose full of it. Odysseus wins the silver bowl. Ajax takes the ox, spitting out the dung, and complains that Athena always helps Odysseus like a mother. Everyone laughs. Antilochus comes last and jokes politely that the gods favor older men. He adds that only Achilles can beat Odysseus in running. Achilles likes the compliment and gives him extra gold.

Then Achilles offers Sarpedon's armor for a fight in full armor, until one man draws blood. Ajax and Diomedes fight. When Diomedes keeps aiming his spear at Ajax's neck, the Greeks become afraid and stop the fight. They share the prize, but Achilles gives Diomedes a fine sword.

Next is a contest to throw a heavy lump of iron, which will also be a useful prize, enough iron to last a farm for five years. Epeus throws it badly, and everyone laughs. Polypoetes throws it the farthest and wins.

In archery, the target is a pigeon tied by a string to a ship's mast. Teucer shoots first, but he forgets to promise a sacrifice to Apollo. He misses the bird and cuts the string, and the pigeon flies away. Meriones quickly grabs the bow, promises a sacrifice to Apollo, and hits the pigeon high in the sky. It falls dead. Meriones wins the first prize, and Teucer gets the second.

Finally, Agamemnon and Meriones stand up for the spear-throwing contest. But Achilles says that everyone knows Agamemnon is the best and strongest. He offers Agamemnon the first prize without a contest, and suggests that Meriones take the spear. Agamemnon agrees. This last gesture shows that Achilles and Agamemnon have truly made peace, with Achilles now giving honor to the king who once dishonored him.

## Book 24: Priam and Achilles

The funeral games are over, and the Greeks go to their ships to eat and sleep. But Achilles cannot sleep. He keeps thinking of Patroclus, of his strength and courage, and of everything they did and suffered together in battle and at sea. He weeps and turns from side to side. Finally he gets up and wanders along the shore. At dawn, he ties Hector's body to his chariot and drags it three times around the tomb of Patroclus. Then he leaves it lying face down in the dust. This happens every morning. But Apollo pities Hector even in death, and covers the body with his golden aegis so that it is not damaged.

The gods look down with pity. Most of them want Hermes, the messenger god and guide, to steal the body away. But Hera, Poseidon, and Athena still hate Troy. The poet reminds us why. Long ago, Paris insulted Hera and Athena when the goddesses came to him, and he chose Aphrodite, who offered him a woman. This choice is known as the judgment of Paris.

On the twelfth day after Hector's death, Apollo speaks angrily to the gods. He says they are cruel. Hector always gave them good sacrifices, and now they will not even rescue his body so that his wife, mother, son, father, and people can see him and give him a funeral. They are siding with Achilles, who has thrown away all pity, like a savage lion. Apollo says that a man may lose someone dearer, even a son or a brother, but after weeping he lets the dead rest, because it takes much sorrow to kill a man. But Achilles keeps dragging Hector's body around the tomb. That does him no honor, and the gods may become angry with him, because he is attacking dead earth in his fury.

Hera answers angrily. Hector was a mortal who drank at a woman's breast, but Achilles is the son of a goddess, whom Hera herself raised and married to Peleus. All the gods came to that wedding, she reminds Apollo, and he played his lyre there. Zeus calms her. The two men will not receive equal honor, he says. But Hector was the dearest to the gods of all the Trojans, because he never failed to give Zeus offerings. Stealing the body is not possible without Achilles knowing, because Thetis is always near him. Instead, Zeus will call Thetis and ask her to tell Achilles to accept a ransom from Priam and give back the body.

Iris dives into the sea and finds Thetis in her cave, weeping for her son, who will soon die far from home. Thetis puts on a black veil and goes to Olympus. The gods welcome her kindly. Athena gives up her seat, and Hera hands her a golden cup. Zeus explains that the gods have been arguing for nine days about Hector's body. He tells Thetis to go to her son and tell him that the gods are angry, and Zeus is angriest of all, because he keeps Hector's body and will not give it back. Meanwhile, Zeus will send Iris to Priam.

Thetis finds Achilles still grieving. She sits beside him and strokes him. She asks how long he will go on eating his heart out, without food, sleep, or love, when he has so little time left to live. Then she gives him Zeus's message. Achilles agrees at once. If Zeus himself orders it, then whoever brings the ransom can take the body.

Zeus sends Iris to Priam with instructions. He must go alone to the Greek ships, with only one old servant to drive a wagon of gifts. He should not fear death. Hermes will guide him to Achilles's tent. And Achilles will not kill him, because he is not foolish or wicked, and he will treat a suppliant, someone who comes humbly to beg for help, with respect.

Iris finds Priam in his courtyard, surrounded by weeping sons. He is wrapped in his cloak, with his head and neck covered in the dirt he has rolled in. His daughters and daughters-in-law weep throughout the house. Iris speaks softly and gives him the message.

Priam tells his sons to prepare a wagon. Then he tells his wife Hecuba his plan. She cries out in horror. Has he lost his famous good sense? How can he go alone to the man who killed so many of his sons? That cruel man will show him no respect or pity. She says it was Hector's fate from birth to be eaten by dogs far from his parents. She wishes she could tear out Achilles's liver with her teeth and eat it, to avenge her brave son.

But Priam is determined. He has seen and heard the goddess himself, so he knows the message is true. And if he must die at the Greek ships, so be it. Let Achilles kill him, as long as he can first hold his son in his arms and weep over him.

He opens his chests and takes out rich gifts: twelve fine robes, twelve cloaks, twelve blankets, twelve mantles, twelve shirts, ten talents of gold, two tripods, four cauldrons, and a beautiful cup given to him by the Thracians. In his grief and anger, he drives the Trojans out of his courtyard with his staff. Then he shouts at his nine remaining sons, including Paris, Helenus, and Deiphobus. He calls them worthless. He wishes they had all died instead of Hector. His best sons are dead, he says, and only the liars and dancers and thieves are left. Frightened, his sons prepare the wagon with mules and load the treasure. They harness Priam's own horses to his chariot.

Hecuba brings him a golden cup of wine. She tells him to pour an offering to Zeus and pray for a sign: an eagle, Zeus's favorite bird, flying on his right. If no eagle comes, he should not go. Priam agrees. He washes his hands, pours the wine, and prays to be received with kindness and pity in Achilles's tent. Zeus hears him and sends a huge dark eagle, with wings as wide as the door of a rich man's house, flying on the right over the city. Everyone is glad. Priam drives out of the city, with the herald Idaeus driving the mule wagon in front. His family follows him, weeping as if he were going to his death, until they reach the plain and turn back.

Zeus sees the old man on the plain and pities him. He sends Hermes to guide him to Achilles without being seen by any Greek. Hermes ties on his golden sandals, which carry him over land and sea, and takes his magic wand, which can put people to sleep or wake them. He takes the shape of a handsome young prince.

It is getting dark. Priam and Idaeus stop to let the animals drink at the river. Idaeus sees the young man and is afraid. He suggests that they flee, or beg for mercy. Priam's hair stands on end. But Hermes takes Priam's hand kindly. He asks why the old man is traveling at night with so much treasure. He says he will protect Priam, because Priam reminds him of his own father. Priam says some god must be watching over him, to send such a wise and handsome young man.

Hermes pretends to be a Myrmidon, one of Achilles's men. He says he is the seventh son of a rich man, and he came to Troy on the same ship as Achilles. Priam asks anxiously whether his son's body is still at the ships, or whether Achilles has cut it up and fed it to the dogs. Hermes comforts him. The body has lain there for twelve days, but it has not decayed, and worms have not touched it. Achilles drags it around the tomb every morning, but it remains unharmed. The body is fresh as dew, the blood is washed away, and all the wounds have closed. This is how much the gods loved Hector. Priam says this shows that it is good to honor the gods, because Hector never forgot them. He offers Hermes a cup as a gift, but Hermes refuses, saying he would never take a gift behind his master's back.

Hermes jumps into the chariot and drives. At the Greek wall, he puts the guards to sleep and opens the gates. He leads Priam to Achilles's large hut, built of pine and roofed with grass, inside a fenced courtyard. The gate has a huge bar that takes three men to move, but Achilles can move it alone. Hermes opens it. Then he reveals that he is a god, sent by Zeus. He will not go inside, because Achilles might be angry to see a god openly helping a mortal. He tells Priam to take hold of Achilles's knees and beg him in the name of his father, his mother, and his son. Then Hermes returns to Olympus.

Priam goes in alone. Achilles has just finished eating, with two companions nearby. Nobody sees the old king enter. He goes straight to Achilles, takes hold of his knees, and kisses the terrible hands that have killed so many of his sons. Achilles is amazed, like people who see a stranger arrive suddenly to ask for protection after a killing in his own land. The others look at each other in surprise.

Then Priam speaks. This is one of the most moving speeches in all literature. He asks Achilles to remember his own father, who is old like Priam, on the sad edge of old age. Perhaps neighbors trouble Peleus, and there is no one to protect him. But at least he hears that his son is alive, and he hopes every day to see him come home. Priam had fifty sons when the Greeks came, nineteen of them from one mother. Most of them have been killed by war. And Hector, the one who protected the city and its people, Achilles has killed. So Priam has come to ransom his body. He asks Achilles to respect the gods and to pity him, remembering his own father. He says he is even more pitiful than Peleus. He has done something that no man on earth has ever done before: he has raised to his lips the hand of the man who killed his son.

These words touch Achilles's heart. He thinks of his father. He takes Priam's hand and gently pushes him away. Then the two men weep together. Priam, lying at Achilles's feet, weeps for Hector. Achilles weeps for his father, and then for Patroclus. The house is filled with their crying. In this moment, the killer and the father of the man he killed share the same human grief.

When Achilles has wept enough, he rises and lifts the old man by the hand, pitying his gray hair and beard. He says that Priam must have a heart of iron to come alone to the man who killed his sons. He asks him to sit, and says they should put their grief aside for now, because crying does no good. Then he explains how he sees human life. The gods live without care, but they give men lives full of sorrow. On the floor of Zeus's palace stand two jars, one full of evil gifts and the other full of good ones. To some men, Zeus gives a mixture, so their lives are sometimes good and sometimes bad. To others, he gives only evil, and they wander the earth, hungry and despised by gods and men. Peleus received great gifts from the gods: wealth, power, and a goddess for a wife. But he also received sorrow. He has only one son, who will die young, and that son cannot care for him in his old age, but sits far away at Troy, bringing grief to Priam and his children. Priam too, Achilles has heard, was once rich and happy, with many sons and a great kingdom. Then the gods sent this war upon him. Achilles tells him to be strong. Weeping will not bring Hector back. Priam will suffer another sorrow before that could happen.

Priam asks him not to make him sit while Hector lies uncared for. He begs Achilles to take the ransom and give him his son at once. He wishes Achilles a safe return home. Achilles looks at him sharply and warns him not to provoke him. He already intends to give back the body; his mother brought him Zeus's order. He also knows that a god must have brought Priam here, since no man could have passed the guards or opened his gate alone. Priam must not anger him, or he may break Zeus's command and hurt the old man, even though he is a suppliant. Here we see that Achilles's anger is still close to the surface, even in his moment of pity.

Priam is afraid and obeys. Achilles leaps out of the house like a lion, with his two companions. They unload the ransom but leave two cloaks and a shirt to wrap the body. Achilles orders the women servants to wash and oil Hector's body away from the hut, so that Priam will not see it. He is afraid that if Priam sees his son's body, he will cry out in anger, and then Achilles might lose control and kill him, breaking the command of Zeus. The servants wash the body and dress it. Then Achilles himself lifts Hector onto a stretcher and places him in the wagon. He groans and calls out to Patroclus. He asks his friend not to be angry if he hears, even in Hades, that Achilles has given Hector's body back to his father. The ransom is a worthy one, and he will give Patroclus his fair share of it.

Achilles returns to his seat. He tells Priam that his son is ready and that he can see him at dawn when he takes him home. Now they should eat. He tells the story of Niobe. She had six sons and six daughters, and she boasted that she had more children than the goddess Leto, who had only two. So Leto's children, Apollo and Artemis, killed all twelve. For nine days the bodies lay unburied, because Zeus had turned the people into stone. On the tenth day the gods buried them. Then even Niobe, worn out with weeping, remembered to eat. Now she is a rock on a mountain, still grieving. So, Achilles says, let us eat too. Priam can weep for his son later, as he carries him home. Hector will cost him many tears.

Achilles kills a white sheep, and his men cook it. They eat together. When they have finished, Priam looks at Achilles with wonder, because he is as tall and beautiful as a god. And Achilles looks at Priam with wonder, admiring his noble face and listening to his words. This moment of mutual respect between enemies is the emotional heart of the poem's ending.

Then Priam asks for a bed. He has not slept since Hector died. He has been rolling in the dirt of his courtyard, groaning, and only now has he eaten bread and drunk wine. Achilles orders beds to be made on the porch. With a kind of gentle humor, he tells Priam he must sleep outside, in case one of the Greek leaders comes in the night for advice and sees him. If Agamemnon found out, it might delay the return of the body. Then Achilles asks how many days the Trojans need for Hector's funeral, so that he can hold back the army. Priam explains that the Trojans are trapped in the city, and it is far to bring wood from the mountains. He asks for nine days of mourning, a funeral on the tenth day, a tomb on the eleventh, and on the twelfth, if necessary, they will fight again. Achilles agrees and promises to stop the war for that long. He takes the old man's right wrist so that he will not be afraid. Priam and Idaeus sleep on the porch. Achilles sleeps inside, with Briseis beside him.

All gods and men are asleep, except Hermes. He wakes Priam in the night. He reminds him that he is sleeping in the middle of his enemies. If Agamemnon learned he was there, Priam's remaining sons would have to pay three times the ransom to free him. Priam is afraid and wakes his servant. Hermes harnesses the animals and drives them quietly through the camp, so no one sees them. At the river ford, Hermes returns to Olympus as dawn begins.

Priam and Idaeus drive toward the city, weeping, with the mules pulling Hector's body. The first to see them is Cassandra, Priam's daughter, as beautiful as Aphrodite, standing on the high citadel. She sees her father and the herald, and then the body on the wagon. She cries out through the city and calls everyone to come and see Hector. If they ever rejoiced to see him come home alive from battle, they should come now to see the pride of their city.

Everyone in Troy comes out. At the gates, Andromache and Hecuba run to the wagon and hold Hector's head, while the crowd weeps. They would have stayed there weeping all day, but Priam asks them to let the wagon pass; they can weep fully once he is home. In the palace, they lay Hector on a bed. Singers lead the songs of mourning, and the women answer them. Three women speak in turn.

First Andromache, holding his head. She says that he died young and has left her a widow, with a son who is still a baby and may never grow up. Before that, the city will fall, because its protector is gone. The women will be taken to the ships as slaves, and she with them. Her son will have to do hard work for a cruel master. Or some Greek, angry because Hector killed his brother or son or father, may throw the child from the city walls. Then she adds her deepest sorrow. Hector did not die in his bed, stretching out his arms to her. He did not say any last words that she could remember forever.

Next Hecuba. She says Hector was the dearest of all her sons, and the gods loved him even in death. When Achilles caught her other sons, he sold them overseas. He dragged Hector around his friend's tomb many times, but it could not bring Patroclus back. And now Hector lies fresh as dew, beautiful, like someone killed by Apollo's gentle arrows.

Finally Helen speaks. She says that Hector was the dearest of all her brothers-in-law. Twenty years have passed since she left her home, and in all that time she never heard one unkind word from him. When others in the family spoke harshly to her, Hector stopped them with gentle words. Priam too was always kind to her, like a father. Now she weeps for Hector and for herself, because no one else in Troy is kind to her. Everyone turns away from her in horror. The crowd weeps with her.

Then Priam tells the Trojans to bring wood without fear, because Achilles has promised that the Greeks will not attack until the twelfth day. For nine days they gather wood. On the tenth day, weeping, they place Hector on top of the pyre and light the fire. On the eleventh day, they put out the fire with wine. His brothers and friends gather his white bones, wrap them in soft purple cloth, and place them in a golden box. They put it in a grave and cover it with large stones. Quickly, they build a mound over it, while guards watch in case the Greeks attack. Then they return to the city and hold a great funeral feast in Priam's palace.

The poem ends with the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses. It does not show the death of Achilles or the fall of Troy, though we know both are coming. Instead, the Iliad ends in a pause in the war: a moment of shared grief, mercy, and respect between enemies, and a proper burial for a fallen hero. The anger of Achilles, which began the poem, has finally given way to pity.

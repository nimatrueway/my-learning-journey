# The Odyssey

Homer

## Before You Begin

The Odyssey is one of the oldest stories in Western literature. It is a long poem from ancient Greece, and people say it was made by a poet called Homer. We know very little about Homer. The Greeks believed he was a blind singer who lived many centuries before them, perhaps around the eighth century before the common era. Today, scholars are not sure that one single man called Homer ever existed. The poem may have grown over many generations, as singers performed it from memory and passed it on, before it was finally written down. So when this summary says "Homer" or "the poet", it simply means the voice that tells the story.

The Odyssey follows the Trojan War. In that war, the Greeks sailed to the city of Troy to bring back Helen, the wife of King Menelaus, who had gone away with the Trojan prince Paris. The war lasted ten years. Another famous poem, the Iliad, also said to be by Homer, tells part of that war. The Odyssey begins after the war is over. Most of the Greek heroes have either died or returned home. But one hero is still missing. His name is Odysseus, king of the small, rocky island of Ithaca. He is famous above all for his cleverness, his tricks, and his patience.

When the poem begins, Odysseus has been away from home for nearly twenty years: ten years fighting at Troy, and almost ten more years lost at sea. At home, his wife Penelope has waited for him faithfully. But many young lords from Ithaca and the nearby islands now believe Odysseus is dead. They have come to his house as suitors. A suitor is a man who wants to marry a woman. These suitors want to marry Penelope and take Odysseus's place. While they wait for her to choose, they live in the palace, eat his cattle and sheep, drink his wine, and behave rudely. His young son, Telemachus, is not yet strong enough to stop them.

The poem is not told in simple time order. It starts in the middle. The first four books follow Telemachus, as he grows up and travels to find news of his father. Then we meet Odysseus himself, trapped on a far island. In Books 9 to 12, Odysseus tells the story of his past adventures to a kind people called the Phaeacians. This is a flashback, a part of the story that goes back in time, and here Odysseus is the storyteller. He describes the one-eyed giant called the Cyclops, the witch Circe, the land of the dead, the singing Sirens, and other dangers. In the second half of the poem, Odysseus finally reaches Ithaca. He disguises himself as an old beggar, tests who is loyal and who is not, and takes his revenge on the suitors. At the end he is reunited with his wife and his father, and the gods bring peace.

Here are the main people you will meet. Odysseus is the clever hero who wants to go home. Penelope is his wise and faithful wife, who is as clever as he is. Telemachus is their son, a young man learning to be brave. Laertes is the old father of Odysseus, who lives sadly on his farm. Eumaeus is the loyal pig-keeper who welcomes Odysseus when he returns in disguise. Eurycleia is the old nurse who cared for Odysseus as a baby and later for Telemachus. Among the suitors, Antinous is the most arrogant and cruel, and Eurymachus is smooth and dishonest. Nestor is the old and wise king of Pylos, and Menelaus is the king of Sparta, who lives again with his wife Helen after the war. Calypso is a goddess who keeps Odysseus on her island for seven years because she wants to marry him. Circe is a powerful goddess and witch who turns men into animals. Alcinous is the king of the Phaeacians, and Nausicaa is his young daughter, who finds Odysseus on the beach.

The gods also play a big part in the story. Zeus is the king of the gods. Athena, the goddess of wisdom, is the great friend and protector of Odysseus and his son, and she often appears in disguise. Poseidon, the god of the sea, hates Odysseus because Odysseus blinded his son, the Cyclops, so he keeps him away from home. Hermes is the messenger of the gods, who carries their orders and guides the souls of the dead.

This summary is based on the prose translation by Samuel Butler, made in 1900, which you can read freely at [Project Gutenberg's edition of Samuel Butler's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727). Butler uses the Latin names for many characters and gods. He calls Odysseus "Ulysses", Zeus "Jove", Athena "Minerva", Poseidon "Neptune", and Hermes "Mercury". This summary uses the Greek names instead, because they are the names most readers know today. If you read Butler's text, just remember that these pairs of names mean the same people.

As you read, look out for a few big ideas. One is hospitality, the sacred duty to welcome and protect guests and strangers, which good people honour and the suitors abuse. Another is loyalty, seen in Penelope, Eumaeus, and the old dog Argos. A third is cleverness and patience, because Odysseus wins not only by strength but by thinking, waiting, and hiding his feelings. And above all, there is the longing for home, which pulls Odysseus across the sea, past monsters and goddesses, back to the island and the family he loves.

## Book 1: Athena Visits Telemachus

The poem begins with a request to the Muse, the goddess who helps poets remember and sing. The poet asks her to tell the story of a clever hero who travelled far after the fall of Troy. This hero is Odysseus. He saw many cities and suffered much at sea. He tried hard to bring his men home, but he could not save them. They died because of their own foolishness, when they ate the cattle of the Sun god. So from the very first lines, we know how the story of his crew will end.

Now all the other Greek heroes who survived the war have reached home or died. Only Odysseus is still away. The goddess Calypso keeps him in her cave on a far island, because she wants to marry him. Years have passed. Most of the gods now feel sorry for him. Only Poseidon, the god of the sea, is still angry and keeps him from home.

One day Poseidon is far away, visiting the Ethiopians at the edge of the world, where they are offering him a great sacrifice. The other gods meet in the house of Zeus on Mount Olympus. Zeus speaks first. He is thinking about Aegisthus, a man who took the wife of King Agamemnon and then killed Agamemnon when he came home from Troy. Zeus had sent Hermes, the messenger god, to warn Aegisthus, but Aegisthus did not listen. Later Orestes, Agamemnon's son, grew up and killed him. Zeus complains that people blame the gods for their troubles, when often their pain comes from their own bad choices. This story of Agamemnon, his wife, and his son Orestes will return many times in the Odyssey. It is a dark mirror of Odysseus's own family.

Athena, the goddess of wisdom, answers. She agrees that Aegisthus deserved his fate, but she says her heart breaks for Odysseus. He is trapped on a lonely island by Calypso, the daughter of Atlas. Calypso tries to make him forget his home, but he only wants to see the smoke rising from his own land again. Athena asks Zeus why he is angry with Odysseus, who always gave the gods good sacrifices.

Zeus says he has not forgotten Odysseus. He explains that Poseidon is angry because Odysseus blinded Polyphemus, a Cyclops and Poseidon's son. Poseidon will not kill Odysseus, but he keeps him away from home. Still, Zeus says, if all the gods agree, Poseidon cannot fight against them. So they decide that Odysseus must go home. Athena makes a plan. First, Hermes should go to Calypso and tell her to let Odysseus go. Second, Athena herself will go to Ithaca, Odysseus's island home, to give courage to his son Telemachus. She will help him call a public meeting and speak against the suitors. A suitor is a man who wants to marry a woman. Here, the suitors are many young lords who want to marry Penelope, Odysseus's wife, and who eat his food every day. Athena will also send Telemachus to Pylos and Sparta to look for news of his father, so that people will respect him.

Athena puts on her golden sandals, which carry her over land and sea, takes her strong spear, and flies down to Ithaca. She appears at the gate of Odysseus's house disguised as a visitor named Mentes, the chief of the Taphians. She sees the suitors sitting on the skins of cattle they have killed and eaten. They are playing games while servants prepare more food and wine for them.

Telemachus sees the stranger first. He is sitting sadly among the suitors, dreaming that his father will come back and drive them out. He is upset that a guest has been left waiting at the gate, so he goes to greet the visitor, takes the spear, and brings the guest inside. He gives the stranger a fine seat away from the noisy suitors, and servants bring water, bread, meat, and wine. This scene shows hospitality, which means being kind and generous to guests and strangers. In Homer's world this is a sacred duty, and Telemachus does it well, while the suitors abuse it.

The suitors come in, eat, and drink. Then they ask for music. They force the singer Phemius to sing for them. While he sings, Telemachus speaks quietly to his guest. He complains that these men live freely at the cost of his father, whose bones may be lying somewhere far away. He asks the stranger who he is. Athena, still as Mentes, says she is the king of the Taphians, sailing with a cargo of iron to trade for copper. She says her family and Odysseus's family are old friends, and that old Laertes, Odysseus's father, can confirm this. She says Laertes now lives alone in the country, working in his vineyard with an old woman to care for him. She tells Telemachus that Odysseus is not dead. He is probably held on an island, and he is so clever that he will find a way home soon. She also says Telemachus looks very much like his father.

Telemachus answers sadly that his mother says he is the son of Odysseus, but he wishes his father had been a happier man. Athena asks about the wild feast. Telemachus explains that the chief men of Ithaca and the nearby islands are eating up his house while they court his mother. Penelope does not refuse them, and she does not choose one either. So they waste his property.

Athena becomes angry for him. She says that if Odysseus came back armed, the suitors would have a short life and a bitter wedding. Then she gives clear advice. Tomorrow Telemachus should call an assembly, a public meeting of the people. He should tell the suitors to go home. If Penelope wants to marry, she should go back to her father's house. Then Telemachus should take a good ship with twenty men and go to Pylos to ask old King Nestor, and then to Sparta to ask King Menelaus, who was the last Greek to return from Troy. If he hears that his father is alive, he can endure the suitors for one more year. If he hears his father is dead, he should come home, hold a funeral, and let his mother marry again. After that, he must think about how to kill the suitors, by tricks or by open force. Athena reminds him of Orestes, who became famous for punishing his father's killer. She tells him he is no longer a child.

Telemachus thanks the guest and offers a fine gift, but Athena says to keep it for her next visit. Then she flies away like a bird. Telemachus feels new courage, and he understands that the visitor was a god.

Phemius is singing about the sad return of the Greeks from Troy. Penelope hears the song from her room upstairs. She comes down with two maids, holding a veil in front of her face, and weeping. She asks Phemius to sing something else, because this song reminds her of her lost husband. But Telemachus speaks firmly. He says the singer is not to blame for the sorrows he sings about. Zeus sends good and bad fortune. Many men did not return from Troy, not only Odysseus. He tells his mother to go back to her own work, because speaking in the hall is the business of men, and he is master of the house now. Penelope is surprised by this new tone. She goes upstairs and cries for her husband until Athena sends her sleep.

Then Telemachus speaks to the suitors. He calls them shameless. He tells them to come to the assembly in the morning, where he will order them to leave and eat at their own houses. If they keep on eating his family's wealth, he says, Zeus will punish them. The suitors are surprised at his boldness. Antinous, son of Eupeithes and the rudest of the suitors, mocks him and hopes he will never be king of Ithaca. Telemachus answers calmly that he would like to rule, but at least he will be master of his own house. Eurymachus, son of Polybus, another leading suitor, speaks more smoothly. He says no one will rob Telemachus, and then asks who the stranger was. Telemachus says the visitor was Mentes, an old friend of his father. He hides the truth, but in his heart he knows it was the goddess.

At night the suitors go home. Telemachus goes up to his room. The old nurse Eurycleia lights his way with torches. Laertes bought her long ago and respected her greatly. She nursed Telemachus when he was a baby and loves him more than any other woman in the house does. She folds his clothes, closes his door, and leaves. All night Telemachus lies awake, thinking about his journey and Athena's advice. This first book shows a young man starting to grow up and take his father's place.

## Book 2: The Assembly and a Secret Departure

At dawn Telemachus gets up, puts on his sword, and sends the town criers to call the people to an assembly. He walks to the meeting place with a spear in his hand and his two dogs beside him. Athena makes him look so handsome and noble that everyone admires him. When he sits in his father's seat, even the old men make room for him.

The first speaker is Aegyptius, a very old man. One of his sons, Antiphus, went to Troy with Odysseus and was later eaten by the Cyclops, although the old man does not know this yet. Another son, Eurynomus, is one of the suitors. Aegyptius says there has been no assembly since Odysseus left, about twenty years ago. He asks who has called the people together, and whether an enemy army is coming.

Telemachus stands up. A herald gives him the staff, which a speaker holds while he has the right to speak. He says he has called the meeting because of his own private trouble, not public news. He has two great misfortunes. First, he has lost his father, who was like a kind father to all of them. Second, the sons of the chief families are forcing his mother to think about marriage. They do not go properly to her father Icarius to ask for her hand. Instead, they stay in Odysseus's house every day, kill his animals, and drink his wine. Telemachus says no house can survive this. He asks the people to feel shame, to respect public opinion, and to fear the anger of the gods. He even says that it would be better if the people of Ithaca ate his wealth themselves, because then he could at least ask them to pay it back. Then he throws the staff to the ground and bursts into tears. Everyone feels sorry for him, but nobody moves.

Only Antinous, the boldest suitor, answers. He says the fault belongs to Penelope, not to the suitors. For more than three years she has given each suitor hope and sent them messages, but she never meant what she said. Then he describes her famous trick. Penelope set up a large loom in her room and began to weave a burial cloth for Laertes, Odysseus's old father. She told the suitors she would choose a husband when the cloth was finished. Every day she wove, and every night, by torchlight, she secretly undid the work. She fooled them like this for three years. In the fourth year a maid told the suitors, and they caught her undoing the cloth, so she had to finish it. The weaving trick shows that Penelope is as clever as her husband. Antinous says that the suitors will stay and eat Telemachus's wealth until Penelope marries one of them. He tells Telemachus to send her back to her father.

Telemachus refuses. He cannot throw his own mother out of the house. He would have to pay her father a large sum. Also, his mother would call on the Erinyes to punish him. The Erinyes are the Furies, angry goddesses who punish crimes against family. He repeats that the suitors should leave and eat in their own houses, or Zeus will make them pay.

At that moment Zeus sends a sign. Two eagles fly down from the mountain over the assembly. They circle, glare at the people below, tear at each other with their claws, and then fly away to the right over the town. Halitherses, an old man who is skilled at reading omens, explains it. An omen is a sign from the gods about the future. He warns the suitors that Odysseus is near and will bring death to them. He reminds everyone that when Odysseus left for Troy, he predicted that Odysseus would lose all his men and come home in the twentieth year, unknown to everyone. Now this is coming true, he says.

Eurymachus, the smoother suitor, mocks the old man. He says birds fly everywhere, and most of them mean nothing. Odysseus is dead, he says, and he wishes Halitherses were dead with him. He threatens to fine the old man. He repeats that Telemachus must send his mother to her father, and that the suitors fear no one.

Telemachus then stops asking. He says the gods and the people now know his story. He asks only for a ship and twenty men so he can go to Sparta and Pylos to look for news of his father. If his father is alive, he will endure the suitors one more year. If his father is dead, he will hold a funeral and let his mother marry.

Next Mentor speaks. Mentor is an old friend of Odysseus, who left him in charge of the household. He is angry with the people of Ithaca more than with the suitors. Odysseus ruled them gently like a father, yet now they sit silently while his house is robbed. The people are many and the suitors are few, so they could stop this if they wished. But a suitor named Leiocritus replies rudely. He says even Odysseus himself could not fight so many men, and if he came back he would die. He tells the people to go home and leaves Mentor and Halitherses to help the boy. He doubts that Telemachus will really go. Then he ends the meeting. The assembly has failed. The people do nothing, and the suitors go back to the palace.

Telemachus walks alone to the seashore, washes his hands in the sea, and prays to the god who visited him. Athena comes to him now in the shape and voice of Mentor. She encourages him. If he has his father's spirit, he will not be a fool or a coward, and his journey will succeed. She warns him to have nothing to do with the suitors, because they will all die on one day. She promises to find a ship and a crew and to sail with him. She tells him to go home and prepare food and wine.

At the palace, Antinous takes his hand and laughs. He invites Telemachus to eat and drink with them as before, and says the people will give him a ship. Telemachus pulls his hand away. He says he can no longer enjoy food with such men, and he will hurt them however he can. The other suitors joke about him. One says he will bring fighters from Pylos or poison from Ephyra. Another says he might die at sea like his father, and then they could share his property.

Telemachus goes down to the storeroom, where his father's gold, bronze, clothes, oil, and best wine are kept. The old nurse Eurycleia guards it. He asks her for twelve jars of good wine and twenty measures of barley meal in leather bags. He tells her to keep the best wine for his father, in case he returns. When she hears about the journey she cries and begs him not to go. She fears the suitors will plan to kill him while he is away. Telemachus tells her that a god supports his plan. He makes her swear not to tell his mother for ten or twelve days, so that Penelope will not weep.

Meanwhile Athena works hard. She takes Telemachus's shape and goes around the town gathering a crew. She borrows a ship from a man named Noemon. At sunset she launches the ship. Then she goes to the palace and makes the suitors so sleepy that they drop their cups and go home early to bed. In the form of Mentor she calls Telemachus. They load the supplies. Telemachus tells the crew that his mother does not know, and only one servant does. Athena sits at the back of the ship with Telemachus beside her. She sends a good west wind. The men raise the mast and the white sails, and the ship runs through the dark water. They pour libations to the gods, especially to Athena. A libation is an offering of wine poured out for a god. All night the ship sails on toward Pylos. Telemachus has taken his first real step as a man of action.

## Book 3: Telemachus Visits Nestor at Pylos

As the sun rises, Telemachus and his crew arrive at Pylos, the sandy city of old King Nestor. On the beach, the people of Pylos are holding a great sacrifice to Poseidon. There are nine groups of five hundred men, and each group offers nine black bulls. The men are eating the inner parts of the animals and burning the thigh bones for the god.

Athena, still disguised as old Mentor, tells Telemachus not to be shy. He has come to learn about his father, so he must go straight to Nestor and ask him to tell the truth. Telemachus is nervous. He is young and not used to speaking with important older men. Athena tells him that some words will come from his own mind and some from the gods, who have always been with him.

They walk up to Nestor, who is sitting with his sons. The people welcome the strangers warmly. Nestor's youngest son, Pisistratus, takes their hands and seats them on soft sheepskins. He gives them meat and a golden cup of wine. He hands the cup to Mentor first, because Mentor is older, and asks the guest to pray to Poseidon, since this is Poseidon's feast. Athena is pleased by his good manners. She prays to Poseidon for Nestor, his sons, and the people of Pylos, and for success on their own journey. It is a little funny that the goddess herself prays to another god. Then Telemachus prays too, and everyone eats.

After the meal Nestor asks the visitors who they are. Are they traders, or pirates? It is polite in this world to feed a guest first and ask questions later. Telemachus speaks boldly now. He says he comes from Ithaca and wants news of his father Odysseus. People know what happened to the other heroes of Troy, but nobody knows whether Odysseus is alive or dead. He begs Nestor to tell him the plain truth, even if it is painful.

Nestor says Telemachus brings back sad memories. Many of the best Greeks died at Troy, including Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus, and Nestor's own son Antilochus. The war lasted nine long years. No one was as clever as Odysseus. Nestor says Telemachus even speaks like his father. He and Odysseus always agreed in council.

Then Nestor tells what happened after Troy fell. Athena was angry with the Greeks, because some of them had behaved without wisdom. She caused a quarrel between the two sons of Atreus, the brothers Agamemnon and Menelaus. They called a badly timed meeting at sunset, when the men were drunk. Menelaus wanted to sail home at once. Agamemnon wanted to stay and make sacrifices to calm Athena. Nestor calls this foolish, because the gods do not change their minds easily. The army split in two. Half stayed with Agamemnon. Nestor's half sailed away. At the island of Tenedos there was another quarrel. Odysseus turned his ships back to rejoin Agamemnon. Nestor, Diomedes, and later Menelaus went on. The gods showed them a sign to cross the open sea, and they made good time. Diomedes reached Argos, and Nestor came home safely to Pylos. So Nestor knows nothing about Odysseus after that point.

Nestor gives what news he has heard. Achilles's son brought his men home, and so did Philoctetes and Idomeneus. Then he speaks of Agamemnon, who was murdered by Aegisthus, and of Orestes, who later killed the murderer. Nestor says how good it is for a man to leave a son like Orestes behind. He tells Telemachus to be brave and make a name for himself.

Telemachus says he wishes he could take revenge on the suitors, but he fears the gods have no such good luck for him. Nestor says he has heard about the suitors. He asks whether the people or the gods are against Telemachus, and says that Odysseus might come back and punish them. He remembers how openly Athena loved Odysseus at Troy. If she loved Telemachus like that, the suitors would soon forget their wooing. Telemachus says this hope is too great.

Athena, as Mentor, scolds him gently. The gods can easily save a man, even from far away. She says she would rather suffer much and come home safely, than come home quickly and be murdered like Agamemnon. But she also admits that death comes to everyone, and even the gods cannot stop it when a person's time comes.

Telemachus does not want to talk more about his father's return, which he thinks is hopeless. Instead he asks Nestor a question. How exactly did Agamemnon die? Where was Menelaus, his brother? Nestor explains. While the Greeks were fighting at Troy, Aegisthus stayed at home and flattered Clytemnestra, Agamemnon's wife. At first she refused him, because she was good by nature. Agamemnon had also left a singer to guard her. But Aegisthus took the singer to a lonely island and left him to die. Then Clytemnestra went to Aegisthus willingly. Aegisthus made many offerings to the gods because he had succeeded beyond his hopes.

Meanwhile Menelaus was delayed on his way home. Apollo killed his excellent helmsman near Athens, and Menelaus stopped to bury him. Then Zeus sent a great storm near Cape Malea. Half of his ships were wrecked on the rocks of Crete. The other five ships were blown to Egypt, where Menelaus collected much gold. During this time Aegisthus ruled in Mycenae for seven years. In the eighth year Orestes returned from Athens and killed him. Orestes held a funeral feast for his mother and for Aegisthus, and on that same day Menelaus came home with his treasure. Nestor says that if Menelaus had found Aegisthus alive, the man would not even have had a grave.

Nestor warns Telemachus not to stay away from home too long while dangerous men are in his house. But he advises him to visit Menelaus in Sparta, because Menelaus has just come back from very far away. Nestor offers a chariot, horses, and one of his sons as a guide.

The sun sets. Athena reminds them to finish the religious ceremony and go to bed early. They burn the tongues of the animals and pour wine for the gods. Athena and Telemachus start to go back to their ship, but Nestor stops them. He will not allow the son of his old friend to sleep on a ship's deck, as if Nestor had no blankets. Athena agrees that Telemachus should stay. She says she must return to the young crew and then travel to collect a debt. She asks Nestor to send Telemachus to Sparta with a chariot and one of his sons. Then she flies away in the form of an eagle.

Everyone is amazed. Nestor takes Telemachus's hand. He says the young man will be a great hero, since the gods travel with him already. He recognizes the goddess Athena and prays to her. He promises to sacrifice a young cow with gilded horns. That night, Telemachus sleeps in Nestor's palace beside Pisistratus.

In the morning, Nestor sits on the white stone seat in front of his house. His six sons gather. He sends them on errands to prepare the sacrifice to Athena. A heifer is brought from the fields, the crew of Telemachus is invited, and a goldsmith covers the animal's horns with gold. Athena herself comes to accept the offering. Nestor prays and the heifer is killed. The women of the family cry out in the ritual way. Thigh bones wrapped in fat are burned for the goddess, and the rest of the meat is roasted for the people. Polycaste, Nestor's youngest daughter, bathes Telemachus and gives him fine clothes.

After the meal, Nestor orders his sons to harness the horses. The housekeeper packs food. Telemachus and Pisistratus drive off together. They stop for the night at Pherae, where a man named Diocles hosts them. Next morning they drive on across wheat fields, and as the sun sets they reach the end of their journey. This book is full of hospitality and religious respect. Nestor's well-ordered house is the opposite of the disorder in Odysseus's palace.

## Book 4: Menelaus and Helen in Sparta, and the Suitors' Plot

Telemachus and Pisistratus, Nestor's son, arrive at Sparta, the city of King Menelaus. They find the palace full of guests. Menelaus is holding a double wedding feast. His daughter Hermione, the only child of Helen, is being sent to marry the son of Achilles, as Menelaus promised long ago at Troy. His son Megapenthes, born to a slave woman, is marrying a girl from Sparta. A singer plays music and two acrobats dance in the hall.

A servant named Eteoneus sees the two young strangers at the gate. He runs to ask Menelaus whether to let them in or send them somewhere else. Menelaus is angry at the question. He reminds the servant that they themselves often received help from strangers on their long journey home. So the horses are fed, and the young men are brought in. They are amazed by the palace, which shines with bronze, gold, amber, ivory, and silver. They are bathed and dressed, and they eat with the king. Menelaus says they must come from a family of kings.

Telemachus whispers to Pisistratus that the house looks like the palace of Zeus himself. Menelaus hears him. He says no man can compare with Zeus, but he admits he is very rich. He gathered this wealth during eight years of wandering, in Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt, Ethiopia, and Libya. But his wealth gives him no joy, because while he travelled, his brother Agamemnon was murdered at home by the plot of his wicked wife. He says he would gladly have only a third of his riches if the men who died at Troy could live again. He grieves for all of them, but most of all for one man, Odysseus, who worked and suffered more than anyone. No one knows if he is alive or dead, and his old father, his patient wife Penelope, and his son Telemachus all mourn for him.

When Telemachus hears his father's name, he cries and hides his face behind his cloak. Menelaus notices and wonders whether to ask him about it. At that moment Helen comes down from her room, as beautiful as a goddess. She is the famous queen whose escape to Troy with the Trojan prince Paris started the Trojan War. Her maids bring her a chair and a silver basket of wool, a gift from Egypt. Helen looks at the young man and says she has never seen anyone so much like another person. He must be Telemachus, the baby Odysseus left behind when the Greeks went to Troy because of her own shameful behaviour. Menelaus agrees. He sees Odysseus in the boy's hands, feet, hair, and eyes, and he saw him cry.

Pisistratus confirms that this is Telemachus. He explains that his friend is shy, and that he has come for advice because there is no one at home to protect him. Menelaus is deeply moved. He says he loved Odysseus. He had planned to give him a city in Argos, so they could live close together as friends. But the gods did not allow Odysseus to come home. Everyone weeps: Helen, Telemachus, Menelaus, and Pisistratus too, who remembers his dead brother Antilochus. Pisistratus politely says he does not like to cry during dinner, and they can mourn the dead in the morning. Menelaus praises his good sense and says he is truly Nestor's son.

Then Helen puts a drug into the wine. It is an Egyptian herb that takes away all sorrow. Anyone who drinks it cannot cry for the rest of the day, even if his parents die in front of him. After this, Helen tells a story about Odysseus at Troy. He once hurt his own body, dressed in rags, and slipped into the city of Troy disguised as a beggar. No one recognized him except Helen. She questioned him, but he was too clever to answer. After she bathed him and swore not to betray him, he told her the Greek plans. He killed many Trojans on his way out. Helen says she was glad, because by then she wanted to go home, and she regretted that the goddess of love had taken her away from her husband and daughter. This story matters, because it shows Odysseus using disguise, which he will do again when he returns home.

Menelaus answers with another story, and it is less flattering to Helen. When the Greek heroes were hiding inside the wooden horse, Helen came and walked around it three times. She called the names of the Greek chiefs and imitated the voices of their wives. Menelaus and Diomedes almost jumped out or answered. One man, Anticlus, started to reply, but Odysseus pressed his strong hands over the man's mouth until Helen left. This saved them all. The two stories show Odysseus's cleverness and self-control. Telemachus says it is sad that all this courage could not save his father. Then the guests go to bed in the gatehouse.

In the morning Menelaus asks Telemachus why he came. Telemachus explains that the suitors are eating his property, and he asks for any true news of his father. Menelaus is shocked. He compares the suitors to a deer that leaves her babies in a lion's den. When the lion comes back, he kills them all. Odysseus will do the same to the suitors.

Then Menelaus tells his long story. On his way home, the gods held him back in Egypt, because he had not offered them enough sacrifices. He was stuck for twenty days on the island of Pharos, with no wind, and his men were starving. A sea goddess named Eidothea, called Idothea in Butler, took pity on him. She told him about her father Proteus, the old man of the sea, who serves Poseidon and knows everything. If Menelaus could catch him and hold him, Proteus would tell him how to get home and what had happened at home.

The goddess gave Menelaus and three of his best men fresh seal skins and hid them in holes in the sand among the seals. The smell was terrible, but she put sweet ambrosia, the food of the gods, under their noses. At noon Proteus came out of the sea, counted his seals, including the disguised men, and lay down to sleep. The men jumped on him and held him. He changed into a lion, a snake, a leopard, a wild boar, running water, and a tree. But they held on, until he gave up and spoke.

Proteus told Menelaus that he must sail back to the river of Egypt and offer proper sacrifices to the gods. Only then could he go home. Menelaus then asked about the other Greeks. Proteus said two leaders died on the way home, and a third was still alive but trapped at sea. The lesser Ajax was shipwrecked, but he was saved, until he boasted that he had escaped the sea against the gods' will. Poseidon split the rock he was sitting on, and he drowned. Agamemnon reached home safely, kissed the ground, and cried with joy. But a watchman of Aegisthus saw him. Aegisthus hid twenty men, invited Agamemnon to a feast, and killed him like an ox at the manger. All of Agamemnon's men died, and all of Aegisthus's men too.

Menelaus wept on the sand when he heard this. Proteus told him to hurry home, because he might still find Aegisthus alive, or at least arrive for the funeral. Then Menelaus asked about the third man. Proteus said it was Odysseus. He was on an island, weeping in the house of the nymph Calypso, who kept him prisoner. He had no ships and no crew to take him home. This is the first real news Telemachus hears about his father. Proteus also told Menelaus that he would not die. Because he is married to Helen, the daughter of Zeus, the gods will send him to the Elysian plain at the end of the world, where life is easy and there is no snow or rain. Menelaus did as Proteus said, made the sacrifices in Egypt, built a memorial mound for Agamemnon, and sailed home.

Menelaus invites Telemachus to stay ten or twelve more days. He offers him a chariot and three horses. Telemachus politely refuses. He would happily stay a year listening to Menelaus, but his crew is waiting at Pylos. Also, Ithaca is rocky and has no flat land for horses. It is good for goats, and he loves it that way. Menelaus smiles and says this answer shows his good family. Instead he will give him a beautiful silver mixing bowl with a gold rim, made by Hephaestus, the smith god.

Now the story moves back to Ithaca. The suitors are throwing discs and spears in front of the palace. Noemon, the man who lent his ship to Telemachus, asks Antinous when Telemachus will return, because he needs his ship. The suitors are shocked. They thought Telemachus was just on a farm somewhere. Noemon says the crew was made of the best young men, and Mentor went as captain. But strangely, Noemon saw Mentor in town the day before. Antinous, full of rage, says this journey is dangerous for them. He asks for a ship and twenty men. He will hide in the strait between Ithaca and Samos and kill Telemachus on his way home.

The herald Medon overhears the plot and tells Penelope. At first she thinks he has come with orders from the suitors, and she angrily says Odysseus was always fair and kind to everyone. Then Medon tells her that the suitors plan to murder her son, who has gone to Pylos and Sparta. Penelope did not even know he had left. She falls to the floor and weeps, and all her maids cry with her. She blames them for not telling her. She wants to send the old gardener Dolius to tell Laertes.

Eurycleia, the old nurse, admits that she knew. She gave Telemachus the supplies and swore to keep the secret. She tells Penelope to wash, change her clothes, and pray to Athena, and not to trouble old Laertes, who already has enough sorrow. Penelope prays to Athena to save her son. The suitors hear her cry out and think she is preparing to marry one of them. Antinous warns them to keep quiet. He takes twenty men, prepares the ship, and waits for night.

Penelope lies in bed without eating, like a lioness trapped by hunters. At last she sleeps. Athena sends her a dream in the shape of her sister Iphthime. The dream tells her not to cry, because her son has done nothing wrong to the gods and will come home. It says Athena herself is with him. Penelope asks about her husband, but the dream will not say whether he is alive or dead. It disappears, and Penelope wakes up comforted.

Meanwhile the suitors sail out and hide behind a small rocky island called Asteris, between Ithaca and Samos, waiting to kill Telemachus. The first part of the poem ends with this danger hanging over the young man.

## Book 5: Calypso Lets Odysseus Go

Now, for the first time, we meet Odysseus himself. The book begins with another meeting of the gods at dawn. Athena again speaks about Odysseus's suffering. She repeats Mentor's angry words from the assembly in Ithaca: the people have forgotten a king who was as kind as a father. Odysseus is lying in pain on Calypso's island, with no ship and no crew, and now wicked men want to murder his son Telemachus on his way home. Zeus answers that Athena herself sent Telemachus on his journey, and she is able to protect him. Then Zeus gives his orders.

He sends Hermes to Calypso. Odysseus must go home, but without help from gods or men. He will sail for twenty days on a raft and suffer much. Then he will reach Scheria, the land of the Phaeacians, a people who live close to the gods. They will honour him like a god, send him home in a ship, and give him more bronze, gold, and clothing than he would have brought back from Troy.

Hermes ties on his golden sandals, takes his magic wand, and flies over the sea like a seabird. He reaches Calypso's far island and walks to her cave. The place is very beautiful. A fire of cedar wood smells sweet. Calypso is weaving with a golden shuttle and singing. Around the cave grow trees full of birds, and a vine heavy with grapes hangs over the entrance. Four springs water soft meadows of violets. Even Hermes, a god, stops to admire it. This beauty is important, because it shows what Odysseus is giving up to go home.

Calypso recognizes Hermes at once and offers him ambrosia and nectar, the food and drink of the gods. Odysseus is not there. As usual, he is sitting on the shore, crying and looking out at the empty sea. Hermes explains that Zeus sent him. He did not want to come so far, where there are no cities to give him sacrifices. Zeus orders Calypso to free the man, because he is not meant to die far from home.

Calypso shakes with anger. She says the gods are jealous whenever a goddess loves a mortal man. She gives examples. When the goddess Dawn loved Orion, the goddess Artemis killed him. When Demeter loved Iasion, Zeus killed him with lightning. Now they want to take her man too. She says she saved Odysseus when he was holding on to a piece of his broken ship after Zeus destroyed it with lightning and all his crew drowned. She loved him and wanted to make him immortal, so that he would never grow old. But no one can go against Zeus, so she agrees. She has no ship or crew to give him, but she will give him honest advice. Hermes warns her to obey, and leaves.

Calypso goes to the beach. Odysseus is crying with homesickness. The poem says plainly that he no longer loves her. At night he sleeps with her in the cave because she wants it, not because he does. By day he sits on the rocks and weeps. Calypso tells him he can leave. He should cut wood and build a raft, and she will give him food, wine, water, clothes, and a good wind.

Odysseus does not trust her. He shivers. He thinks she must be planning something bad, because sailing so far on a raft is terribly dangerous. He asks her to swear a great oath that she means him no harm. Calypso smiles, touches him, and says he is very clever, but wrong this time. She swears by the river Styx, the most serious oath a god can make, that she means him no harm. Her heart is not made of iron, she says, and she feels sorry for him. This is a typical moment for Odysseus. He is always careful and suspicious, and this has kept him alive.

They go back to the cave and eat together, he with human food and she with ambrosia. Calypso makes one last attempt. If he knew how much he must still suffer, she says, he would stay and become immortal. She asks if his wife is really more beautiful than she is. Odysseus answers wisely and gently. He agrees that Penelope is a mortal woman and cannot match a goddess in height or beauty. But still he wants to go home and thinks of nothing else. If a god wrecks him at sea, he will bear it, as he has borne so much already. Here Odysseus chooses a short human life with his family over immortality with a goddess. That night they sleep together for the last time.

In the morning Calypso gives him a large bronze axe and a sharp tool for smoothing wood, and shows him where the tallest dry trees grow. Odysseus cuts down twenty trees and shapes them well. He drills holes, joins the timbers, and builds a wide raft with a deck, sides, a mast, a sail, and a rudder. Calypso brings cloth for the sail. In four days the work is done. On the fifth day she bathes him, gives him clean clothes, wine, water, and food, and sends a warm, gentle wind.

Odysseus sails happily. He does not sleep. He steers by the stars, keeping the constellation of the Bear on his left, as Calypso told him. For seventeen days he sails, and on the eighteenth day he sees the mountains of the Phaeacian land, like a shield on the sea.

But Poseidon, coming back from Ethiopia, sees him from far away. He is furious. He realizes the other gods changed their minds while he was gone. He knows that Odysseus is fated to escape once he reaches the Phaeacians, but he decides to give him much more pain before then. He gathers the clouds, stirs the sea with his trident, and lets all four winds blow at once. Night falls from the sky.

Odysseus loses heart. He thinks Calypso was right about his troubles. He says the Greeks who died at Troy were lucky, because they received honourable funerals and lasting fame. He wishes he had died in the battle over the body of Achilles. Now he expects a miserable death in the sea, with no grave. Then a huge wave throws him off the raft. The mast breaks. His heavy clothes pull him under, and he stays underwater a long time. At last he comes up, spits out salt water, swims after his raft, and climbs back on. The winds throw the raft around like dry thistle seeds on a road.

A sea goddess sees him. She is Ino, also called Leucothea, who was once a mortal woman. She feels pity for him. She rises from the waves like a seagull and sits on the raft. She tells him to take off his clothes, leave the raft, and swim to the land of the Phaeacians. She gives him her magic veil to tie around his chest. While he wears it, he cannot die. When he reaches land, he must throw it back into the sea without looking. Then she dives away.

Odysseus is again suspicious. He wonders if this is another god's trick to destroy him. He decides to stay on the raft while it holds together, and only swim when it breaks. Just then Poseidon sends a huge wave that smashes the raft into pieces. Odysseus sits on one plank as if riding a horse. He takes off Calypso's clothes, ties on the veil, and jumps into the sea. Poseidon watches him and says, more or less, let him swim now until he finds people to help him, and he will not say he was let off easily. Then Poseidon drives away to his palace.

Athena now acts. She calms all the winds except a strong north wind, which pushes Odysseus toward the Phaeacian land. For two nights and two days he floats on the heavy sea, close to death. On the third morning the wind stops, and he sees land nearby. The poem compares his joy to the joy of children when their sick father gets better. But as he comes closer, he hears waves crashing on rocks. There is no beach or harbour, only cliffs. He fears a wave will smash him against the rocks, or carry him back to sea where a sea monster might attack him.

A wave throws him at the rocks. With Athena's help, he grabs a rock with both hands and holds on until the wave passes. But the backwash pulls him away again, tearing the skin off his hands, like an octopus pulled from its hole with stones stuck to it. He goes deep under the water. Athena gives him good sense. He swims away from the rocks, along the coast, looking for a safe place. He finds the mouth of a river. He prays to the river god, calling himself a suppliant. A suppliant is a person who asks for protection and mercy, and the gods expect people to respect such requests. The river god stops his current and makes the water calm, and Odysseus comes safely into the river mouth.

He is completely broken by the sea. His body is swollen, and sea water runs from his mouth and nose. He faints. When he recovers, he throws Ino's veil back into the water, as she told him, and she catches it. He lies down among the reeds and kisses the earth.

Now he must decide where to sleep. If he stays by the river, the cold night and morning wind may kill him. If he goes up into the woods, wild animals may eat him. He chooses the woods. He finds two olive bushes growing from one root, so thick that no wind, sun, or rain can get through. Under them lies a deep pile of dry leaves. He makes a bed and covers himself with leaves, like a person who hides a burning coal in ashes to keep the fire alive. Athena pours sleep over his eyes, and he forgets his sorrows. The great hero has nothing left. He is naked, alone, and on a strange shore. From here he must rebuild everything with his own wits and the kindness of strangers.

## Book 6: Nausicaa Finds Odysseus

While Odysseus sleeps under the olive bushes, Athena goes to the city of the Phaeacians. The book first explains who these people are. They used to live in a place called Hypereia, close to the Cyclopes, giant one-eyed creatures who were stronger than them and kept robbing them. So their king Nausithous moved them far away to Scheria. He built a wall around the city, made houses and temples, and divided the land. Now his son, King Alcinous, rules them wisely.

Athena enters the beautiful bedroom of Nausicaa, the young daughter of Alcinous. Nausicaa is as lovely as a goddess. Athena appears in her dream in the form of a friend, the daughter of a sea captain named Dymas. The dream-friend scolds her gently. Her clothes are lying around dirty, but she will marry soon, because all the best young men are courting her. She will need clean clothes for herself and her wedding party. So she should ask her father for a wagon and mules and go to wash clothes at the river tomorrow. Then Athena returns to Olympus, the home of the gods, where there is no wind, rain, or snow, only bright, calm light.

In the morning Nausicaa goes to her parents. Her mother is spinning purple wool by the fire. Her father is leaving for a council meeting. Nausicaa asks him for a big wagon so she can wash the family's clothes. She gives practical reasons. Her father needs clean clothes for meetings, and her brothers want fresh linen for dances. She is too shy to mention her own marriage. But her father understands and smiles inside. He gives her what she wants. Her mother packs food, wine, and a golden flask of olive oil for washing. Nausicaa drives the mules herself, and her maids come with her.

At the river there are washing pools with clean, running water. The girls let the mules eat grass. They put the clothes in the pools and stamp on them to wash them, making a game of it. They lay the clothes on the stones by the sea to dry. Then they bathe, rub themselves with oil, and eat lunch. Afterwards they take off their head scarves and play ball, and Nausicaa sings. The poem compares her to Artemis, the hunting goddess, who stands taller and more beautiful than all the nymphs around her.

When it is time to go home, Athena makes a plan to wake Odysseus. Nausicaa throws the ball to a maid, but it misses and falls into the deep water. The girls scream, and the noise wakes Odysseus. He wonders where he is. Are the people here cruel and wild, or kind to strangers? He hears girls' voices and thinks they might be nymphs. He decides to look.

He comes out of the bushes, naked and covered with dry salt. He breaks off a leafy branch to cover himself. The poem compares him to a hungry mountain lion walking through wind and rain to find food. He looks terrible. The maids run away along the beach. Only Nausicaa stands still, because Athena gives her courage.

Odysseus now has to decide how to approach her. Normally a suppliant, someone who begs for help, kneels and holds the knees of the person he asks. But he is a naked stranger, and he thinks she might be frightened or offended. So he stays at a distance and speaks very politely. This is a good example of Odysseus's wise, careful speech.

He asks whether she is a goddess, perhaps Artemis. If she is a mortal, her parents and brothers must be very proud, and the man who marries her will be the happiest of all. He says he once saw a young palm tree at Delos, beside Apollo's altar, and admired it as he now admires her. He tells her he has spent twenty days on the sea since leaving the island of Ogygia, and he fears the gods still have more pain for him. He begs her to show him the way to town and give him a cloth to wear. He wishes her a husband, a home, and a happy marriage. He says nothing is better than a husband and wife who live together with one mind. Their enemies are sad, their friends are glad, and they themselves know their happiness best. This idea of a shared mind in marriage will matter later, when he returns to Penelope.

Nausicaa answers kindly. She says he seems sensible and good. Zeus gives good and bad luck as he wishes, and a man must accept it. Now that he is here, she says, he will have clothes and whatever a poor traveller needs. She tells him that this is the land of the Phaeacians and she is the daughter of King Alcinous.

Then she calls her maids back. She asks them why they run from a man. The Phaeacians live far from others and are loved by the gods, so no enemy can come here. This is just a poor lost man. Strangers and beggars are protected by Zeus, so they must be kind to him. She tells them to give him food and drink and to wash him in a sheltered place. Nausicaa understands hospitality, the sacred duty to welcome strangers.

The maids bring him a shirt, a cloak, and the oil. But Odysseus asks them to step away, because he is ashamed to wash naked in front of young women. He washes the salt from his back and hair, rubs on the oil, and dresses. Then Athena makes him look taller and stronger, and his hair thick and curly like hyacinth flowers. She makes him shine like a silver cup covered with gold by a skilled craftsman. He sits on the beach looking young and handsome. Nausicaa is amazed. She tells her maids quietly that the gods must have sent him. At first she thought he looked plain, but now he looks like a god. She says she wishes her future husband could be such a man, if he would stay. The maids give him food, and he eats hungrily, because he has not eaten for a long time.

Nausicaa then gives him careful instructions. While they are passing through the fields, he can walk behind the wagon with the maids. But near the city they must separate. She describes the city: a high wall, harbours on both sides, ships pulled up along the road, and a market place with a temple of Poseidon. She explains that the Phaeacians are not archers. They are proud sailors who love ships, masts, and oars.

She explains why they must separate. She is afraid of gossip. Some rude person might see them and say that she has found a foreign husband, maybe a sailor from a ship, or a god, because she refuses all the fine young men of her own people. She admits she would also disapprove of a girl who walked about with men before marriage. So Odysseus must wait in a grove of poplar trees sacred to Athena, near her father's garden. When he thinks she has reached home, he should walk into town and ask for the house of Alcinous. Any child can show him. Inside, he should pass her father, who will be sitting and drinking like a god, and go straight to her mother. He must kneel and hold her knees. If her mother likes him, he will surely get home, however far it is.

Nausicaa drives slowly so that Odysseus and the maids can keep up on foot. At sunset they reach Athena's grove. Odysseus sits down and prays to Athena. He reminds her that she did not help him when Poseidon wrecked him, and he asks her now to make the Phaeacians welcome him with pity and kindness. Athena hears his prayer. But she does not appear openly to him, because she respects her uncle Poseidon, who is still very angry. This lovely book shows the gentlest side of the Odyssey: a young woman's courage, natural kindness, and the hope of a safe home after disaster.

## Book 7: In the Palace of King Alcinous

Nausicaa drives home. Her brothers, who are as handsome as gods, unload the clothes and take care of the mules. She goes to her room, where her old nurse Eurymedusa has lit a fire and brings her supper.

After some time, Odysseus gets up and walks toward the city. Athena covers him with a thick mist, so that no proud Phaeacian will see him, insult him, or ask him questions. Near the town gate, Athena meets him in the form of a little girl carrying a water jar. Odysseus asks her politely to show him the house of King Alcinous, since he is a stranger who knows no one. The girl agrees, because she says she lives near the king. She warns him not to speak or look at anyone, because the people here do not like strangers. They are sailors who travel the sea by Poseidon's favour, in ships as fast as a bird or a thought.

So Odysseus follows her through the city, unseen. He admires the harbours, the ships, the meeting places, and the tall walls. At the palace, Athena gives him advice. He should go in boldly, because a brave man usually succeeds, even in a foreign land. He should go first to the queen, whose name is Arete. Athena explains her family. Arete and Alcinous both descend from Poseidon. Poseidon had a son, Nausithous, who became king of the Phaeacians. Nausithous had two sons. One, Rhexenor, was killed by Apollo when he was young and left only a daughter, Arete. Alcinous, the other son, married her. So Arete is the king's niece as well as his wife. Alcinous honours her more than any other wife is honoured. The people treat her almost like a goddess and greet her in the streets. She is wise and good, and she even helps settle quarrels between men. If Odysseus wins her favour, he can hope to see home again. Then Athena leaves for Athens.

Odysseus stops at the bronze doorstep and looks at the palace. It shines like the sun and moon. The walls are bronze with a blue top edge, the doors are gold, and the doorposts are silver. On each side stand gold and silver dogs, made by Hephaestus, the smith god. They are alive and will never grow old, and they guard the house. Inside, seats with fine woven covers line the walls, and golden statues of young men hold torches to light the hall at night. Fifty women work in the house. Some grind grain, others weave and spin. The Phaeacian men are the best sailors, and the women are the best weavers, because Athena taught them.

Outside the court is a large walled garden and orchard. It has pears, pomegranates, apples, figs, and olives. The fruit never fails, in winter or in summer, because a soft west wind always blows. New fruit ripens while the old fruit is still on the tree. In the vineyard, some grapes are drying, some are being picked, some are being pressed into wine, and some are still flowering. There are flower beds that bloom all year, and two springs of water. This magic garden shows that the Phaeacians live a rich, easy life, close to the gods.

Odysseus goes in, still hidden by the mist. The Phaeacian lords are pouring their last drink offerings of the night to Hermes. He walks straight to the queen and puts his hands on her knees. At that moment the mist disappears, and everyone sees him. They are silent with surprise. Odysseus speaks quickly. He prays that the gods bless the queen, the king, and their guests, and he asks them to send him home soon, because he has suffered far from his friends for a long time. Then he sits down in the ashes by the fire. Sitting in the ashes of the hearth is the position of a suppliant, someone who asks for protection.

Everyone is quiet. Finally an old and wise lord, Echeneus, speaks. He tells Alcinous that it is not right for a guest to sit in the ashes. The king should raise him, give him a good seat, and order wine poured to Zeus, who protects suppliants. Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand and seats him in the chair of his favourite son, Laodamas. Servants bring water, bread, and food. Wine is poured for Zeus.

Alcinous tells the lords to go home and sleep. Tomorrow he will invite even more of them to a feast for the guest. Then they will plan how to send the stranger home quickly and safely, however far it may be. After he arrives home, he will have to face whatever fate holds for him. Alcinous also wonders if the stranger might be a god in disguise, because the gods sometimes visit the Phaeacians openly and sit at their feasts.

Odysseus answers that he is not a god at all. He is like the most unhappy of men. If he told all his sorrows, they would think him even worse off. Then, with a touch of humour, he says that even so he must eat, because the stomach is very demanding. It forces a man to eat and forget his pain, no matter how sad he is. He asks them to help him go home in the morning. He says he would die happy if he could see his property, his servants, and his great house once more. Everyone agrees that he spoke well, and they go home.

Now Odysseus is alone with the king and queen. Arete has noticed something. The shirt and cloak he wears are clothes that she and her maids made. So she asks him who he is, where he comes from, and who gave him these clothes. This is a sharp question, because a strange man wearing her daughter's laundry could mean trouble.

Odysseus does not tell his name yet. He answers only the parts she asked about. He tells them about the island of Ogygia, where the goddess Calypso, daughter of Atlas, lives alone. Zeus struck his ship with lightning and all his men drowned. He held on to the keel of the ship for nine days, and on the tenth night the gods brought him to Calypso. She was kind to him and wanted to make him immortal, but he refused. He stayed with her seven years, always wetting her gifts of clothing with his tears. In the eighth year she let him go, perhaps because Zeus ordered it, perhaps because she changed her mind. She gave him a raft, food, wine, clothes, and a warm wind. He sailed for seventeen days and saw their mountains on the eighteenth. But Poseidon raised a storm, the raft broke, and he swam until he reached their land. He could not land on the rocks, so he swam to a river mouth. He slept in the leaves all night and until the afternoon of the next day. Then he saw their daughter on the beach with her maids. He asked for her help, and she was wiser and kinder than most young people. She gave him food, a bath, and these clothes.

Alcinous says his daughter made a mistake. She should have brought the stranger home with her maids. Odysseus protects Nausicaa. He tells a small, kind lie. He says she did tell him to follow with the maids, but he himself was afraid and ashamed, worried that the king might be angry. All people are a little suspicious sometimes, he says.

Alcinous answers that he is not a man who gets angry for no reason. He is so pleased with the stranger's character and good sense that he says he wishes the man would stay, marry Nausicaa, and become his son-in-law. He would give him a house and land. But no one will keep him against his will. Tomorrow Alcinous will arrange his journey. Odysseus can sleep the whole way. The Phaeacian sailors will carry him over calm seas to his home, however far, even beyond Euboea, which they think is the farthest place in the world. Their ships are the best in the world, he boasts. They once went there and back in a single day.

Odysseus is happy. He prays to Zeus that Alcinous will keep his promise, which will bring the king eternal fame and bring Odysseus home. Arete has the maids make a soft bed with red blankets in the gatehouse. They tell him his bed is ready, and he is glad to go and rest. The book shows how a stranger with nothing can win respect through good words, courage, and careful manners.

## Book 8: Games, Songs, and Tears Among the Phaeacians

In the morning King Alcinous takes Odysseus to the meeting place near the ships. Athena, in the shape of a royal herald, goes around the city calling everyone to come and see the stranger who looks like a god. Many people come. Athena makes Odysseus look taller and stronger, so that the Phaeacians will respect him and so that he will do well in the contests they will offer him.

Alcinous speaks to the people. He says that a stranger has come to his house and wants to be taken home. They should prepare a ship, as they have done for other guests, because no visitor has ever complained of waiting too long. Fifty-two of the best young sailors should prepare a new ship. Then everyone should come to the palace for a feast. The king also sends for Demodocus, the best singer in the land.

The young men prepare the ship and then come to the palace. Alcinous kills twelve sheep, eight pigs, and two oxen for a great feast. A servant leads in Demodocus. The Muse loved him and gave him both good and bad things. She gave him the gift of song, but she took away his eyesight. A servant seats him, hangs his lyre above his head, and shows him how to reach it.

After the meal Demodocus sings about the Trojan War. He sings about a quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles at a feast. Agamemnon was happy to hear his best men quarrelling, because an oracle at Delphi had once told him this would happen before Troy fell. An oracle is a holy place where a god answers questions about the future. When Odysseus hears this song about himself and his old friends, he pulls his purple cloak over his head to hide his tears. Each time the singer stops, he wipes his eyes and pours a drink offering. When the singer starts again, he cries again. Nobody notices except Alcinous, who sits next to him and hears him sighing.

Alcinous kindly changes the activity. He says they have had enough food and music, so now they should go out for sports. Then the guest can tell his friends how good the Phaeacians are at boxing, wrestling, jumping, and running. Thousands of people come to watch. Many young men compete, including three sons of Alcinous and a handsome young man named Euryalus. Clytoneus, a son of the king, wins the foot race easily. Euryalus wins the wrestling. Amphialus wins the jump. Elatreus wins the disc throw. Laodamas, the king's favourite son, is the best boxer.

Laodamas says they should invite the stranger to compete. He looks strong, though the sea has worn him out. Euryalus agrees. Laodamas politely invites Odysseus, saying that sport is the best way for a man to win honour. Odysseus answers that he is full of sorrows, not thinking about games. He only wants to go home.

Then Euryalus insults him. He says the stranger must not know any sports. He looks like a greedy merchant who sails around thinking only about cargo and profit, not like an athlete. This is a real insult, because in this world noblemen are supposed to be athletes and warriors, not traders.

Odysseus becomes angry. He answers with a wise speech. The gods do not give every gift to every man, he says. One man may be plain to look at, but the gods give him good speech, so people listen to him and honour him. Another man may be as handsome as a god, but he has no sense. That, he tells Euryalus, is your case. You are very handsome, but you are a fool. Then he says he was once one of the best athletes, though now his suffering has made him weak. Still, the insult has stung him, so he will compete.

He does not even take off his cloak. He picks up a disc that is much bigger and heavier than the ones the Phaeacians use. He throws it, and it hums through the air. The Phaeacians duck in fear. It lands far beyond all the other marks. Athena, disguised as a man, marks the spot and says even a blind man could find this mark, it is so far ahead. No Phaeacian can beat it.

Odysseus is pleased to have a friend in the crowd. He challenges anyone to box, wrestle, or run against him, except Laodamas, because Laodamas is his host's son, and a guest should not compete against his host's family. He boasts that he is an excellent archer. At Troy only Philoctetes was better with the bow. But he would not compete with the great archers of the past, like Heracles or Eurytus, who even challenged the gods. Apollo killed Eurytus for that. He admits that he might lose a running race, because hunger at sea has made him weak. This boast about the bow is important, because at the end of the poem Odysseus will prove his skill with a bow in his own house.

Everyone is silent. Alcinous answers tactfully. He says the stranger has every right to be angry at the rude words. He admits that the Phaeacians are not the best boxers or wrestlers. They are fast runners and great sailors, and they love good food, music, dancing, clean clothes, warm baths, and soft beds. He calls for dancing, so that the guest can see what they really do best.

Demodocus is given his lyre, and the best young dancers perform with quick, light feet. Odysseus is amazed. Then Demodocus sings a funny and famous story about the gods. Ares, the god of war, and Aphrodite, the goddess of love, have a secret affair in the house of Aphrodite's husband Hephaestus, the lame smith god. Helios, the Sun, sees them and tells Hephaestus. Hephaestus goes to his forge and makes chains so fine that even gods cannot see them, but so strong that nobody can break them. He hangs them around his bed. Then he pretends to leave for the island of Lemnos. Ares immediately comes to Aphrodite, and the two lovers lie down on the bed. The invisible chains catch them, and they cannot move.

Hephaestus returns and shouts for all the gods to come and see. He complains that Aphrodite despises him because he is lame, and loves Ares because he is handsome and fast. He says he will keep them there until her father Zeus gives back the wedding gifts. The male gods come, though the goddesses stay home out of shame. The gods laugh and laugh. They say that bad deeds do not prosper, and that slow, lame Hephaestus has caught Ares, the fastest god. Apollo jokes with Hermes and asks if he would like to be caught in chains with Aphrodite. Hermes says yes, even with three times as many chains and all the gods watching. Everyone laughs again. Only Poseidon is serious. He asks Hephaestus to free Ares and promises that Ares will pay. If Ares does not pay, Poseidon will pay himself. So Hephaestus lets them go. Ares flies to Thrace, and Aphrodite goes to Cyprus, where the Graces bathe her and dress her. This story of a clever husband trapping lovers in his own bedroom quietly connects with Odysseus's own story, where cleverness wins against stronger men.

Then two sons of the king do a special dance with a red ball. One throws it high toward the clouds, and the other leaps and catches it. They dance and pass it to each other while the young men clap and stamp. Odysseus praises them sincerely. Alcinous is delighted. He says the guest is a man of good judgement. He asks the twelve leading lords to each give the stranger a clean cloak, a shirt, and a talent of gold. A talent is a weight of gold, a very valuable amount. He also says Euryalus must apologize and give a gift. Euryalus agrees. He gives Odysseus his bronze sword with a silver handle and an ivory sheath. He wishes him good luck and asks the winds to blow away any bad words. Odysseus accepts warmly and hopes Euryalus will never miss the sword. The quarrel ends in friendship.

At sunset the gifts arrive at the palace. Arete packs them in a beautiful chest, and Alcinous adds a golden cup so Odysseus will remember him whenever he pours an offering to the gods. Arete tells Odysseus to tie the lid himself so no one robs him while he sleeps on the ship. He ties it with a special knot that Circe taught him. Then he enjoys a warm bath, his first since he left Calypso.

As he walks to the feast, Nausicaa stands by a pillar and admires him. She says goodbye and asks him to remember her when he is home, because he owes his life first to her. Odysseus promises he will honour her like a goddess all his days, because she saved him. This is their gentle farewell.

At dinner, Odysseus sends the blind singer a fine piece of roast pork, as a sign of honour. He says singers deserve respect everywhere, because the Muse teaches them. He praises Demodocus for singing the story of the Greeks so accurately, as if he had been there himself. Then he asks for a special song: the story of the wooden horse, which Epeus built with Athena's help, and which Odysseus brought into Troy full of hidden soldiers.

Demodocus sings it. The Greeks burned their tents and pretended to sail away, while the best warriors hid inside the horse with Odysseus. The Trojans pulled the horse into their city and argued about it. Some wanted to break it, some wanted to throw it off a cliff, and some wanted to keep it as an offering to the gods. They kept it, and so the city was doomed. The Greeks came out at night and destroyed Troy. Odysseus fought like the war god at the house of Deiphobus, and with Athena's help he won.

As Odysseus listens, he weeps bitterly. The poem gives a powerful comparison. He weeps like a woman who throws herself on her husband's body after he dies defending his city. Enemy soldiers beat her and drag her off to slavery. It is striking that the conqueror of Troy cries like one of the Trojan victims. The song of his own victory brings him sorrow, not pride. Again only Alcinous notices.

The king stops the song. He says that a guest and suppliant should be treated like a brother, and this guest is clearly in pain. Now Alcinous asks directly for his name, his country, and his city. He explains that Phaeacian ships do not need pilots or rudders. The ships understand what their sailors are thinking and know every city in the world, and they travel safely even through fog. But Alcinous also remembers that his father warned him of a prophecy. Poseidon is angry that the Phaeacians give everyone safe journeys. One day he will destroy one of their ships as it returns home and bury their city under a mountain. Whether this will happen, the god will decide.

Finally Alcinous asks Odysseus to tell where he has wandered, which peoples were cruel and which were kind, and why he cries when he hears about Troy. Did he lose a relative or a dear friend there? This question opens the door for the great story that follows.

## Book 9: Odysseus Tells His Name, and the Cyclops

From this book until the end of Book 12, Odysseus himself is the storyteller. He is speaking to King Alcinous and the Phaeacians at the feast, telling them what happened to him in the years after Troy. Everything in these books is his own account of his past.

Odysseus begins politely. He says there is nothing more pleasant than a feast where people sit in order, the tables are full, and a fine singer entertains them. But since the king asks about his sorrows, he hardly knows where to begin. First he tells them his name. He is Odysseus, son of Laertes, famous among all people for his clever tricks. His home is Ithaca, a rocky island with a forested mountain called Neritum, near other islands. It is rough, he says, but it raises good men, and no place is sweeter to his eyes. Calypso kept him and wanted to marry him, and so did the goddess Circe, but neither could change his heart. Nothing is dearer to a man than his own country and his parents. A rich home in a foreign land means nothing if it is far from them.

Then he starts his story. After leaving Troy, the wind carried his twelve ships to Ismarus, the city of the Cicones. His men attacked the town, killed the men, and took the women and many goods, which they shared fairly. Odysseus told his men to leave quickly, but they foolishly refused. They stayed on the beach drinking wine and killing sheep and cattle. Meanwhile the Cicones called their neighbours from inland, who were more numerous and better fighters. In the morning they came as thick as leaves in spring. The Greeks held out until afternoon, but then they were defeated. Six men from each ship were killed. The survivors escaped and sailed on, sad for their dead friends. Before leaving, they called the name of each dead man three times, as a ritual farewell. This first adventure already shows a pattern. Odysseus gives good advice, but his men do not listen, and they suffer.

Zeus then sent a terrible north wind. The sails were torn, and they rowed to land and rested two days. Odysseus says he would have reached home at that time, but as he was sailing round Cape Malea, near the southern tip of Greece, the wind and currents pushed him off course, past the island of Cythera. From this point, his journey moves into a strange world of monsters and magic.

For nine days the winds drove them. On the tenth day they reached the land of the Lotus-Eaters, a people who eat a flower called the lotus. Odysseus sent three men to learn about the people. The Lotus-Eaters did not hurt them. They simply gave them lotus to eat. But the lotus was so sweet that the men forgot about home and wanted to stay forever, eating lotus. Odysseus dragged them back to the ships, crying, and tied them under the benches. He ordered everyone else to row away before they could taste it. The lotus is a danger of a different kind. It does not kill the body, but it kills the wish to go home.

Next they came to the land of the Cyclopes. Odysseus describes them as wild and lawless. They do not plant or plough. Wheat, barley, and grapes grow for them without work. They have no laws and no assemblies. Each one lives in a cave on a mountain and rules only his own wife and children, with no care for his neighbours. They also have no ships. Near their coast is a small island full of wild goats. It has rich soil, meadows, water, and a perfect harbour, but nobody lives there, because the Cyclopes cannot sail. Odysseus notices how a civilized people could make it into a fine farm. This description shows what Homer's world thinks of as savage: no farming, no laws, no meetings, no ships, and no respect for strangers.

On a dark, misty night, a god guided their ships into the harbour of this goat island. In the morning they hunted goats. Each of the twelve ships got nine goats, and Odysseus's ship got ten. They feasted all day with wine they had taken from the Cicones. They could see smoke and hear sheep on the land of the Cyclopes nearby. The next day Odysseus told the others to stay. He would go with his own ship to find out whether the people over there were savages or people who respected guests.

Near the shore they saw a large cave with a stone wall around a yard full of sheep and goats. The owner was a huge monster who lived alone, apart from the others, and looked more like a rocky mountain peak than a man. Odysseus chose his twelve best men and took a goatskin of very strong, sweet wine. A priest of Apollo named Maron had given him this wine at Ismarus, because Odysseus protected him and his family during the attack. The wine was so strong that Maron mixed one cup of it with twenty cups of water. Odysseus says he had a feeling that he might meet a strong, savage creature who respected no law.

The giant was out with his flocks. The men entered the cave and saw racks full of cheese, pens full of lambs and young goats, and pails full of milk. The men begged Odysseus to steal some cheese and animals and run back to the ship. Odysseus admits that this would have been better. But he refused, because he wanted to see the owner and hoped to receive a guest gift. Here Odysseus's curiosity and wish for gifts put his men in great danger.

They lit a fire, offered some cheese to the gods, ate some, and waited. The Cyclops came home with a huge load of firewood and threw it down with a crash. The terrified men hid at the back of the cave. He brought in his female animals and then closed the entrance with a gigantic stone, so heavy that twenty-two wagons could not move it. He milked his animals carefully, set aside milk for cheese, and lit the fire. Then he saw the men. He asked who they were, traders or pirates.

Odysseus answered that they were Greeks from Troy, men of the great King Agamemnon, blown off course. He asked for hospitality and gifts, as is proper for guests. He reminded the Cyclops that Zeus protects strangers and suppliants. But the Cyclops answered cruelly. He said the Cyclopes do not care about Zeus or any god, because they are much stronger. He would spare them only if he felt like it. Then he cleverly asked where their ship was. Odysseus saw the trick and lied. He said Poseidon had wrecked their ship on the rocks, and only they had survived.

The Cyclops did not answer. He grabbed two men, smashed them on the ground like puppies, tore them apart, and ate them completely, like a lion. The men cried and raised their hands to the gods. Then the giant drank milk and lay down to sleep. Odysseus first wanted to stab him in the heart with his sword. But he realized that if the giant died, they could never move the huge stone from the door, and they would all die trapped inside. So, sobbing, they waited for morning. This is a key moment. Odysseus controls his anger and thinks before acting.

In the morning, the Cyclops milked his animals, ate two more men, and went out with his flock, easily putting the stone back. Odysseus made a plan. In the cave lay a huge club of green olive wood, as large as the mast of a ship. Odysseus cut off a piece about six feet long. His men smoothed it, and he sharpened the end and hardened it in the fire. They hid it under the dung on the cave floor. The men drew lots to choose four helpers, and the lot fell on exactly the four men Odysseus would have chosen.

In the evening the Cyclops came back. This time he brought all his animals inside, even the rams, perhaps because a god made him do it. He ate two more men. Then Odysseus came to him with a bowl of the strong wine. He said the giant should taste it after his meal of human flesh. He had brought it as a gift, hoping for kindness, but the Cyclops was behaving terribly. The giant drank and loved it. He asked for more and asked for the stranger's name, so he could give him a gift. Three times Odysseus filled the bowl, and three times the giant drank it all. When the wine had gone to his head, Odysseus said his name was Nobody. In Butler's translation this name is Noman. The Cyclops said his gift would be this: he would eat Nobody last, after all his friends. Then he fell on his back in a heavy, drunken sleep and vomited wine and pieces of human flesh.

Odysseus pushed the stake into the fire until it was about to burst into flame. He encouraged his men. Together they drove the red-hot point into the giant's single eye. Odysseus leaned on it and twisted it, like a carpenter drilling a hole in a ship's plank. The blood boiled, and the eye hissed like hot iron dipped into cold water by a blacksmith. The Cyclops screamed so loudly that the cave rang. He pulled out the stake and shouted for the other Cyclopes.

They came to his cave and asked what was wrong. Was someone stealing his sheep, or killing him by trick or by force? The Cyclops, whose name is Polyphemus, shouted that Nobody was killing him by trickery, and no one by force. The others answered that if nobody was hurting him, he must be sick, and sickness comes from Zeus. They told him to pray to his father Poseidon, and they went away. Odysseus laughed inside at the success of his trick. The name trick shows his special kind of cleverness, using words as weapons.

Blind and in pain, Polyphemus moved the stone and sat in the doorway with his hands spread out, to catch any man who tried to leave with the sheep. Odysseus thought hard about how to escape. He tied the big woolly rams together in groups of three, using willow branches from the giant's bed. A man hung under the middle ram of each group, hidden by the other two. Odysseus himself took the biggest ram and held on under its belly, face up, gripping the thick wool.

At dawn the rams went out to graze. Polyphemus felt their backs but did not think to feel under their bellies. The big ram came out last, slowed by the weight of Odysseus. Polyphemus stopped it and spoke to it sadly. He said the ram was usually first to go out and first to return. Was it sad for its master's lost eye? If only it could speak, he said, it would tell him where Nobody was hiding, and he would smash his brains on the floor. Then he let it go. This moment gives the monster a strange, lonely tenderness.

Outside, Odysseus let go of the ram and untied his men. They drove the fat sheep to the ship. The crew was happy to see them but wept for the six men who had been eaten. Odysseus signalled them to be quiet and row away fast.

But when they were a short distance out, Odysseus could not resist shouting insults. He told the Cyclops that Zeus and the gods had punished him for eating his guests. The furious giant tore off a mountain top and threw it. It fell just in front of the ship, and the wave pushed them back toward the shore. Odysseus pushed off with a pole and signalled his men to row hard. When they were twice as far away, he wanted to shout again. His men begged him not to, because the giant could still crush them with rocks. But Odysseus, full of anger and pride, shouted his real name. He told the Cyclops that if anyone asked who blinded him, he should say it was Odysseus, son of Laertes, of Ithaca.

Polyphemus groaned. He said an old prophet had told him long ago that a man named Odysseus would take his sight. He had expected a giant hero, not a small weakling who tricked him with wine. He invited Odysseus to come back for gifts and promised that his father Poseidon would help him and heal his eye. Odysseus mocked him again and said not even Poseidon could heal that eye.

Then Polyphemus prayed to Poseidon. He asked that Odysseus never reach home. Or, if he must reach home, that he arrive late and in misery, after losing all his men, in a ship that belongs to strangers, and that he find trouble in his house. Poseidon heard the prayer. The giant threw another, even bigger rock. It fell just behind the ship, and the wave pushed them toward the goat island.

This moment explains much of the whole poem. By telling his name in pride, Odysseus allowed the Cyclops to curse him personally. The rest of his journey follows that curse almost exactly. His cleverness saved him in the cave, but his pride gave his enemy a name to curse.

Back on the goat island, the other crews were waiting and grieving. They shared the Cyclops's sheep fairly. The men gave Odysseus the big ram as an extra prize. He sacrificed it to Zeus on the shore. But Odysseus says that Zeus did not accept the sacrifice, and was already planning to destroy his ships and his friends. They feasted all day, and the next morning they sailed on, sad for their lost friends but glad to be alive.

## Book 10: The Bag of Winds, the Giant Cannibals, and Circe

Odysseus continues telling his story to the Phaeacians. After the land of the Cyclopes, his twelve ships came to the island of Aeolus. This island floats on the sea and has a wall of bronze all around it. Aeolus lives there with his wife, six sons, and six daughters. He married the sons to the daughters, and they all feast together every day in a house that smells of roasting meat.

Aeolus welcomed Odysseus kindly. For a whole month he asked about Troy and the Greek heroes, and Odysseus told him everything. When Odysseus asked for help to go home, Aeolus agreed at once. Zeus had made Aeolus the keeper of the winds. He took the skin of an ox and made it into a bag. Inside he shut up all the stormy winds. He tied the bag tightly with a silver thread and placed it in Odysseus's ship. He let only the gentle west wind blow, to carry them home. But, as Odysseus says, they were ruined by their own foolishness.

For nine days and nights they sailed. On the tenth day they saw Ithaca. They came so close that they could see people's fires on the land. Odysseus had held the steering oar himself the whole time, without sleep, so that they would get home faster. Now, exhausted, he fell asleep. His men began to talk. They believed the bag was full of gold and silver. They complained that Odysseus always received honour and gifts wherever he went, while they had travelled just as far and had nothing. They decided to open the bag to see the treasure. When they did, all the winds rushed out and made a terrible storm. It blew the ships far away from their home, back to the island of Aeolus.

Odysseus woke up. He wondered whether to jump into the sea and die, or to live and endure. He chose to endure. He covered his head and lay in the ship while the men cried. This choice to endure, even in despair, is one of the main qualities of Odysseus.

On Aeolus's island, Odysseus went to the king's house with a herald and one man and sat on the doorstep as a suppliant. The family was amazed. Odysseus explained that his foolish men and cruel sleep had ruined him, and he begged for help again. But Aeolus called him the worst of men and told him to leave the island. A man so hated by the gods, he said, does not deserve help. Odysseus left in sorrow.

They rowed for six days, worn out, because there was no wind. On the seventh day they reached the land of the Laestrygonians. In this land the days are so long that a man who did not need sleep could earn two wages, one for herding cattle and another for herding sheep. The harbour was surrounded by high cliffs, with a narrow entrance and perfectly calm water. All the other captains brought their ships inside. Odysseus kept his own ship outside the harbour and tied it to a rock. From a high rock he saw only smoke. He sent two men and a herald to find out who lived there.

The men met a young woman fetching water. She was the daughter of the king, Antiphates. She showed them her father's house. Inside they found the queen, a giant woman as big as a mountain, and they were terrified. She called her husband, who seized one of the men at once and ate him. The other two ran back to the ships. Antiphates raised the alarm, and thousands of giants came running. They were monsters, not men. They threw huge rocks down from the cliffs onto the ships inside the harbour. Odysseus heard the ships breaking and his men screaming. The giants speared the men like fish and carried them home to eat. Odysseus cut the rope of his own ship with his sword and told his crew to row for their lives. They escaped to the open sea. Every other ship and all their crews were destroyed. Now only one ship is left out of twelve. Odysseus's caution in staying outside the harbour saved his own crew.

Sadly they sailed on until they reached the island of Aeaea, home of Circe. She is a powerful goddess and a witch. She is the daughter of the Sun god and the sister of the magician Aeetes. Exhausted and grieving, they lay on the beach for two days. On the third morning Odysseus took his spear and sword and climbed a hill to look around. He saw smoke rising from Circe's house in the middle of a thick forest. He decided to go back first and feed his men.

On the way, a god sent a huge stag across his path. Odysseus killed it with his spear. He tied its legs with a rope of twisted grass and carried it back on his neck, leaning on his spear, because it was so big. He cheered his men. They would not die yet, he said, while they had food. The men admired the stag, cooked it, and feasted all day.

The next morning Odysseus called a meeting. He admitted that they were lost. They did not even know east from west. He had seen that this was an island, with smoke rising from the forest. The men broke down and wept, remembering the Cyclops and the Laestrygonians. Odysseus divided them into two groups. He led one group, and his relative Eurylochus led the other. They drew lots in a helmet, and Eurylochus's group of twenty-two men had to go.

They found Circe's stone house in a clearing. Around it were wolves and lions, but they were tame, because Circe had bewitched them with drugs. The animals wagged their tails and rubbed against the men like dogs greeting their master, which frightened the men even more. At the gate they heard Circe singing beautifully as she wove at her loom. Polites, a man Odysseus loved and trusted most, suggested calling to her. Circe opened the door and invited them in. They all went in, except Eurylochus, who suspected a trap.

Inside, Circe gave them seats and a drink of cheese, honey, barley, and wine. But she mixed in evil drugs to make them forget home. When they drank, she struck them with her wand and turned them into pigs. They had the heads, bristles, and voices of pigs, but their minds stayed the same. They remembered everything. She shut them in pigsties and threw them acorns.

Eurylochus ran back to the ship, so upset he could hardly speak. At last he told what had happened. Odysseus took his sword and bow and told Eurylochus to show him the way. Eurylochus held on to him and begged him not to go. He was sure Odysseus would not come back. He said they should just escape with the men who were left. Odysseus told him to stay, but he himself must go. He would not abandon his men.

On the way through the forest, Odysseus met Hermes, disguised as a handsome young man. Hermes warned him that he could not free his men alone and would become a pig himself. But Hermes offered help. He gave Odysseus a magic herb, called moly by the gods. It has a black root and a milk-white flower. Humans cannot dig it up, but gods can do anything. The herb would protect Odysseus from Circe's drug. Hermes explained what to do. When Circe struck him with her wand, he should draw his sword and rush at her as if to kill her. She would be frightened and invite him to her bed. He should not refuse, because he needed her to free his men. But first he must make her swear a great oath not to harm him, or she might take away his manhood when he was naked.

Odysseus went to Circe's house. She invited him in, sat him in a fine chair, and gave him the drugged drink. He drank it, but nothing happened. She struck him with her wand and told him to go to the pigsty with his friends. He drew his sword and rushed at her. She screamed, fell, held his knees, and asked who he was. No man had ever resisted her drug. Then she realized he must be Odysseus, because Hermes had told her he would come one day on his way home from Troy. She invited him to bed, so they could become friends and trust each other.

Odysseus said he could not trust her after she had turned his men into pigs. He would not go to her bed unless she swore not to harm him. She swore the oath, and then he went to bed with her.

Afterwards, Circe's four maids, who are nymphs of the springs and woods, prepared a beautiful room, a table, wine, and a hot bath. They bathed him, rubbed him with oil, and dressed him. Food was set before him. But Odysseus did not eat. He sat silent and sad. Circe asked why. Was he still suspicious? He answered that no decent man could eat before his friends were set free. If she wanted him to eat, she must free them so he could see them with his own eyes. This shows his loyalty to his men.

Circe went to the pigsties and let the pigs out. She rubbed each one with another drug. The bristles fell off, and they became men again, younger, taller, and more handsome than before. They recognized Odysseus, held his hands, and cried with joy. Even Circe felt pity. She told Odysseus to go to the shore, pull his ship onto land, hide the goods in a cave, and bring the rest of his men.

At the ship, the men who had stayed behind were weeping. When they saw Odysseus, they ran around him like calves jumping around their mothers in the evening. They were as happy as if they had reached Ithaca. Odysseus told them to hide the ship's goods and come to Circe's house, where their friends were feasting. But Eurylochus tried to stop them. He said Circe would turn them into pigs, wolves, or lions. He reminded them of the Cyclops, and blamed Odysseus's foolishness for the death of the men in the cave. Odysseus was so angry that he thought of cutting off Eurylochus's head, even though he was a close relative. The other men begged him to leave Eurylochus to guard the ship. In the end, Eurylochus was frightened by the scolding and came along anyway.

At Circe's house, the two groups of men met and wept with joy. Circe told them to stop crying. She knew how much they had suffered. She told them to stay and eat and drink until they recovered their strength and good spirits.

They stayed with Circe for a whole year, feasting on meat and wine. When the year had passed, the men took Odysseus aside. They told him it was time to think about home. It is interesting that this time the men, not Odysseus, remember the goal. That night in bed, Odysseus held Circe's knees and asked her to keep her promise to help them go home.

Circe agreed that they did not have to stay. But first, she said, he must make another journey. He must go to the house of Hades, the land of the dead, and ask advice from the ghost of Tiresias, the blind prophet from Thebes. Persephone, queen of the dead, lets only Tiresias keep his mind after death. All other ghosts drift around without understanding.

Odysseus was horrified. He sat up in bed and wept. He did not want to live any longer. When he had finished crying, he asked who would guide him, since no ship has ever sailed to Hades. Circe told him that he needed no guide. The north wind would carry his ship across the great river Ocean, which flows around the world. He would reach Persephone's land, with its groves of poplars and willows. He must beach the ship and walk to the place where two rivers flow into the river Acheron, beside a rock.

There, she said, he must dig a pit about a cubit wide and deep. A cubit is the length of a forearm. He must pour three offerings for the dead: first milk mixed with honey, then wine, then water, and sprinkle white barley over it. He must pray to the dead and promise that, back in Ithaca, he would sacrifice his best cow to them and give Tiresias a black sheep. Then he must kill a ram and a black ewe, with their heads turned toward the darkness, and let the blood flow into the pit, while he himself turns away toward the river. Many ghosts will come. His men must burn the sheep as an offering to Hades and Persephone. He must sit with his sword drawn and keep all other ghosts away from the blood until Tiresias has spoken. The prophet will tell him how to sail home.

At dawn Circe dressed him and herself. Odysseus went among his men and woke them. But they did not all leave safely. A young man named Elpenor, who was neither very wise nor very brave, had drunk too much and gone up to sleep on the cool roof. When he heard the noise of the others getting ready, he jumped up, forgot to use the ladder, fell off the roof, broke his neck, and died. His soul went to the house of Hades.

Odysseus told the men that they were not going home yet, but to the land of the dead. They fell on the ground, groaning and tearing their hair, but crying did not help. At the shore they found that Circe had already tied a ram and a black ewe by the ship. She had passed among them without being seen. As Odysseus says, who can see a god come and go if the god does not wish it?

## Book 11: Odysseus Speaks with the Dead

Odysseus is still telling his story to the Phaeacians. He describes how he and his men sailed from Circe's island, weeping and full of fear. Circe sent a steady wind, and all day the ship ran with full sails. At sunset they reached the deep waters of the river Ocean, at the edge of the world. There live the Cimmerians, a people covered in fog and darkness, who never see the sun. They pulled the ship onto the shore and walked to the place Circe had described.

Odysseus did exactly as Circe told him. Two men held the sheep. He dug a pit with his sword and poured offerings for the dead: milk and honey, then wine, then water, with white barley sprinkled over them. He prayed to the ghosts and promised them sacrifices in Ithaca, and a black sheep for Tiresias. Then he cut the throats of the two sheep and let the blood run into the pit. The ghosts came rushing up from the darkness: young brides, young men, old men, girls who died of love, and soldiers still wearing bloody armour. They made a strange, thin screaming sound, and Odysseus went pale with fear. He told his men to burn the sheep for Hades and Persephone. He sat with his sword drawn and kept all the ghosts away from the blood until Tiresias came. In this world, ghosts are weak and confused, but drinking blood gives them back their memory and voice for a short time.

The first ghost was Elpenor, the young man who fell from Circe's roof. His body was still lying unburied. Odysseus cried and asked how he had arrived so quickly, on foot, faster than the ship. Elpenor explained that bad luck and too much wine had killed him. He begged Odysseus, by his wife, his father, and his son Telemachus, to go back to Circe's island and give him a proper funeral. He asked him to burn his body with his armour, build a grave mound by the sea, and plant his oar on top, so future people would remember him. Odysseus promised. Proper burial mattered deeply to the Greeks. Without it, the dead could not rest.

Next came the ghost of Odysseus's own mother, Anticlea. She was alive when he left for Troy. He did not know she had died. He wept to see her. But even so, he did not let her drink the blood before Tiresias. This is very painful, but it shows his discipline.

Then Tiresias came, holding a golden staff. He recognized Odysseus and asked him to lower his sword so he could drink and speak the truth. After drinking, the prophet gave his famous prophecy. Odysseus wants to go home, he said, but the gods will make it hard. Poseidon is still angry about his son's eye. Still, they may get home if they can control themselves on the island of Thrinacia, where the cattle and sheep of the Sun god graze. If they leave those animals unharmed, they will reach Ithaca after hardship. If they harm them, the ship and all the men will be destroyed. Even if Odysseus escapes, he will come home late, in misery, without his men, in a stranger's ship, and he will find trouble in his house. Proud men will be eating his wealth while courting his wife. These words echo the Cyclops's curse almost exactly.

Tiresias continued. When Odysseus gets home, he will kill the suitors, by trick or by open force. After that, he must take an oar on his shoulder and walk inland, until he finds people who know nothing about the sea, who do not put salt in their food and have never seen a ship. He will know the place when a traveller meets him and calls the oar a winnowing shovel, a tool farmers use to separate grain from husks. There he must plant the oar in the ground and sacrifice a ram, a bull, and a boar to Poseidon. Then he must go home and make sacrifices to all the gods. Death will come to him gently, away from the sea, when he is old and comfortable, and his people will be happy around him. So the prophecy looks beyond the end of the poem.

Odysseus accepted this. Then he asked how he could speak to his mother, who sat silently and did not recognize him. Tiresias explained that any ghost who drinks the blood will speak sensibly with him. Then the prophet went back into the house of Hades.

Anticlea came and drank. She knew him at once. She asked how he, still living, had come to this dark place across the great waters. Had he still not reached Ithaca? Odysseus explained that he had come to consult Tiresias, and that he had suffered ever since he left for Troy. He asked how she died. Was it a long illness or a gentle death? He asked about his father, his son, his property, and his wife. Was Penelope still faithful, or had she remarried?

His mother answered. Penelope was still in his house, faithful but crying night and day. No one had taken his property yet. Telemachus managed the estate and was invited to feasts like a respected man. Laertes, his father, never came to town. He lived in the country, slept by the fire with the servants in winter, and wore rags. In summer he slept on leaves in the vineyard. He grieved for his lost son and grew weaker with age. As for herself, she did not die of illness or a quick death sent by the gods. She died from missing him, from longing for her son and his gentle ways. This is one of the most moving moments in the poem. It also tells us the cost of Odysseus's long absence for his whole family.

Odysseus tried to hug his mother. Three times he reached for her, and three times she slipped through his arms like a dream or a shadow. In pain he asked why she would not stay still. Was Persephone mocking him with an empty image? His mother explained that this is simply what happens to the dead. When the body is burned, the flesh and bones are gone, and the soul flies away like a dream. She told him to go back to the light and remember everything, so he could tell his wife.

Then Persephone sent up the ghosts of many famous women, wives and daughters of great men. They crowded around the blood. Odysseus held his sword so they would drink one at a time and tell their stories. He saw Tyro, who loved a river god and was seduced by Poseidon disguised as that river. She gave birth to twins, Pelias and Neleus, the father of Nestor. He saw Antiope, who bore two sons to Zeus who built the walls of Thebes. He saw Alcmena, mother of the hero Heracles by Zeus. He saw Epicaste, mother of Oedipus, who married her own son without knowing it, after he had killed his father. When the truth came out, she hanged herself. He saw Chloris, mother of Nestor, and Leda, mother of the twin heroes Castor and Pollux, who take turns living and dying each day. He saw Iphimedeia, whose giant sons tried to pile mountains on top of each other to climb to heaven, until Apollo killed them. He saw Phaedra, Procris, Ariadne, and many others, including Eriphyle, who betrayed her husband for gold.

At this point Odysseus stops his story. He says it would take all night to name every woman he saw, and it is time to sleep. This pause reminds us that he is a storyteller speaking to an audience. The Phaeacians sit silent, under his spell. Queen Arete praises him. She says he is handsome and clever, and asks the lords not to send him away too fast, and to give him generous gifts. Old Echeneus agrees, but says the king must decide. Alcinous promises to gather more gifts and send him home the next day. Odysseus politely says he would happily stay a whole year if it meant more gifts, because he would be more respected at home.

Alcinous praises his storytelling. He says Odysseus is clearly not a liar or a cheat, like the many travellers who tell believable false stories. He speaks like a skilled singer. The king asks to hear more, especially whether he saw his old companions from Troy. The night is long, he says, and he could listen until morning. Odysseus agrees.

He continues. After Persephone sent the women away, the ghost of Agamemnon came, with the men who died with him. He drank the blood, recognized Odysseus, and cried, reaching out his arms, but he had no strength. Odysseus asked how he died, at sea or in battle. Agamemnon said that Aegisthus and his own wicked wife killed him. Aegisthus invited him to a feast and slaughtered him like an ox, and his men were killed like pigs around the tables, among the wine bowls, with blood covering the floor. He heard Cassandra, the daughter of the Trojan king Priam, scream as Clytemnestra killed her beside him. As he lay dying, Clytemnestra would not even close his eyes or mouth. He says her crime has brought shame on all women, even good ones.

Odysseus says Zeus has always hated the family of Atreus because of its women, first Helen and now Clytemnestra. Agamemnon gives him advice. Do not be too kind even to your own wife. Do not tell her everything. Then he adds that Penelope is very good and wise, and will not murder him. He remembers her as a young bride with a baby. Her son must now be a man, and father and son will have a happy meeting, unlike Agamemnon, who never saw his son again. Still, he warns Odysseus to come home secretly, because women cannot be trusted. Agamemnon asks about his son Orestes, but Odysseus has no news. Agamemnon's story is a warning, and it shapes how carefully Odysseus will return home in disguise.

Then came the ghost of Achilles, the greatest Greek warrior, with his friends Patroclus, Antilochus, and the great Ajax. Achilles asked what daring adventure had brought Odysseus down to the dead. Odysseus explained, and then he tried to comfort Achilles. In life the Greeks honoured him like a god, and now he is a great prince among the dead. So he should not be sad about death. Achilles gave a famous answer. He said not to speak kindly of death. He would rather be a poor servant working for a poor farmer on earth than be king over all the dead. This is a powerful statement. Even the greatest hero values simple life more than glory after death.

Achilles asked about his son and his old father Peleus. Odysseus knew nothing of Peleus. But he told Achilles about his son Neoptolemus, whom Odysseus himself brought to Troy. The young man was wise in council and brave in battle. He killed many enemies, including a great hero named Eurypylus. Inside the wooden horse, while the other leaders were shaking and wiping away tears, Neoptolemus never went pale. He kept begging Odysseus to let them out to fight. After Troy fell he went home with rich prizes and no wounds. Achilles was proud. He walked away across a meadow of asphodel flowers with long, happy steps.

Other ghosts told their sad stories. Only one ghost stood apart: Ajax, son of Telamon. He was still angry with Odysseus. After Achilles died, there was a contest for his famous armour. Odysseus won it, and Ajax, in shame and anger, killed himself. Odysseus says he wishes he had never won that contest. He spoke gently to Ajax, asking him to forget his anger, saying that the Greeks mourned him as much as Achilles and that Zeus was to blame. But Ajax said nothing. He turned and walked away into the darkness. His silence is one of the most powerful moments in the book.

Then Odysseus saw other famous figures of the underworld. Minos, son of Zeus, sat as a judge of the dead with a golden staff. The giant hunter Orion was chasing the ghosts of animals he had killed. He also saw famous sinners and their punishments. Tityus, a giant, lay stretched over nine acres while two vultures ate his liver, because he had attacked the goddess Leto. Tantalus stood in a lake up to his chin, but whenever he bent down to drink, the water vanished. Fruit hung over his head, but whenever he reached for it, the wind blew the branches away. This is where the English word "tantalize" comes from. Sisyphus pushed a huge stone up a hill with all his strength, but every time it was near the top, it rolled back down, and he had to start again.

Last he saw the ghost of Heracles, the greatest hero of the old days. Odysseus explains that it was only his shadow. The real Heracles lives with the gods and is married to the goddess Hebe. The shadow looked dark as night, with his bow ready to shoot, and the ghosts fled from him like frightened birds. He wore a wonderful golden belt decorated with bears, boars, lions, battles, and killings. Heracles recognized Odysseus and pitied him. He asked if Odysseus also had a life of endless labour like his. Heracles was a son of Zeus, yet he had to serve a lesser man, who gave him impossible tasks. Once he was even sent to bring the dog of the underworld up from Hades, and he did it with the help of Hermes and Athena.

Odysseus waited, hoping to see other old heroes, like Theseus. But thousands of ghosts came crowding around with terrible cries. He became afraid that Persephone might send up the head of the Gorgon, a monster whose face turns people to stone. So he hurried back to the ship. His men rowed down the river Ocean, and then a good wind began to blow. This journey to the dead teaches Odysseus, and the reader, how precious life and home are.

## Book 12: The Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the Cattle of the Sun

This is the last part of the long story Odysseus tells to the Phaeacians about his past. He describes how his ship left the river Ocean and returned to Circe's island. They slept on the beach. At dawn they fetched the body of Elpenor from Circe's house. They wept for him and gave him a proper funeral. They burned his body with his armour, built a mound of stones, and set his oar on top, as he had asked.

Circe came with her maids, bringing bread, meat, and wine. She said they were bold to go down alive to the house of Hades. Other people die only once, but they will die twice. She told them to feast all day and sail the next morning. That night, while the men slept, Circe took Odysseus aside. He told her about the land of the dead, and then she gave him careful instructions for the dangers ahead.

First, she said, he will meet the Sirens. They sit in a green meadow and sing so sweetly that any sailor who hears them forgets his wife and children and never comes home. Around them lie heaps of bones of dead men, with rotting skin. To pass safely, he must put soft wax in his men's ears. If he wants to hear the song himself, he must have his men tie him upright to the mast. If he begs to be freed, they must tie him even tighter.

After the Sirens there are two possible routes, and Odysseus must choose. One way leads to the Wandering Rocks, where waves crash with terrible force. Not even birds can pass. Only one ship, the famous Argo of the hero Jason, ever got through, and only because the goddess Hera helped it.

The other way passes between two great rocks. On the high rock, whose peak is always in cloud and whose sides are as smooth as polished stone, there is a cave. In it lives Scylla. Her voice sounds like the bark of a young puppy, but she is a horrible monster. She has twelve feet and six very long necks, and on each neck is a head with three rows of close teeth. She reaches out from her cave and catches dolphins, sea creatures, and sailors. No ship has ever passed her without losing men, because each of her six heads grabs one man.

The other rock is lower, and only an arrow's flight away. On it grows a big fig tree. Under it is Charybdis, a terrible whirlpool. Three times a day she swallows the sea water, and three times she throws it back up. If the ship is there when she is swallowing, not even Poseidon can save it. Circe advises him to sail close to Scylla's side and go fast, because it is better to lose six men than the whole crew.

Odysseus asks if there is a way to avoid Charybdis and also fight off Scylla. Circe calls him a daredevil who always wants to fight, even against the gods. Scylla is not mortal. She cannot be defeated. The only thing to do is to row past as fast as possible. If he stops to put on armour and fight, she will catch six more men. He should call out to Scylla's mother, who will stop her from attacking a second time. This choice between two evils is the origin of the English phrase "between Scylla and Charybdis", which means being forced to choose between two dangers.

Finally Circe warns him about the island of Thrinacia, where the Sun god keeps seven herds of cattle and seven flocks of sheep, fifty in each. They never have babies and never die. Two daughters of the Sun look after them. Like Tiresias, Circe says that if they leave the animals unharmed, they may reach home. If they hurt them, the ship and crew will be destroyed, and Odysseus will come home late and alone.

At dawn Circe went away, and the men rowed out. Circe sent a good wind. Odysseus decided to tell the men part of what she had said, so that they could live or die with open eyes. He told them about the Sirens and the plan to tie him to the mast. But soon the wind stopped completely. The men took down the sail and started rowing. Odysseus cut a large piece of wax into small pieces with his sword, softened it in his hands and in the sun, and filled the men's ears. They tied him hand and foot to the mast and kept rowing.

When they came near, the Sirens began to sing. They called Odysseus by name, the glory of the Greeks. They said no one ever sailed past without listening to their sweet voices, and that whoever listens goes away happier and wiser. They know everything that the Greeks and Trojans suffered at Troy, and everything that happens on earth. Odysseus longed to hear more. He frowned and signalled to his men to untie him. But they rowed harder, and two of them, Eurylochus and Perimedes, tied him tighter. When the ship was out of hearing, the men removed the wax and untied him. The Sirens tempt Odysseus not with pleasure but with knowledge, especially stories about his own glory. He is clever enough to plan for his own weakness.

Soon after, Odysseus saw spray and heard a huge roaring. The terrified men dropped their oars, and the ship stopped. Odysseus went among them and encouraged them. He reminded them that they had survived the Cyclops's cave through his courage and planning, and they would survive this too. He told them to row hard and trust Zeus. He told the steersman to keep the ship away from the boiling water and close to the rock.

But Odysseus did not tell them about Scylla. He knew that if they knew, they would stop rowing and hide in the bottom of the ship. Here he makes a hard leader's choice. He disobeyed Circe in one thing: he put on his armour, took two spears, and stood at the front of the ship, ready to fight Scylla. But he could not see her anywhere on the dark rock.

They entered the narrow strait. On one side Charybdis was swallowing water. When she spat it out, the water boiled like a pot on a fire, and spray flew to the tops of the rocks. When she sucked it down, they could see the black sand at the bottom of the sea. The men were sick with fear. While everyone was watching Charybdis, Scylla struck. Her six heads came down and snatched up Odysseus's six best men. He saw their hands and feet high above him in the air, and he heard them call his name for the last time. Scylla ate them at the mouth of her cave while they screamed and reached out to him, like fish caught by a fisherman. Odysseus says this was the most terrible sight he saw in all his travels.

Past Scylla and Charybdis, they came near the island of the Sun. From the ship they could hear the cattle mooing and the sheep bleating. Odysseus remembered the warnings of Tiresias and Circe. He told the men about the prophecy and ordered them to sail past the island.

The men were in despair. Eurylochus answered rudely. He said Odysseus was hard and cruel, made of iron, and never tired, while the men were exhausted. He wanted them to land, cook supper, and sleep on the shore. Sailing at night was dangerous, he said, because storms come suddenly in the dark. The men agreed with Eurylochus. Odysseus felt that some god was planning harm for them. He said he must give in, because he was one man against many. But he made them all swear a solemn oath that they would not kill any cattle or sheep, and would eat only the food Circe had given them. They swore, landed, ate, and cried for their friends eaten by Scylla until they fell asleep.

In the night, Zeus sent a great storm. In the morning they pulled the ship into a cave. Odysseus reminded them again not to touch the animals, because they belong to the Sun, who sees and hears everything. They promised again. But for a whole month the wind blew only from the south and east, and they could not leave. While the food and wine lasted, the men did not touch the cattle. When the food ran out, they hunted birds and fished with hooks, because they were starving.

One day Odysseus went inland alone to pray to the gods for a way to escape. After washing his hands and praying, he fell into a sweet sleep sent by the gods. Meanwhile Eurylochus gave the men evil advice. He said every death is bad, but starving is the worst. Why not kill the best cattle and sacrifice them to the gods? If they reached Ithaca, they could build a rich temple to the Sun god to make up for it. And if the god sank their ship in anger, he would rather drown quickly than starve slowly on this island. The men agreed.

They drove the best cows in, prayed, and killed them. They had no barley, so they used oak leaves instead. They had no wine, so they poured water. They burned the thigh bones for the gods and roasted the meat. This sacrifice is wrong in every way. The animals are stolen from a god, and the ritual is done with the wrong materials.

Odysseus woke up and walked back. When he smelled roasting meat, he groaned and cried out to Zeus and the gods that they had ruined him by sending him to sleep. Meanwhile, Lampetie, one of the Sun's daughters, told her father. The Sun was furious. He told Zeus that unless the men were punished, he would go down to Hades and shine among the dead instead of the living. Zeus told him to keep shining for gods and men. He promised to destroy their ship with a thunderbolt as soon as it reached the open sea. Odysseus explains that he learned this conversation from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes. This shows him as a careful storyteller who explains how he knows things he did not see.

Odysseus scolded the men, but the cows were already dead. Then the gods sent strange signs. The skins of the dead cattle crawled on the ground, and the meat on the spits, both raw and cooked, made mooing sounds. Still, for six days the men kept killing and eating the best cattle. On the seventh day the wind dropped, and they sailed away.

When they were out at sea, with no land in sight, Zeus placed a black cloud over the ship. A violent west wind broke the mast, which fell and crushed the head of the steersman. He fell into the sea like a diver. Then Zeus struck the ship with lightning. It spun around, full of fire and the smell of sulphur. All the men fell into the sea and floated around like seabirds, and then they drowned. Not one of them reached home. This fulfils the poem's very first lines, which said the men died because of their own foolishness in eating the cattle of the Sun.

Odysseus held on to the ship until the waves broke it apart. He used a leather rope to tie the mast and the keel together and sat on them, carried by the wind. Then the wind turned to the south, and to his horror he was carried back toward Charybdis. By sunrise he had reached the strait. Charybdis was swallowing the sea, and his raft was sucked down. Odysseus jumped up and grabbed the fig tree above the whirlpool. He hung there like a bat. He could not climb up or rest his feet. He waited a long time for the whirlpool to spit out his timbers. He says he was as glad to see them return as a judge is to go home to supper after a long day in court. He let go, fell into the water beside his raft, climbed on, and paddled with his hands. Zeus did not let Scylla see him, or he would have died.

For nine days he drifted. On the tenth night the gods brought him to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso lives. She took him in and was kind to him. But Odysseus ends his story here. He says he already told the king and queen about Calypso yesterday, and he hates to tell the same story twice. With this, the long flashback is complete, and we are back in the Phaeacian palace, where Odysseus's journey to Calypso's island connects with the beginning of the poem.

## Book 13: Odysseus Wakes Up in Ithaca

When Odysseus finishes his story, the Phaeacians sit in silence, enchanted. Then King Alcinous speaks. He tells Odysseus that now he has reached this house, he will surely get home without more trouble. He asks each of the lords to give the guest an extra gift, a large tripod and a cauldron. A tripod is a three-legged metal pot or stand, a valuable gift in this world. Alcinous says they will collect the cost later from the people, because one person should not carry such a large expense alone. Everyone agrees and goes home to sleep.

In the morning the lords bring the bronze gifts to the ship. Alcinous himself makes sure everything is packed safely under the benches, so it will not hurt the rowers. Then they return to the palace and sacrifice a bull to Zeus. They feast, and the blind singer Demodocus sings. But Odysseus keeps looking at the sun, wishing it would go down faster. The poem compares him to a tired ploughman who has worked all day and longs for supper. When the sun finally sets, Odysseus says goodbye. He thanks the Phaeacians for the gifts and the escort. He prays that he will find his good wife and his friends safe at home, and that the Phaeacians will be happy with their wives and children.

Wine is poured for Zeus. Odysseus places his cup in the hands of Queen Arete. He wishes her happiness with her children, her people, and the king, until old age and death come to her, as they come to everyone. Then he leaves. Servants carry his clothes, his chest of gifts, and food to the ship. The sailors spread a rug and a linen sheet on the deck. Odysseus lies down silently, and as the men begin to row he falls into a deep, sweet sleep, very like death.

The ship flies over the sea faster than a falcon, like a team of four horses racing under the whip. It carries a man as clever as the gods, now sleeping peacefully and forgetting all he suffered in war and at sea. Just before dawn, as the morning star appears, the ship reaches Ithaca.

There is a harbour on Ithaca named after Phorcys, an old sea god. Two headlands protect it, so ships can stay inside without even being tied up. At the top of the harbour is a large olive tree. Near it is a beautiful cave sacred to the Naiads, the water nymphs. Inside are stone bowls and jars, bees, and stone looms where the nymphs weave sea-purple cloth. The cave has two entrances, one for humans and one for the gods.

The Phaeacians know the place. They row so fast that the ship slides half its length onto the beach. They lift Odysseus out, still asleep on his rug, and lay him on the sand. They place his treasure by the olive tree, away from the path, so that no one will steal it before he wakes. Then they sail home.

But Poseidon has not forgotten his anger. He goes to Zeus and complains. He says the gods will not respect him any more, because the Phaeacians, who are his own descendants, have ignored his wishes. He admits that he never said Odysseus would never get home, since Zeus had already promised it. But now the Phaeacians have brought him home asleep, with more treasure than he would have won at Troy. Zeus answers that the gods still honour Poseidon, and he may punish the mortals however he likes. Poseidon wants to destroy the Phaeacian ship on its return and bury their city under a mountain. Zeus suggests something more dramatic. When the people are watching their ship come home, Poseidon should turn it into a rock shaped like a ship. Then he can bury the city under the mountain.

So Poseidon goes to Scheria. As the ship approaches the harbour, he touches it, turns it into stone, and pushes it into the sea floor with the flat of his hand. The Phaeacians are amazed. They ask who has rooted their ship in the sea, when they could see it moving only a moment ago. Alcinous remembers his father's prophecy that Poseidon would be angry with them for giving everyone safe journeys, and would destroy a ship and bury their city. He tells the people that they must stop giving escorts to strangers, and sacrifice twelve bulls to Poseidon, so that he might spare their city. The people are frightened and prepare the bulls. The poem leaves them praying at Poseidon's altar, and we never learn whether the city is buried. The Phaeacians pay a high price for their kindness to Odysseus.

At the same moment, Odysseus wakes up on his own land. But he does not recognize it. He has been away a long time, and Athena has covered the island with mist. She wants to keep him unseen, so she can explain everything and so that neither his wife, nor his people, nor his friends will know him before he takes revenge on the suitors. Everything looks strange to him: the paths, the harbours, the cliffs, and the trees. He slaps his thighs in despair. He wonders what kind of people live here, and whether they are savage or kind to strangers. He wishes he had stayed with the Phaeacians. He thinks they tricked him and left him in the wrong place, and he asks Zeus to punish them. Then, practical as always, he counts his treasure to see if the crew stole anything. Nothing is missing. Still he walks along the shore, weeping for his homeland.

Athena comes to him disguised as a young shepherd, elegant like a prince, with a fine cloak, sandals, and a spear. Odysseus is glad. He greets the young man respectfully, asks him to protect him and his treasure, and asks what land this is. Is it an island or part of the mainland?

Athena answers that he must be very foolish or come from very far away not to know. This is a famous place, she says. It is rocky and not good for horses, but it is not poor. It grows grain and grapes, has rain and dew, feeds goats and cattle, has all kinds of trees and springs that never dry. Its name is known even in Troy. The name is Ithaca.

Odysseus is filled with joy. But he does not show it, and he does not tell the truth. Instead, by instinct, he invents a lying story. He says he heard of Ithaca when he was in Crete. He says he fled Crete with this treasure, leaving as much again for his children, because he killed Orsilochus, the son of King Idomeneus and the fastest runner in Crete. The young man tried to take his share of the treasure from Troy, claiming that he had not served his father loyally. So, he says, he hid by the road with a friend and killed him at night with a spear. Then he paid some Phoenician sailors to take him to Pylos or Elis. But the wind blew them here. They put him and his treasure on the shore while he slept, and sailed away to Sidon.

Athena smiles and touches him with her hand. Now she takes the form of a tall, beautiful, wise woman. She laughs at him. Anyone who could beat him at tricks would have to be a master liar, even a god. He is a daredevil, full of tricks, never tired of lying. Even in his own country he cannot stop. But she says they are both the same. He is the best planner and speaker among men, and she is the most clever among the gods. Did he not know that she was Athena, who has always been with him, protected him, and made the Phaeacians love him? She has come to help him hide his treasure and tell him about the troubles waiting in his house. He must tell no one that he has returned, and he must patiently accept every insult in silence. This scene is warm and even playful. The goddess clearly loves Odysseus because his mind is like hers.

Odysseus answers honestly. He says it is hard for a man to recognize her, because she keeps changing her shape. He knows she was kind to him during the war. But after Troy fell, he never saw her help him again until the Phaeacian land. He had to wander alone and in pain. Now he begs her to tell him the truth. Is he really in Ithaca, or is she mocking him?

Athena says this suspicion is exactly why she cannot abandon him, because he is so sensible, sharp, and careful. Any other man would rush home to see his wife and children. But he wants to test his wife first, even though she spends her days and nights crying for him. As for why she stayed away, she says she always knew he would get home, though he would lose all his men. And she did not want to quarrel with her uncle Poseidon, who never forgave Odysseus for blinding his son. Then she shows him the land. This is the harbour of Phorcys, here is the olive tree, here is the cave of the nymphs where he often made sacrifices, and there is the forested mountain Neritum.

She clears away the mist, and Odysseus sees his home. He is overjoyed. He kisses the earth. He prays to the nymphs and promises them gifts as in the old days, if Athena lets him live and see his son grow to manhood. Athena tells him not to worry. They carry all the gold, bronze, and clothing into the cave and hide it. Athena puts a stone over the entrance.

Then they sit under the olive tree and plan how to destroy the suitors. Athena tells him that for three years these shameless men have ruled his house and courted his wife. Penelope weeps for him, but she gives each suitor hope with messages, while meaning the opposite. Odysseus answers that without Athena's warning, he might have died in his own house like Agamemnon. He asks her to stand beside him and give him courage, as she did at the fall of Troy. With her help he would fight three hundred men.

Athena promises to stay with him. Some of the suitors will soon cover the floor with their blood. She will disguise him so no one knows him. She will wrinkle his skin, remove his fair hair, dull his bright eyes, and dress him in disgusting rags. He will look ugly to the suitors, to his wife, and to his son. Then he must go to Eumaeus, the swineherd, the man who keeps his pigs. Eumaeus has always been loyal to Odysseus and loves Penelope and Telemachus. He keeps the pigs near a rock called Raven, by the spring of Arethusa. Odysseus must stay with him and learn everything, while Athena goes to Sparta to bring Telemachus home.

Odysseus asks why Athena did not simply tell his son the truth, instead of making him wander at sea while others ate his property. Athena says she sent Telemachus so that people would speak well of him. He is safe and comfortable with Menelaus. The suitors are waiting in a ship to kill him, but she does not think they will succeed. More likely, some of them will die first.

Then Athena touches Odysseus with her wand. His skin wrinkles, his hair disappears, his eyes become dull, and his body looks old and weak. She dresses him in a dirty torn shirt and cloak, black with smoke, and a rough deerskin. She gives him a staff and a beggar's bag full of holes, hanging from a twisted strap. Now the king of Ithaca looks like a poor old beggar. They separate, and Athena flies to Sparta to bring Telemachus back. This disguise begins the second half of the poem, where Odysseus must use patience and secrecy, not force, to win back his home.

## Book 14: In the Hut of Eumaeus, the Loyal Pig-Keeper

Odysseus, now disguised as an old beggar, leaves the harbour and climbs a rough path through the woods and over the hills. He reaches the place where Athena said he would find Eumaeus, the swineherd, or pig-keeper. Eumaeus is the most careful and hard-working of all his servants. He is sitting in front of his hut. While his master was away, Eumaeus built large pig yards out of stones, with a fence of thorn bushes and strong oak posts, without even asking Penelope or Laertes for help. Inside are twelve pens, each with fifty female pigs. The male pigs sleep outside, and there are only three hundred and sixty of them, because the suitors keep eating them. Every day Eumaeus must send the best pig to the palace. Four dogs, fierce as wolves, guard the pigs. At this moment Eumaeus is cutting a pair of sandals from a piece of leather. His helpers are out with the herds, and one has gone to town with a pig for the suitors.

When the dogs see the stranger, they rush at him, barking wildly. Odysseus cleverly sits down and drops his stick, which is the way to calm angry dogs. Even so, they might have torn him apart. But Eumaeus runs out, shouts at the dogs, and throws stones until they go away. He tells the old man that the dogs almost killed him, which would have brought Eumaeus more trouble, and he already has enough sorrow. He has lost the best of masters. He must raise pigs for other men to eat, while his master may be starving in some far country. Then he invites the stranger inside to eat and drink, and only then to tell his story.

Eumaeus makes a soft seat of brushwood and covers it with the thick goatskin he uses as his own bed. Odysseus thanks him and prays that Zeus will give him whatever he wishes. Eumaeus answers that it would not be right to insult even a poorer stranger, because all strangers and beggars come from Zeus. Here the poet does something unusual. He speaks directly to Eumaeus, saying "you answered, O swineherd Eumaeus." Homer does this several times, and it shows special affection for this humble, loyal man.

Eumaeus explains that servants live in fear under young masters. His old master would have rewarded him with a house, land, and a wife, as a good master does for a loyal servant. But he is gone. Eumaeus curses Helen, because the war for her killed so many good men, and it took his master to Troy.

Then he goes to the pens, chooses two young pigs, kills them, roasts them, and serves the hot meat to his guest. He mixes wine in a wooden bowl. He apologizes that this is only servants' pork. The fat pigs go to the suitors, who eat them without shame or fear of the gods. Even pirates, he says, feel fear of the gods' judgement when they return home with their loot. But the suitors must think Odysseus is dead, because they do not court Penelope in the proper way. They just waste his wealth by force, killing many animals every day and drinking his wine. Eumaeus describes how rich his master was. He had twelve herds of cattle on the mainland, twelve flocks of sheep, twelve herds of pigs, and twelve herds of goats, plus more goats on Ithaca. Every herdsman must send the best animal to the suitors each day.

Odysseus eats and drinks silently and hungrily, while he quietly plans his revenge. Then Eumaeus gives him his own cup of wine. Odysseus asks who this rich master was. He has travelled a lot, so perhaps he has met him.

Eumaeus says no traveller with news will be believed by Odysseus's wife and son any more. Many beggars come to Ithaca and tell Penelope lies to get food and clothes. She welcomes them, asks them questions, and cries. Eumaeus thinks the old man will do the same for a shirt and a cloak. He is sure that his master's body has been eaten by dogs and birds, or by fish, and his bones lie on some foreign shore. He says he will never find so good a master, not even if he went back to his own parents. He grieves for Odysseus more than for them. He respects him so much that even now he does not like to say his name casually.

Odysseus answers that Eumaeus is too stubborn in his disbelief. He swears an oath that Odysseus is coming home. He says he does not want a reward for this news until Odysseus has actually returned. Then Eumaeus can give him a shirt and cloak. He says he hates a man who lies because he is poor as much as he hates the gates of death. He swears by Zeus, by the table of hospitality, and by the hearth of Odysseus, that Odysseus will return this very year, between the end of this month and the start of the next, and will punish everyone who has wronged his wife and son. There is irony here. Odysseus is telling the truth while pretending to be a liar.

Eumaeus says he will not pay for good news because Odysseus will never come. He asks him to talk about something else, because it hurts to remember his master. He wishes Odysseus would come, as Penelope, Laertes, and Telemachus all wish. He is also worried about Telemachus, who was growing into a fine young man. Someone, god or man, has disturbed his mind, and he has gone to Pylos for news. Now the suitors are waiting to ambush and kill him on his return, so that the family of Odysseus will disappear from Ithaca. Then Eumaeus asks the stranger to tell his own story.

Odysseus tells a long false story. Many of the details sound like his real life, mixed with inventions. He says he is from Crete. His father, Castor, was a rich man, and his mother was a slave. His father treated him like his other sons, but when the father died, his brothers gave him only a small share. Still, he married a woman from a rich family, because of his courage. He was a great fighter. He loved ships, battles, spears, and arrows, not farm work or quiet family life. Before the Trojan War he led nine raids overseas and grew rich. Then the people of Crete made him and King Idomeneus lead their ships to Troy. After ten years of war he came home, but stayed only a month with his family. Then he decided to raid Egypt with nine ships.

When they arrived in Egypt, he says, he told his men to guard the ships while scouts looked around. But his men disobeyed. They attacked the farms, killed the Egyptian men, and took the women and children. The Egyptians came out in a great army at dawn. Zeus filled the raiders with panic. Many were killed, and others were taken as slaves. The stranger says he threw down his helmet, shield, and spear, ran to the king's chariot, and kissed his knees, begging for mercy. The king spared him and protected him from the angry soldiers, because he respected Zeus, protector of strangers. This part of the story echoes the real disaster with the Cicones in Book 9, where Odysseus's men also refused to obey.

He says he lived in Egypt for seven years and became rich from gifts. In the eighth year, a cunning Phoenician tricked him into coming to Phoenicia. After a year there, the Phoenician put him on a ship to Libya, secretly planning to sell him as a slave. But Zeus destroyed the ship with lightning, and all the men drowned. He held on to the mast and drifted for nine days. On the tenth night, he reached the land of the Thesprotians. There King Pheidon's son found him half dead with cold, and the king gave him clothes and hospitality.

Then comes the clever part. In Thesprotia, he says, he heard news of Odysseus. King Pheidon told him that Odysseus had been his guest and showed him a huge treasure of bronze, gold, and iron that Odysseus had left there, enough to feed his family for ten generations. The king said Odysseus had gone to Dodona to ask the sacred oak tree of Zeus whether he should return to Ithaca openly or in secret. The king swore that a ship was ready to take him home. But before Odysseus returned from Dodona, the king sent the stranger away on a ship to Dulichium. The sailors plotted to sell him as a slave. They took his good clothes and gave him these rags. Near Ithaca, they tied him up and went ashore for supper. But the gods loosened the ropes. He covered his head with his rags, slid down the rudder into the sea, swam to shore, and hid in the woods. The sailors searched and then gave up. And so the gods brought him to the door of a good man.

Eumaeus is moved by the story of the stranger's suffering. But he does not believe the part about Odysseus. He thinks the gods hate his master. If Odysseus had died at Troy, he would at least have had a grave and lasting fame. Now the storm winds have carried him away to nowhere. Eumaeus says he rarely goes to town now, except when Penelope calls him because of some news. He stopped believing such stories after an Aetolian man, a killer on the run, came to his hut. Eumaeus was kind to him, and the man claimed he had seen Odysseus in Crete with Idomeneus, repairing his ships, and that he would return that summer or autumn with great wealth. It was a lie. So Eumaeus tells the stranger not to try to please him with false hopes. He will treat him kindly anyway, out of respect for Zeus, god of guests, and out of pity.

Odysseus offers a bet. If Odysseus comes home, Eumaeus must give him a shirt and cloak and send him to Dulichium. If not, Eumaeus can have his men throw him off a cliff, as a warning to lying beggars. Eumaeus replies that he would look very good, wouldn't he, if he welcomed a man as his guest and then killed him. He would need to pray hard after that. Then he says it is time for supper.

The other herdsmen come home with the pigs, which squeal loudly as they are shut in for the night. Eumaeus tells them to bring the best boar, a fat five-year-old pig, to sacrifice for their guest. Eumaeus is a religious man. First he throws some hairs from the pig's head into the fire and prays that Odysseus will come home. Then they kill the pig and roast it. Eumaeus divides the meat fairly into seven portions. One portion he sets aside for Hermes and the nymphs. He gives Odysseus the best part, the long cut from the back, as a special honour. Odysseus is pleased. He prays that Zeus will love Eumaeus as much as he does, for treating a poor outcast with such respect. Eumaeus answers simply that God gives one thing and takes another as he pleases. A servant named Mesaulius, whom Eumaeus bought with his own money, serves the bread. They eat and drink, and then prepare for bed.

The night is cold, dark, and rainy, with no moon, and a strong wet west wind. Odysseus wants to test Eumaeus. Will the swineherd give him his own cloak, or ask one of his men to give one? So he tells a story, saying the wine makes him talk too much. He describes a night at Troy, when he lay in an ambush in the reeds near the city walls with Odysseus and Menelaus. It was freezing, with snow falling and ice on their shields. He had foolishly left his cloak behind and was wearing only a shirt. In the middle of the night he told Odysseus, who was lying next to him, that he would die of cold. Odysseus, clever as always, quietly raised his head and told the others that he had had a dream from the gods. They were far from the ships, he said, and someone should run back and ask Agamemnon for more men. A soldier named Thoas jumped up, threw off his cloak, and ran to the ships. The storyteller then wrapped himself in the cloak and slept warmly. Now he wishes he were young and strong again, because then someone here would give him a cloak.

Eumaeus understands the hint and likes the story. He says the stranger will have a cloak and whatever else he needs tonight. But tomorrow he must put on his own rags again, because they have few spare clothes. When Telemachus comes home, he will surely give him a cloak and shirt and send him wherever he wants. Eumaeus makes a bed by the fire with sheep and goat skins and covers Odysseus with a big heavy cloak that he keeps for very bad weather.

The young men sleep around Odysseus. But Eumaeus does not want to sleep away from his pigs. He puts on his sword and a thick cloak, takes a goatskin and a spear to protect himself from men and dogs, and goes out into the storm to sleep under a rock near the pigs. Odysseus is glad to see how carefully Eumaeus protects his master's property, even when his master is away. This book is a quiet portrait of loyalty and hospitality from a humble man, which is the opposite of the greed of the suitors.

## Book 15: Telemachus Sails Home, and Eumaeus Tells His Story

Athena goes to Sparta to bring Telemachus home. She finds him in the porch of Menelaus's palace, lying awake and thinking about his father. His friend Pisistratus, Nestor's son, is asleep. Athena stands near him and warns him that he should not stay so long away from home, leaving his property with dangerous men. She says Penelope's father and brothers are pushing her to marry Eurymachus, who has offered the largest wedding gifts. She warns that a woman who remarries tends to care for her new husband and forget the children of her first marriage. This is probably meant to push Telemachus to act, not a fair picture of Penelope. Athena tells him to go home and put the house under the care of the most trustworthy woman servant until he has a wife of his own.

She also warns him of the suitors' ambush. Their leaders are waiting in the narrow sea between Ithaca and Samos to kill him. He must sail day and night and keep away from the islands. A god will send him a good wind. When he reaches Ithaca, he must send his ship and men to the town, but go first, alone, to the hut of the swineherd, who is loyal to him. He should spend the night there and send the swineherd to tell Penelope he is home safe.

Telemachus kicks Pisistratus awake with his heel and tells him to harness the horses. Pisistratus says that they cannot drive in the dark. They must wait for morning, receive Menelaus's gifts, and say goodbye properly. A guest should always remember a kind host.

At dawn Menelaus comes out, and Telemachus asks to leave. Menelaus agrees. He says that a good host is neither too eager to keep a guest nor too quick to send him away. Moderation is best. One should treat a guest well while he stays, and help him on his way when he wants to go. Menelaus even offers to take Telemachus on a tour of Greece, where every city would give them gifts. But Telemachus says he must go home at once, because his property is unprotected.

Menelaus orders breakfast. He goes to his storeroom with Helen and his son Megapenthes. He chooses a double cup, and his son brings the silver mixing bowl with a gold rim, made by Hephaestus, which Menelaus promised earlier. Helen chooses the largest and most beautiful robe that she embroidered herself. It shines like a star. Menelaus gives his gifts and prays that Zeus will bring Telemachus home safely. Helen gives the robe as a keepsake from her own hand. She says it is for his bride to wear on her wedding day, and his mother should keep it until then.

After breakfast the young men get into the chariot. Menelaus stands before the horses with a golden cup of wine and asks them to give Nestor his greetings, because Nestor was like a father to him at Troy. Telemachus promises, and says he wishes he could also find Odysseus at home and tell him about this kindness.

At that moment an eagle flies by on their right. In its claws is a large white goose from a farmyard, and people are running after it and shouting. The eagle passes close in front of the horses. Everyone is happy, because a bird on the right is a good sign. Pisistratus asks Menelaus whether the omen is for him or for them. While Menelaus is thinking how to answer, Helen speaks first. She says the eagle came from the mountains where it was born, and in the same way Odysseus will come home after long wandering and take revenge. Perhaps he is already home, planning trouble for the suitors. Telemachus says if this comes true, he will pray to Helen as to a goddess. Then they drive away.

They spend the night again at Pherae with Diocles. The next day they reach Pylos. Telemachus asks his friend a favour. He asks Pisistratus not to take him to Nestor's house, because the old man would want to keep him, out of kindness, and Telemachus must hurry home. Pisistratus thinks about it and agrees. He drives straight to the ship and loads the gifts. He tells Telemachus to leave quickly, before he tells his father. He knows Nestor is stubborn and would come down himself to bring Telemachus back, and he will be angry. Telemachus gathers his crew and prepares to sail.

While Telemachus is praying and sacrificing to Athena at the back of the ship, a stranger comes to him. He is Theoclymenus, a prophet from a famous family of seers. The poem tells his family history. His ancestor was Melampus, a rich man of Pylos, who was driven out by King Neleus, suffered imprisonment, and later won a bride for his brother by bringing Neleus a herd of cattle. Melampus then went to Argos and ruled there. His descendants included famous prophets. Theoclymenus is now running away from Argos because he killed a man of his own family. The dead man's relatives are powerful and are chasing him.

Theoclymenus asks Telemachus who he is. Telemachus says he is from Ithaca, the son of Odysseus, who must have died somewhere miserably. Theoclymenus explains his situation and begs as a suppliant to come on board so his enemies will not kill him. Telemachus agrees and promises him hospitality in Ithaca. He takes the man's spear and seats him beside him at the stern. They raise the mast and sail, and Athena sends a strong, good wind. They sail through the night, past several places. Telemachus steers toward the islands, wondering whether he will escape death or be captured.

Meanwhile, back in Eumaeus's hut, Odysseus decides to test the swineherd again. Will he keep the stranger longer, or send him away to town? After supper, Odysseus says he wants to leave in the morning and beg in the city, so that he will not be a burden. He asks for a guide. He says he wants to visit the house of Odysseus, give Penelope news, and see if the suitors will feed him. He boasts that he would make an excellent servant for them. He is good at making fires, cutting wood, cooking, carving, and pouring wine.

Eumaeus is shocked. He says that if the stranger goes near the suitors he will surely be killed, because their pride and violence reach up to the sky. They would never take an old man like him as a servant. Their servants are handsome young men with fine clothes and neat hair. He tells him to stay. No one minds him here. When Telemachus comes, he will give him clothes and send him where he wishes. Odysseus thanks him warmly. He says there is nothing worse than wandering as a beggar. Since he will stay, he asks about Odysseus's mother and father. Are they still alive?

Eumaeus says Laertes is still alive. But he prays every day for death. He suffers because of his missing son and because of his wife's death, which made him old before his time. She died of sorrow for her son, and Eumaeus wishes such a sad death on no one who has been kind to him. While she lived, Eumaeus liked to visit her. She raised him together with her own youngest daughter, Ctimene, almost as if he were her own child. When they grew up, Ctimene was married to a man on the island of Same, and Eumaeus was given good clothes and sent to work in the country. His mistress always loved him. But now Penelope cannot show kindness to the servants, because the house is full of wicked men. Servants like to talk with their mistress and receive small gifts. That is what keeps them happy.

Odysseus asks how Eumaeus came to Ithaca. Was his city destroyed, or was he kidnapped while he was watching animals? Eumaeus says the nights are long, so there is time for stories. When a man has suffered much, he enjoys remembering old sorrows.

He tells his story. He was born on an island called Syria, a rich land with good pastures, wine, and wheat. There is no hunger or disease there. People die only when they are old, struck gently by the arrows of Apollo and Artemis. His father, Ctesius, was the king of both cities on the island. So Eumaeus, now a pig-keeper, was born a prince.

One day some Phoenician traders came to the island with a ship full of cheap pretty things. In his father's house there was a tall, beautiful Phoenician woman servant. One of the traders seduced her by the ship. She told him that she came from Sidon and was the daughter of a rich man, but pirates had kidnapped her and sold her here. The trader offered to take her home to her parents. She agreed, if they swore not to harm her. She asked them to keep the plan secret. She promised to bring gold, and also something else to pay her fare: she was the nurse of the king's little son, and she would bring the child, whom they could sell for a high price in a foreign land.

The traders stayed a whole year, loading their ship. When they were ready, a messenger came to the palace with a gold and amber necklace. While Eumaeus's mother and her maids were looking at it and discussing the price, the messenger signalled to the woman. She took the little boy by the hand and led him out. On the way she took three golden cups from the tables where guests had been feasting. The boy followed her, because he did not understand. They hurried in the dark to the ship and sailed away. After six days, on the seventh day, Artemis struck the woman dead, and she fell into the ship's hold like a seabird diving into water. The sailors threw her body into the sea for the seals and fish. The little boy was left alone and sad. The wind brought the ship to Ithaca, where Laertes bought him. That is how Eumaeus came to this land.

Odysseus says he is truly sorry for him. But Zeus has given him good things along with the bad. He has a kind master who gives him enough food and drink, and a good life, while the stranger must still beg from city to city. They talk until there is only a little time left for sleep before dawn. Eumaeus's story shows that a slave can be noble in birth and in character. His loyalty is a choice, not just a duty.

Meanwhile, at dawn, Telemachus and his crew reach Ithaca safely, avoiding the ambush. They lower the sail, row into a harbour, and eat breakfast on the shore. Telemachus tells the crew to take the ship to the town. He will go to visit his herdsmen and come to the city in the evening. Tomorrow he will give them a feast to thank them.

Theoclymenus asks where he should go. Telemachus says that normally he would send him to his own house, but now it would not be comfortable. He himself will be away, and his mother rarely comes down, because she stays upstairs weaving, away from the suitors. He suggests instead the house of Eurymachus, son of Polybus, who is the most respected suitor and the most eager to marry his mother and take Odysseus's place. But only Zeus knows whether they will all die before the wedding.

As he speaks, a bird flies on his right. It is a hawk, the messenger of Apollo, holding a dove and tearing out its feathers, which fall between Telemachus and the ship. Theoclymenus takes him aside. He says a god sent this bird as an omen. It means that no family in Ithaca will be more royal than his, and his family will always be strong. Telemachus says if this is true, he will reward him richly.

Then Telemachus changes his plan for the guest. He asks his loyal friend Piraeus, who served him well on the journey, to take the stranger home and look after him. Piraeus agrees gladly. The ship sails to the city. Telemachus ties on his sandals, takes a strong bronze-tipped spear, and walks quickly up to the farm where his pigs are kept, to the hut of the good swineherd Eumaeus. So father and son are now moving toward the same small hut, without knowing it.

## Book 16: Father and Son Meet Again

At dawn Odysseus and Eumaeus, the loyal pig-keeper, light a fire in the hut and prepare breakfast. The other herdsmen have gone out with the pigs. Then Odysseus hears footsteps. The fierce dogs do not bark. Instead they wag their tails and greet the newcomer. Odysseus says it must be one of Eumaeus's friends.

Before he finishes speaking, his son Telemachus is standing in the doorway. Eumaeus jumps up so suddenly that the wine bowls fall from his hands. He runs to Telemachus and kisses his head, his eyes, and his hands, crying with joy. The poem compares him to a father welcoming back his only son, born in his old age, after ten years abroad. Eumaeus says he thought he would never see the young man again after he left for Pylos. He teases him a little, saying Telemachus rarely visits the farm because he stays in town to watch the suitors.

Telemachus says he came to see Eumaeus and to learn whether his mother is still at home, or whether she has married someone, leaving the bed of Odysseus empty and covered with spider webs. Eumaeus says Penelope is still at home, weeping day and night.

Telemachus comes inside. Odysseus, disguised as an old beggar, stands up to give him his seat. But Telemachus politely tells the stranger to sit down. He can find another seat. This is a lovely moment, because the son is showing good manners to his own father without knowing it. Eumaeus makes a seat of green branches and a sheepskin and serves them cold meat from the day before, bread, and wine.

After eating, Telemachus asks who the stranger is. Eumaeus says he claims to be from Crete, a great traveller, and has escaped from a ship of the Thesprotians. He puts the stranger in Telemachus's hands as a suppliant. Telemachus is troubled. He says he is young and cannot protect a guest in his house against so many men. His mother cannot decide whether to stay faithful to her husband's memory or marry the best suitor. He offers to give the stranger good clothes, a sword, and sandals, and to send him wherever he wants. Or he can stay here, and Telemachus will send food and clothes. But he must not go near the suitors. They are violent and would mistreat him, and one man cannot fight so many.

Odysseus, still in disguise, speaks with feeling. He is shocked that the suitors behave like this to a man like Telemachus. Does he accept it quietly? Are the people against him? Does he have no brothers to help him? He says if he were young, and the son of Odysseus, or Odysseus himself, he would rather die fighting in his own house than watch such shameful things every day: guests insulted, servant women dragged around the house, and wine and bread wasted.

Telemachus explains that the people do not hate him, but he has no brothers. Zeus has made his family a line of only sons. Laertes was the only son of his father, Odysseus was the only son of Laertes, and Telemachus is the only son of Odysseus. So he has no one to help him. The leading men of all the nearby islands are eating his house while courting his mother. Then he sends Eumaeus to tell Penelope privately that her son is home safe from Pylos. No one else must know, because many people want to harm him.

Eumaeus asks if he should also tell old Laertes. He says that since Telemachus left for Pylos, Laertes has not eaten or drunk properly. He does not look after his farm, but sits crying, and his body is wasting away. Telemachus is sorry, but tells Eumaeus to go straight to Penelope and come back. His mother can send a maid secretly to tell Laertes. So Eumaeus puts on his sandals and goes to town.

As soon as Eumaeus leaves, Athena comes to the farm in the form of a tall, beautiful, wise woman. She stands by the gate. Odysseus sees her, and so do the dogs, who whine and run away in fear. But Telemachus cannot see her, because the gods do not show themselves to everyone. Athena signals to Odysseus with her eyebrows. He goes outside. She tells him it is time to reveal himself to his son, and together they must plan the death of the suitors. She will soon join them, because she is eager for the fight.

She touches him with her golden wand. He is suddenly dressed in a clean shirt and cloak. He becomes younger and stronger. His skin becomes tanned again, his cheeks fill out, and his beard becomes dark. Then Athena leaves, and Odysseus goes back into the hut.

Telemachus is astonished and frightened. He turns his eyes away, thinking that this must be a god. He says the stranger has changed completely, in clothes and in colour. He begs him to be kind and promises sacrifices and golden gifts.

Odysseus answers simply. He is not a god. He is Telemachus's father, the man for whom he has suffered so much at the hands of lawless men. He kisses his son, and tears run down his face. Until now he has held back all his tears.

But Telemachus cannot believe it. He thinks a god is tricking him with false hope, to give him more pain later. No mortal man could make himself old and then young again in a moment. Odysseus explains that there is no other Odysseus who will come. He is the one, back in his own country in the twentieth year, after much wandering and suffering. The change is Athena's work. She can make him look like a beggar one moment and a fine young man the next. It is easy for the gods to make a man look rich or poor.

Then Odysseus sits down. Telemachus throws his arms around his father and cries. Both of them weep loudly, like eagles or vultures crying when farmers have stolen their young from the nest before they could fly. They might have cried until sunset. This is one of the most emotional moments in the poem. A father and son who have never really known each other are finally together.

At last Telemachus asks what ship brought him home. Odysseus explains that the Phaeacians, great sailors who give everyone escorts, brought him while he slept and gave him gifts of bronze, gold, and clothing, now hidden in a cave. Athena told him to come here so they could plan how to kill their enemies. He asks Telemachus for the number and names of the suitors, so he can decide whether the two of them can fight them alone, or whether they need help.

Telemachus says he has always heard of his father's fame in war and in council, but this task is too great. Two men cannot fight so many. There are not ten or twenty suitors, but many more. Fifty-two young men from Dulichium, with six servants. Twenty-four from Same. Twenty from Zacynthus. Twelve from Ithaca itself. They also have the herald Medon, the singer, and two carvers. So there are more than one hundred men. Telemachus asks if his father can think of someone to help them.

Odysseus asks whether Athena and her father Zeus will be enough as helpers. Telemachus admits that these two are excellent allies, since they rule over gods and men from high in the clouds. Odysseus says they will not stay away from the fight.

Then Odysseus gives his instructions. Telemachus must go home early tomorrow and mix with the suitors as before. Later, Eumaeus will bring Odysseus to the city, disguised again as a miserable old beggar. If the suitors insult or hurt him, even if they drag him out by the feet or throw things at him, Telemachus must control his heart and do nothing, except politely ask them to stop. They will not listen, because their day of punishment is near.

Odysseus also gives a secret plan. When Athena gives him the idea, he will nod to Telemachus. Then Telemachus must take all the weapons and armour from the great hall and lock them in the storeroom. If the suitors ask why, he should say he is moving them away from the smoke, which has made them dirty since Odysseus left. He should also say he fears that the suitors might quarrel when drunk and hurt each other, because seeing weapons tempts men to use them. But he should leave two swords, two spears, and two leather shields for father and son, ready to grab.

Finally, Odysseus tells Telemachus that if he is truly his son, he must tell no one that Odysseus is home. Not Laertes, not Eumaeus, not any servant, not even Penelope. They will test the women servants and some of the men, to see who is loyal and who is not.

Telemachus promises he can keep a secret. He says his father will learn what kind of son he is. But he respectfully disagrees with one part of the plan. Going around all the farms to test the men would take too long, and meanwhile the suitors would keep wasting the property. He suggests testing only the women in the house for now, and leaving the men for later. Here Telemachus speaks to his father almost as an equal, which shows how much he has grown.

Meanwhile, the ship that brought Telemachus home from Pylos reaches the town. The crew take the gifts to the house of Clytius and send a herald to tell Penelope that her son is safe in the country. The herald and Eumaeus arrive at the palace at the same time with the same news. The herald announces loudly in front of the maids that her son has returned. Eumaeus goes close to Penelope and quietly gives her the private message. Then he goes back to his pigs.

The suitors are surprised and angry. They hold a meeting outside the palace. Eurymachus says Telemachus's journey is a serious problem, and they must send a message to call their friends back from the ambush. But Amphinomus, one of the suitors, sees that the ambush ship is already coming into the harbour. He laughs. Some god must have warned them, or they saw Telemachus's ship pass and could not catch it.

The ambushers come back and meet in the assembly place, without letting anyone else sit with them. Antinous speaks. He says the gods have saved Telemachus. They kept lookouts on the cliffs all day, and waited on their ship all night, but some god brought the young man home. Now they must kill him quickly, before he calls an assembly and tells the people how they plotted to murder him. The people will be angry and might drive the suitors out of the country. They should catch him on his farm or on the road, kill him, share his property, and give the house to Penelope and her new husband. If the others do not like this plan, then they should stop eating his wealth, go home, and make offers for Penelope properly from their own houses. She can marry whoever gives the most.

Everyone is silent. Then Amphinomus speaks. He is the leader of the suitors from Dulichium. Penelope likes his conversation better than the others', because he has a good heart. He says he does not want to kill Telemachus. It is a terrible thing to kill a man of royal blood. They should first ask the gods. If Zeus's oracles approve, he will help kill him. If not, they should stop. His words please the others, and they go back to the palace. Amphinomus seems decent, but he still stays among the suitors, and this choice will matter later.

Penelope decides to come down and face the suitors. The herald Medon has told her about the plot to kill her son. She stands by a pillar with her maids, holding a veil in front of her face, and scolds Antinous. She calls him a wicked schemer. People say he is the best speaker of his age, but he is not. She asks why he plots to kill Telemachus, ignoring the suppliants whom Zeus protects. She reminds him that his own father, Eupeithes, once ran to this house in fear. He had joined pirates and attacked a people who were at peace with Ithaca, and the angry people wanted to kill him and take his property. Odysseus protected him. And now Antinous eats Odysseus's property, courts his wife, and tries to kill his son. She tells him to stop.

Eurymachus answers smoothly. He tells Penelope not to worry. While he lives, no man will touch Telemachus, or his own spear will be red with that man's blood. He says Odysseus often held him on his knee when he was a child and gave him meat and wine, so Telemachus is his dearest friend. Of course, if death comes from the gods, no one can stop it. The poem tells us plainly that he says this only to calm her, while secretly he is planning to kill Telemachus. Penelope goes upstairs and weeps for her husband until Athena brings her sleep.

In the evening Eumaeus returns to the hut. Odysseus and Telemachus have killed a young pig and are preparing supper. Before Eumaeus arrives, Athena touches Odysseus with her wand again and makes him an old beggar in rags, so that the swineherd will not recognize him and run to tell Penelope.

Telemachus asks Eumaeus for news. Have the suitors come back, or are they still waiting to ambush him? Eumaeus says he did not ask. He only delivered his message and hurried back. But he tells what he saw. A messenger from Telemachus's crew reached Penelope first. And from the hill of Hermes above the town, he saw a ship coming into the harbour full of men with shields and spears. He thinks it was the suitors, but he is not sure. Telemachus smiles at his father, but Eumaeus does not see it. Then they eat supper together and go to sleep. Father and son now share a secret, and the plan for revenge has begun.

## Book 17: The Beggar Comes Home, and the Old Dog Argos

At dawn Telemachus takes his spear and gets ready to go to the city. He tells Eumaeus, the loyal pig-keeper, that he must show himself to his mother, because she will not stop crying until she sees him. He also tells Eumaeus to bring the stranger to town to beg. He says, a little roughly, that he has enough troubles and cannot carry other people's burdens. He is playing his part, so that Eumaeus will not suspect anything. Odysseus agrees. He says a beggar does better in town, and he does not want to stay on a farm under a master. He only asks to warm himself by the fire first, because his clothes are thin and the morning is cold.

Telemachus walks to the palace, thinking about revenge. The first person to see him is the old nurse Eurycleia, who is putting fleeces on the chairs. She runs to him crying, and the other maids kiss his head and shoulders. Penelope comes down, as beautiful as Artemis or Aphrodite. She throws her arms around her son and kisses his eyes. She calls him the light of her eyes and says she thought she would never see him again. She gently scolds him for leaving without telling her, and asks what he learned.

Telemachus asks her not to upset him, since he has just escaped death. He tells her to wash, change her clothes, go upstairs with her maids, and promise sacrifices to all the gods if Zeus will grant them revenge on the suitors. He must go to the assembly place to meet the guest he brought from Pylos. Penelope does as he says.

Telemachus goes out with his spear and his two dogs. Athena makes him look so fine that everyone admires him. The suitors crowd around him with kind words, but with evil in their hearts. He avoids them and sits with his father's old friends, Mentor, Antiphus, and Halitherses. Then Piraeus arrives with Theoclymenus, the prophet. Piraeus asks if he should send over the gifts from Menelaus. Telemachus tells him to keep them for now. If the suitors kill him, he would rather Piraeus have the gifts than the suitors. If he kills them, Piraeus can bring them back.

Telemachus takes Theoclymenus home. They bathe, dress, and eat, while Penelope sits nearby, spinning wool. She says she will go up to her bed, which she has wet with tears since Odysseus left for Troy, but first she wants to know if he heard any news of his father.

Telemachus tells her. Nestor welcomed him like a son, but knew nothing about Odysseus, so he sent him to Menelaus. There he saw Helen, the cause of so much suffering. Menelaus was angry about the suitors and compared them to a deer who leaves her babies in a lion's den. He said that if Odysseus came back, the suitors would die. Then Menelaus told him what the old man of the sea had said: Odysseus was alive, on an island, held prisoner by the nymph Calypso, weeping and without a ship. Penelope is deeply moved.

Then Theoclymenus speaks. He tells Penelope that Telemachus does not fully understand. He swears by Zeus, by the guest table, and by the hearth of Odysseus that Odysseus is already in Ithaca. He is either moving about or staying in one place, learning about the suitors' crimes and preparing their punishment. He saw an omen on the ship that meant this. Penelope says that if this comes true, she will reward him richly.

Meanwhile, the suitors are throwing discs and spears in front of the palace, as rude as ever. When it is time for dinner, the herald Medon calls them inside, and they kill sheep, goats, pigs, and a cow for their feast.

At the farm, Eumaeus prepares to take the beggar to town. He says he would prefer to keep him on the farm as a worker, but he must obey his young master. Odysseus asks for a walking stick, since the road is rough. He puts his torn bag over his shoulder, takes the stick, and follows Eumaeus. He looks like a broken old tramp.

Near the city they come to a beautiful spring surrounded by poplar trees, with an altar to the nymphs where travellers make offerings. There they meet Melanthius, the goatherd, son of Dolius. He is driving the best goats to the palace for the suitors. Melanthius is a servant of Odysseus's house, but he has joined the suitors' side. He insults them both with ugly words. He says that bad men always find each other, like birds of a feather. He calls the beggar a disgusting creature who would make anyone sick at dinner. A man like this will never do honest work, he says. He only wants to fill his greedy belly with scraps. If he goes to the palace, the suitors will break his head with stools. Then, as he passes, Melanthius kicks Odysseus on the hip.

Odysseus does not move. For a moment he thinks about killing Melanthius with his stick, or throwing him down and smashing his head. But he controls himself and endures it. Eumaeus, however, scolds Melanthius and prays to the nymphs that Odysseus will come home and end the insults of men like him. Melanthius answers that one day he will sell Eumaeus as a slave abroad. He says he wishes Apollo or the suitors would kill Telemachus today, just as surely as Odysseus will never return. Then he hurries ahead to the palace and sits down with the suitors, near Eurymachus, who likes him best.

Odysseus and Eumaeus reach the palace. They hear music, because Phemius is starting to sing. Odysseus takes Eumaeus's hand and admires the house. It is fine, with building after building, a strong wall, and well-made double doors. It would be hard to take it by force. He smells roasting meat and hears the lyre. Eumaeus asks whether the stranger wants to go in first, or wait. He warns him not to wait long outside, or someone might throw something at him. Odysseus tells Eumaeus to go first. He is used to being hit. He has suffered so much in war and at sea that he can take more. But the hungry belly cannot be hidden, he says. It is the reason men sail ships and make war.

While they talk, an old dog lying nearby lifts his head and pricks up his ears. This is Argos, the dog Odysseus raised before he left for Troy, though he never had the chance to hunt with him. In the past, young men took Argos hunting for wild goats, deer, and hares. Now, with his master gone, he lies neglected on a pile of animal dung in front of the stable doors, covered with fleas. When Argos sees Odysseus, he recognizes him at once, after twenty years. He lowers his ears and wags his tail. But he is too weak to come to his master.

Odysseus sees the dog across the yard. He secretly wipes away a tear so that Eumaeus will not notice. He cannot greet the dog without giving away his secret. He asks Eumaeus about this fine dog. Is he a good hunter, or just a pet kept for show? Eumaeus says the dog belonged to his master who died far away. When Odysseus left, no wild animal could escape him in the forest. But now he is neglected. The women do not care for him. Servants do not work well when the master is gone, Eumaeus says, because Zeus takes away half of a man's goodness on the day he becomes a slave.

Eumaeus goes inside. And Argos, having seen his master again after twenty years, dies. This is one of the most famous and touching scenes in the poem. The old dog waited, faithful to the end, and he is the only one who recognizes Odysseus at once, without any sign.

Inside, Telemachus sees Eumaeus and invites him to sit at his table. Soon Odysseus comes in, looking like a miserable old beggar, leaning on his stick in his rags. He sits on the wooden doorstep, inside the hall, against a doorpost. Telemachus takes a whole loaf of bread and as much meat as he can hold in both hands and tells Eumaeus to give it to the stranger. He adds that the stranger should go around and beg from each suitor, because a beggar should not be shy. Odysseus thanks him and prays that Zeus will give Telemachus all he desires. He eats while the singer sings.

Then Athena comes to Odysseus and tells him to go around the hall begging bread from each suitor, to learn which men are decent and which are wicked. But the poem says clearly that she will not save any of them in the end. Odysseus goes around from left to right, holding out his hand like a real beggar. Some suitors pity him and give him food. They ask each other who he is. Melanthius says that the swineherd brought him, but he does not know where he comes from.

Antinous then insults Eumaeus. He asks why he brought another beggar to bother them, as if there were not enough already wasting his master's food. Eumaeus answers bravely. Antinous has good birth, he says, but bad words. No one invites a stranger from far away unless he is useful, like a prophet, a doctor, a carpenter, or a singer. No one invites a beggar. He says Antinous is always harsher than the other suitors to Odysseus's servants, but he does not care, as long as Penelope and Telemachus are alive.

Telemachus tells Eumaeus to be quiet. Antinous has the bitterest tongue and makes the others worse. Then Telemachus speaks to Antinous with sharp irony. He says Antinous protects his property as carefully as a father protects his son's. He tells Antinous to give the beggar something himself, since it is Telemachus's food anyway. But he knows Antinous would rather eat than give.

Antinous answers that if every suitor gave as much as he will give, the beggar would stay away for three months. He grabs the footstool under his feet as if to throw it. Meanwhile the other suitors fill the beggar's bag with bread and meat.

Before going back to his place, Odysseus goes to Antinous and asks for something. He says Antinous looks like the leader, so he should give the most, and the beggar will praise his generosity everywhere. He says he was once a rich man with a fine house and many servants, and he gave freely to beggars like himself. Then he tells a shortened version of his Egyptian story. His men attacked Egypt against his orders. The Egyptians killed many and enslaved others. He was handed over to a man going to Cyprus, and from there he came here in misery.

Antinous is disgusted. He asks what god sent this plague to ruin their dinner. He tells the beggar to get away, or he will show him a new Egypt and Cyprus. He says the other suitors gave freely only because it is easy to be generous with someone else's property. Odysseus answers, as he moves away, that Antinous has good looks but no good sense. In his own house he would not give a beggar even a grain of salt. Even now, sitting at another man's table full of food, he cannot give a piece of bread.

Antinous is furious. He says the beggar will pay for these words. He throws the footstool and hits Odysseus on the right shoulder. Odysseus stands firm like a rock. He does not even stagger. He silently shakes his head and thinks about revenge. Then he goes back to the doorstep and speaks to all the suitors. A man does not mind being hit when he fights for his property or his animals. But Antinous struck him because of his hungry belly, which brings so much trouble. If the poor have gods and Furies who protect them, he prays that Antinous will die before his wedding.

Antinous threatens to have him dragged out by his hands and feet and skinned alive. But the other suitors are angry with Antinous. One young man warns him that it was wrong to hit a poor beggar. What if he is a god? The gods sometimes travel in disguise as strangers from far away, to see who does right and who does wrong. Antinous ignores them. Telemachus is furious about the blow to his father. He does not cry, but he silently shakes his head and plans revenge.

Penelope hears in her room that the beggar was struck. She says she wishes Apollo would strike Antinous. Her housekeeper Eurynome says that if their prayers came true, not one of the suitors would live to see the morning. Penelope says she hates all the suitors, but she hates Antinous like death itself. A poor man came begging, everyone else gave him something, and Antinous hit him.

Then Penelope calls Eumaeus. She wants to speak to the stranger, because he seems to have travelled a lot and might have news of her husband. Eumaeus says that if the suitors would be quiet, she would be enchanted by the man's stories. He stayed three nights in his hut, and Eumaeus listened to him like a famous singer. The man says his family were old friends of Odysseus, that he comes from Crete, and that he heard Odysseus is alive and near, in the land of the Thesprotians, bringing home great wealth.

Penelope tells him to bring the stranger. She complains again about the suitors, who keep their own food and wine safe at home while they eat and drink hers. If Odysseus came back, he and his son would soon take revenge. At that moment Telemachus sneezes very loudly, and the whole house echoes. Penelope laughs. In this world, a sneeze at such a moment is a good omen. She says it means that all the suitors will die, and not one will escape. She promises that if the stranger tells the truth, she will give him a good shirt and cloak.

Eumaeus brings the message to Odysseus. Odysseus says he will tell Penelope only the truth. He knows her husband well and suffered with him. But he is afraid to cross the hall full of violent suitors. Just now a man hit him, and no one, not even Telemachus, protected him. He asks Penelope to wait until sunset and give him a seat near the fire, since his clothes are thin. Then she can ask about her husband.

Eumaeus returns. Penelope asks why he has not brought the stranger. Is he afraid, or ashamed? Eumaeus says the man is sensible. He wants to avoid the suitors, and it will be better for Penelope to talk with him alone in the evening. Penelope agrees. The man is no fool, she says, because no men in the world are as wicked as these suitors.

Eumaeus then goes to Telemachus and whispers that he must return to the pigs. He warns him to be careful, because many people wish him harm. Telemachus tells him to go home after dinner and come back in the morning with animals for the sacrifice. He says to leave everything else to the gods and to him. Eumaeus eats and goes back to his farm. The suitors spend the evening singing and dancing. They have no idea that the old beggar on the doorstep is the master of the house.

## Book 18: The Beggar's Fight and Penelope's Appearance

A well-known beggar of Ithaca now arrives at the palace. He is a big, heavy man who eats and drinks without limit, but he has no real strength. His mother named him Arnaeus, but the young men call him Irus, because he runs errands for anyone, like Iris, the messenger goddess. As soon as he sees Odysseus, still disguised as an old beggar, Irus tries to drive him away from the doorway. He says the suitors are signalling to him to throw the old man out, and if the stranger does not leave, they will fight.

Odysseus frowns. He says he is doing Irus no harm. There is room for both of them on this doorstep, and he does not envy what people give Irus. But he warns him not to talk too much about fighting. Old as he is, he could cover Irus's mouth and chest with blood, and then Irus would not come back to the house of Odysseus. Irus becomes angry. He calls Odysseus a filthy glutton who chatters like an old woman and threatens to knock his teeth out.

Antinous sees the quarrel and laughs. He says this is the best entertainment the gods have ever sent to this house. The suitors gather around the two beggars. Antinous offers a prize. There are goat stomachs stuffed with blood and fat cooking on the fire. The winner can choose one, will be allowed to eat at their table, and no other beggar will be allowed in. Everyone agrees.

Odysseus, to hide his real strength, says that an old man worn out by suffering cannot beat a young one, but his hungry belly forces him to fight. He asks the suitors to swear that none of them will help Irus by secretly hitting him. They swear. Telemachus adds that if anyone hits the stranger, he will have to fight more than one man. He is the host, and Antinous and Eurymachus, sensible men, agree with him.

Odysseus ties up his rags around his waist. Suddenly the suitors see his powerful thighs, broad chest and shoulders, and strong arms. Athena makes his body even stronger. The suitors are amazed. One says that Irus will soon have nothing left. Irus begins to shake with fear. The servants have to push him forward. Antinous scolds him. He says Irus should never have been born if he is afraid of this old wreck. If the stranger wins, Antinous will send Irus to King Echetus on the mainland, a cruel king who will cut off his nose and ears and feed his body to the dogs.

The two men raise their fists. Odysseus thinks about whether to hit Irus so hard that he dies, or only knock him down. He chooses the lighter blow, so that the suitors will not suspect who he is. Irus hits Odysseus on the right shoulder. Then Odysseus hits Irus on the neck under the ear and breaks the bones. Blood pours from his mouth. He falls in the dust, groaning and kicking. The suitors throw up their hands and almost die laughing. Odysseus drags Irus by the foot out to the gate, sits him against the wall, and puts a stick in his hand. He tells him to sit there and keep the dogs and pigs away, and not to try to be king of the beggars again.

The suitors laugh and congratulate Odysseus. They pray that the gods give him what he wants for getting rid of the greedy tramp. Odysseus takes their words as a good omen. Antinous gives him the big goat stomach. Amphinomus, the kindest of the suitors, gives him two loaves of bread and drinks to his health with a golden cup. He wishes him better luck in the future.

Odysseus answers Amphinomus seriously. He praises his father Nisus of Dulichium. Then he gives him a warning, almost like a lesson in wisdom. Of all creatures on earth, man is the weakest and most foolish. While the gods give him health and strength, he thinks nothing bad can happen to him. When the gods send sorrow, he must bear it, because the gods change men's fortunes day by day. The beggar says he himself was once rich and did wrong, trusting in his strength and in his father and brothers. So a man should always respect the gods and accept what they give without pride. Then he points to the suitors' crimes. They are wasting the property and insulting the wife of a man who will come back soon. He advises Amphinomus to go home before that man arrives, because when he returns, there will be blood.

Odysseus pours a drink offering and gives the cup back. Amphinomus walks away with his head down, troubled, feeling that something bad is coming. But he does not leave. The poem says that Athena has already decided he will die by Telemachus's spear. He returns to his seat. This moment is sad. Odysseus gives a good man a fair chance to escape, but Amphinomus does not take it.

Now Athena puts an idea into Penelope's mind. She wants Penelope to show herself to the suitors, to make them want her even more, and to win greater honour from her son and husband. Penelope laughs strangely, without real joy. She tells her housekeeper Eurynome that she has decided to show herself to the suitors, even though she hates them. She also wants to warn her son not to spend so much time with them, because they speak kindly but plan evil.

Eurynome says this is wise. She tells Penelope to wash and put oil on her face first, and not to go down with cheeks wet from tears. She reminds her that Telemachus is now a grown man with a beard, as she always prayed he would be. Penelope refuses to make herself beautiful. She says the gods took away her beauty on the day her husband sailed. But she asks for two maids to go with her, because it would not be proper to go among the men alone.

While the maids are coming, Athena makes Penelope fall into a sweet sleep on her couch. Then the goddess makes her more beautiful. She washes her face with the divine beauty that Aphrodite wears when she dances with the Graces. She makes her taller and more stately, and her skin whiter than polished ivory. The maids come in talking and wake her. Penelope says she had a wonderful sleep, despite her sorrow. She wishes Artemis would give her such a gentle death right now, so she would no longer waste away mourning her husband.

She goes down with two maids and stands by a pillar, holding a veil in front of her face. When the suitors see her, their knees go weak with desire, and each one prays to have her as his wife.

Penelope speaks first to Telemachus. She scolds him. When he was younger, he had better judgement. Now he is grown, and he looks like the son of a great man, but he has allowed a stranger to be treated badly in his house. What if the guest had been seriously hurt? It would bring shame on the family. Telemachus answers that he understands and knows right from wrong now. But it is hard to always do the right thing, because the wicked men around him confuse him, and he has no one to help him. Still, he says, the fight between Irus and the stranger did not end as the suitors hoped, because the stranger won. He wishes Zeus, Athena, and Apollo would break the necks of all the suitors, and leave them hanging their heads like Irus at the gate, who looks like a drunk man and cannot even stand.

Eurymachus then flatters Penelope. He says if all the Greeks could see her now, she would have even more suitors tomorrow, because she is the most beautiful and wisest woman in the world. Penelope answers that the gods took away her beauty when Odysseus left for Troy. If he came back, her life would be better. Then she tells them what her husband said when he left. He took her right wrist and told her that not all the Greeks would come back from Troy, because the Trojans were good fighters. He did not know if he would return. He asked her to take care of his parents. And when their son had grown a beard, she should marry whoever she wished and leave the house. Now, she says, this time has come. Soon she will have to accept a marriage she hates.

Then she adds a complaint. The suitors are not courting her in the proper way. Normally, men who want to marry a noble woman bring their own cattle and sheep to feast her family, and give her rich gifts. They do not eat someone else's property without paying.

Odysseus is delighted to hear this. He sees that she is cleverly getting gifts from the suitors with charming words, while her heart has other plans. This scene is much discussed, because Penelope's real intentions are not fully clear. Is she truly ready to marry, or is she tricking the suitors again? Odysseus admires her cleverness, which matches his own.

Antinous says she may accept gifts from anyone. It is not good to refuse gifts. But they will not leave until she marries the best of them. Each suitor sends a servant home for a gift. Antinous gives a large, beautifully embroidered robe with twelve golden pins. Eurymachus gives a golden necklace with amber beads that shine like the sun. Eurydamas gives earrings with three bright jewels. Pisander gives a beautiful necklace. All the others give gifts too. Penelope goes back upstairs, and her maids carry the gifts behind her.

The suitors dance and sing until evening. When it gets dark, they set up three fire pans, called braziers, with dry wood to give light. The maids take turns holding torches. Odysseus tells the maids to go to the queen and keep her company, spinning wool. He will hold the light for the suitors himself. He can stay awake until morning. He is used to hardship.

The maids look at each other and laugh. One of them, Melantho, insults him. She is the daughter of Dolius and the sister of Melanthius the goatherd. Penelope raised her like her own daughter and gave her toys when she was little. But Melantho has no pity for her mistress. She is having a love affair with Eurymachus. She tells the beggar he has lost his mind. He should go sleep in the blacksmith's shop or some public place instead of talking here in front of his betters. Is he drunk? Has his victory over Irus made him crazy? A better man might soon beat him and throw him out bleeding.

Odysseus looks at her angrily. He calls her a shameless dog and says he will tell Telemachus what she said, and Telemachus will have her cut to pieces. The maids are terrified and run into the house, shaking. Odysseus stands by the fire, holding up the light and watching the suitors, thinking about what is to come. The disloyal maids are now marked, and this will matter at the end.

Athena does not let the suitors stop insulting Odysseus, because she wants his anger to grow even stronger. Eurymachus mocks him, and the others laugh. He says that the light in the hall is not coming from the torches but from the beggar's bald head. Then he offers the beggar a job building stone walls and planting trees on a distant farm, with food and clothes all year. But he says the beggar would never accept, because he is lazy and would rather beg for food.

Odysseus answers with strong words. He challenges Eurymachus to a contest. In early summer, give them each a scythe, and see who can cut grass longer, from dawn to dark, without food. Or give them each a pair of strong oxen and a large field, and see who can plough a straighter line. Or if war broke out today, give him a shield, two spears, and a helmet, and Eurymachus would see him fighting in the front line and stop making jokes about his belly. Eurymachus is rude and cruel, he says, and thinks himself great only because he lives among small and worthless men. If Odysseus comes home, the doors of his house are wide, but Eurymachus will find them too narrow as he tries to escape.

Eurymachus is furious. He grabs a footstool to throw. Odysseus quickly sits down at the knees of Amphinomus for protection. The stool misses him and hits the wine-server on the right hand. The man falls on his back with a cry, and his wine jug crashes to the ground. The suitors make a great noise. They complain that the stranger brings nothing but trouble, and that arguing about a beggar spoils their feast.

Telemachus stands up. He asks the suitors if they are mad. They cannot even eat and drink properly. Some evil spirit must be in them. He says he does not want to drive anyone away, but they have eaten, and the sooner they go home to bed, the better. The suitors bite their lips, surprised by his boldness.

Again Amphinomus calms everyone. He says Telemachus is right and they should not answer angrily. No one should hurt the stranger or any servant of Odysseus. Let them pour the last drink offerings and go home. Telemachus should decide what to do with the stranger, since he came to his house. Everyone agrees. They pour offerings to the gods, drink, and go home to sleep. Odysseus has now seen clearly who is cruel and who is decent, and his anger is ready.

## Book 19: Penelope Meets the Beggar, and the Scar

After the suitors go home, Odysseus stays in the hall, thinking about how to kill them with Athena's help. He tells Telemachus that now is the time to remove the weapons. He repeats the excuse they planned: the weapons are dirty from smoke, and the suitors might hurt each other if they fight when drunk.

Telemachus calls Eurycleia, the old nurse, and tells her to lock the women servants in their rooms while he carries his father's armour to the storeroom to protect it from smoke. Eurycleia says she wishes he would take charge of the whole house. She asks who will carry the light for him, since he has sent the maids away. Telemachus says the stranger will do it. Anyone who eats his bread must work for it.

So father and son carry helmets, shields, and spears into the storeroom. Athena walks in front of them with a golden lamp, and a wonderful light fills the room. Telemachus is amazed. He says the walls and roof beams are shining as if on fire, and some god must be present. Odysseus tells him to be quiet and not ask questions. This is how the gods act. He sends Telemachus to bed, because he wants to speak with Penelope and the maids. Telemachus goes to his room.

Penelope comes down, as lovely as Artemis or Aphrodite, and sits by the fire in her special chair made of silver and ivory. The maids clear away the suitors' tables and food and put more wood on the fire pans. Then Melantho, the rude maid, insults Odysseus again. She asks if he plans to stay all night spying on the women, and tells him to go outside or she will drive him out with a burning stick.

Odysseus answers with a warning. He asks why she is angry with him. Is it because he is dirty and poor? He was once rich and generous, until Zeus took everything away. She should be careful, he says. She too may lose her favour, her mistress may become angry, or Odysseus may come home. And even if Odysseus is dead, he has left a son, Telemachus, who is now a man and notices what the maids do wrong. Penelope hears this and scolds Melantho. She says Melantho knew perfectly well that Penelope wanted to question the stranger about her husband, and she will pay for her rudeness.

Penelope asks her housekeeper Eurynome to bring a chair with a fleece for the stranger. Then she asks him who he is and where he comes from. This is the first conversation between husband and wife in twenty years, but only one of them knows it.

Odysseus begins with high praise. Penelope's fame reaches heaven, he says. She is like a good king who rules justly, so that the land produces wheat and fruit, the sheep give lambs, the sea is full of fish, and the people live well. But he asks her not to ask about his family, because it would bring back painful memories and make him cry. People might think he was drunk.

Penelope answers that the gods took away her beauty when her husband went to Troy. The leading men of the islands are courting her against her will and wasting her property. She cannot even care properly for strangers and suppliants, because she is always grieving for Odysseus. They want her to marry at once, so she has had to invent tricks. Then she tells, in her own words, the story of the weaving. She told the suitors she must first finish a burial cloth for Laertes. She wove by day and undid it by night, for three years. In the fourth year, her disloyal maids told the suitors, who caught her, and she had to finish it. Now she has no more tricks. Her parents are pressing her to marry. Her son is angry about the waste, and he is now old enough to run the estate. Still, she insists that the stranger tell her who he is. He was not born from an oak tree or a rock, she says, so he must have parents.

So Odysseus tells another false story. He says he comes from Crete, a large island with ninety cities and many peoples. Its great city is Cnossus, where King Minos once ruled. He claims to be a grandson of Minos and the younger brother of Idomeneus, the Cretan leader at Troy. He gives himself the name Aethon. He says that when Odysseus was sailing to Troy, the wind blew him off course to Crete. He came looking for Idomeneus, who had already left, so Aethon welcomed him himself. He gave Odysseus and his men food, wine, and cattle, and they stayed twelve days because of a strong north wind. On the thirteenth day they left.

Odysseus tells many lies that sound like the truth, and Penelope weeps as she listens. The poem compares her tears to snow melting on the mountains in spring until the rivers overflow. She is crying for her husband, who is sitting right next to her. Odysseus pities her deeply, but he keeps his eyes as hard as horn or iron. He does not let them move or show a single tear. This shows his tremendous self-control.

When Penelope has cried enough, she decides to test him. If he really hosted Odysseus, he should describe his clothes and his companions. Odysseus says it was twenty years ago, but he remembers. Odysseus wore a thick purple cloak, fastened with a golden brooch. On the brooch was a picture of a dog holding a young deer in its paws, with the deer struggling to escape. Everyone admired how real it looked. His shirt was so soft and shiny that it was like the thin skin of an onion, and women admired it. The stranger says he does not know whether Odysseus brought these clothes from home or received them as gifts. He also describes Odysseus's herald, Eurybates, a man with round shoulders, dark skin, and curly hair, whom Odysseus valued highly because they thought alike.

Penelope recognizes these details and cries again. She says she herself gave Odysseus those clothes and that brooch. From now on, the stranger will be honoured in her house. But she is sure she will never welcome her husband home. He sailed to that evil city, Troy, whose name she cannot even say.

Odysseus tells her not to harm her beauty with so much grief, though it is natural to mourn a husband. Then he gives her news, mixing truth and invention. He has heard that Odysseus is alive and close by, in the land of the Thesprotians, collecting treasure. He says Odysseus's ship and crew were lost after leaving the island of the Sun, because his men killed the Sun's cattle, and Zeus and the Sun punished them. Odysseus himself held on to the keel and reached the Phaeacians, who treated him like a god and wanted to send him home. Here he is telling the true story. He says Odysseus would already be home if he had not decided to collect more wealth, because there is no man as clever as he is. Then he repeats the story about King Pheidon, the treasure, and the oak of Dodona. Finally he swears an oath that Odysseus will return this very year, between the end of this month and the start of the next.

Penelope says she hopes he is right, but she does not believe it. Odysseus will not come home. Then she orders the maids to wash the stranger's feet and make him a soft bed with blankets. In the morning they should bathe him so that he can eat with Telemachus. Anyone who insults him will be sorry. She says a person who is cruel is hated in life and mocked in death, while a person who is kind is praised everywhere. She wants the stranger to see that she is truly wise and good.

Odysseus refuses the soft bed. He says he has not used blankets since he left Crete, and he will sleep rough as usual. He also does not want the young maids, who have insulted him, to wash his feet. But if there is an old, respectable woman who has suffered as much as he has, she may do it.

Penelope says no guest has ever spoken so sensibly. She has just such a woman: the old nurse who held Odysseus when he was born and raised him. She calls Eurycleia to wash the feet of a man the same age as her master. She adds, sadly, that Odysseus's hands and feet are probably like this man's now, because trouble makes people old quickly.

Eurycleia covers her face and cries. She speaks to her absent master as if he were present. She says no one was more faithful to the gods than Odysseus, yet Zeus hates him and keeps him from home. She imagines that women in some foreign palace are mocking him just as the shameless maids here mock this stranger. She will gladly wash the stranger's feet, for Penelope's sake and for his own, because she feels pity for him. Then she says that of all the strangers who have come here, no one has ever looked so much like Odysseus in body, voice, and feet. Odysseus quickly answers that people who have seen them both have always said they look very alike.

Eurycleia prepares a bowl of warm water. Odysseus sits by the fire, but he suddenly turns his face away from the light. He has remembered that he has a scar on his leg, and the nurse may recognize it. And that is exactly what happens.

At this point the poem pauses to tell the story of the scar, in a long flashback. When Odysseus was a baby, his grandfather Autolycus, his mother's father, came to Ithaca. Autolycus was the greatest thief and trickster in the world, a gift from the god Hermes. Eurycleia put the baby on his knees and asked him to choose a name. Autolycus said that he had been angry with many people in his life, so the child should be named Odysseus, which sounds like the Greek word for anger or hatred. He promised that when the boy grew up and visited him on Mount Parnassus, he would give him great gifts.

When Odysseus was a young man, he went to Parnassus. His grandparents welcomed him with a great feast. The next morning he went hunting with his uncles and their dogs. In a thick wood they found a huge wild boar. It burst out of its hiding place with its bristles raised and fire in its eyes. Odysseus was the first to attack with his spear. But the boar was faster. It charged him and tore a deep wound above his knee with its tusk. Odysseus speared it through the shoulder and killed it. His uncles bandaged him and sang a magic chant to stop the bleeding. They healed him, gave him fine gifts, and sent him home. His parents asked how he got the scar, and he told them the story. This flashback shows that the scar is part of who Odysseus is, from his name to his first adventure as a young man.

Now, back in the present, Eurycleia takes his leg in her hands and feels the scar. She recognizes it at once. She drops his foot. His leg falls into the bowl, which rings, tips over, and spills all the water. Her eyes fill with tears of joy and sorrow, and her voice is stuck in her throat. She touches his beard and says that he must be Odysseus, her dear child. She did not know him until she touched him.

She turns toward Penelope to tell her. But Athena has turned Penelope's attention away, so she notices nothing. Odysseus seizes Eurycleia by the throat with his right hand and pulls her close with his left. He tells her quietly that she will ruin him. She nursed him at her own breast, and now he has come home after twenty years. Since a god has let her recognize him, she must be silent. If she tells anyone, then when he kills the suitors, he will not spare her, even though she is his nurse, when he punishes the disloyal women.

Eurycleia answers that he knows she is as hard as stone or iron and will keep the secret. She offers to tell him later which women in the house have been disloyal and which are innocent. Odysseus says he does not need her help to judge them. He tells her to be quiet and leave everything to the gods. Eurycleia brings new water, finishes washing him, and rubs him with oil. Odysseus moves closer to the fire and hides the scar under his rags.

Then Penelope speaks to him again. She says she cannot sleep. By day she cries while working, and by night her mind is tortured. She compares herself to the nightingale, who sings sadly in spring for the child she killed by mistake. Her mind keeps turning back and forth. Should she stay with her son and protect the house, out of respect for her husband and public opinion? Or is it time to marry the best of the suitors, the one who gives her the most gifts? When her son was young, he would not let her leave. Now that he is a man, he wants her to go, because he is angry about the suitors wasting his property.

Then she asks the stranger to explain a dream. She keeps twenty geese at home, and she loves to watch them eat. In her dream, a great eagle flew down from the mountain and broke all their necks, killing them. Then it flew away. She wept in her dream, and her maids came around her. Then the eagle came back, sat on a roof beam, and spoke with a human voice. It told her not to cry. This was not a dream, but a true vision. The geese are the suitors, and the eagle is her husband, come home to destroy them. Then she woke up and saw her geese eating as usual.

Odysseus says the dream can only mean one thing. Odysseus himself, in the dream, has already explained it. The suitors will die, and not one will escape.

Penelope answers that dreams are strange and do not always come true. There are two gates for dreams. False dreams come through a gate of ivory. True dreams come through a gate of horn. She does not think her dream came through the gate of horn, though she and her son would be very glad if it did.

Then she announces her decision. Tomorrow will be the terrible day when she leaves the house of Odysseus. She will hold a contest. Odysseus used to set up twelve axes in a row in the hall, like the supports used when building a ship. Then he would stand far back and shoot an arrow through all twelve. She will make the suitors try the same thing. Whoever can most easily string Odysseus's bow and shoot an arrow through the twelve axes, she will follow him and leave this rich and beautiful house, though she will remember it in her dreams.

Odysseus tells her not to delay the contest. Odysseus will be home before any of them can string the bow and shoot through the iron. Penelope says she would happily sit and talk with him all night, but people need sleep. She goes upstairs with her maids and weeps for her husband until Athena brings her sleep. This long night scene is full of tension, hidden feelings, and near-discovery. Husband and wife understand each other in many ways, and now the contest of the bow will decide everything.

## Book 20: Signs Before the Storm

That night Odysseus sleeps in the porch on an untanned cowhide, with sheepskins from the animals the suitors have eaten. The housekeeper Eurynome throws a cloak over him. But he lies awake, thinking about how to kill the suitors. Then he sees some of the maids leaving the house, laughing and giggling, on their way to sleep with the suitors. He is filled with anger. He wonders whether to jump up and kill them all at once, or to let them sleep with the suitors one last time.

His heart growls inside him, like a mother dog growling to protect her puppies from a stranger. But he hits his chest and speaks to his own heart. He tells it to be patient. It endured worse on the day the Cyclops ate his friends. Then, too, he kept quiet until his cleverness got him out of the cave. So he forces himself to wait. Still, he turns from side to side, like a man turning a sausage of blood and fat over a hot fire, thinking about how one man can kill so many.

Athena comes down in the form of a woman and stands over his head. She asks why he is still awake. This is his house, his wife is inside, and his son is a young man any father would be proud of. Odysseus explains his two worries. First, how can he, one man, kill so many suitors? Second, even if he succeeds with the help of Zeus and Athena, where can he escape from the families of the dead men, who will want revenge? This second question looks ahead to the end of the poem.

Athena scolds him gently. People trust weaker friends, even mortal ones, and she is a goddess who has always protected him. Even if fifty armies surrounded them, he would drive away their cattle and sheep. She tells him to sleep, because staying awake all night is bad, and his troubles will soon end. She pours sleep over his eyes and goes back to Olympus.

While Odysseus sleeps, Penelope wakes and cries. She prays to Artemis to kill her with an arrow, or to let a storm wind carry her away to the end of the world. She tells the story of the daughters of Pandareus. Their parents were killed by the gods, and the goddesses raised them and gave them beauty and skill. But when Aphrodite went to Zeus to arrange their marriages, storm winds carried them off to become servants of the Furies. Penelope wishes she could disappear like that, and go under the earth with the image of Odysseus in her mind, rather than marry a worse man. She says that people can bear sorrow in the day if they can sleep at night. But her pain follows her into her dreams. That very night she dreamed that a man lay beside her who looked like Odysseus as he was when he left for Troy, and she was happy, because she thought it was real.

As dawn comes, Odysseus hears her crying. For a moment he feels as if she already knows him and is standing beside him. He gets up, puts the cloak and fleeces on a seat, and carries the cowhide outside. He raises his hands and prays to Zeus. He asks for two signs: a word spoken by someone inside the house, and a sign from the sky outside.

Zeus answers at once. Thunder sounds from Olympus, out of a clear sky. At the same time, inside the house, a woman grinding grain speaks. Twelve women grind wheat and barley for the household. The others have finished their work and gone to rest, but this one is weaker and still working. When she hears the thunder, she stops and prays aloud. She says Zeus has thundered from a clear sky, so it must be a sign for someone. She asks that this be the last day the suitors eat in the house of Odysseus, because they have worn her out with grinding grain for them. Odysseus is glad. The thunder and the woman's words are the two signs he asked for. They tell him that his revenge will succeed.

The house wakes up. The maids light the fire. Telemachus dresses, takes his sword and spear, and asks Eurycleia whether she took good care of the stranger. He complains that his mother sometimes honours unimportant people and neglects better men. Eurycleia defends Penelope. She says the stranger drank as much as he liked and refused more bread. Penelope offered him a proper bed, but he wanted only a cowhide and sheepskins, and Eurycleia herself covered him with a cloak. Telemachus then goes to the assembly with his spear and his two dogs.

Eurycleia orders the maids to work hard. They must sweep, sprinkle water on the floor, put covers on the seats, clean the tables, wash the cups, and fetch water from the spring. The suitors will come early, because today is a feast day, a holy day of Apollo. Twenty maids go for water, and the others clean the house. The suitors' servants come and chop wood.

Then Eumaeus arrives with three of his best pigs. He kindly asks the stranger whether the suitors are treating him better now. Odysseus answers that he hopes the gods will punish them for their shameless violence in another man's house.

Melanthius the goatherd arrives with the best goats for the suitors' dinner. He insults Odysseus again. He asks why the beggar is still here bothering people, and says they will not be friends until they have had a fist fight. Odysseus does not answer. He only shakes his head silently and thinks about revenge.

Then a third herdsman comes. This is Philoetius, the cowherd, bringing a young cow and some goats across from the mainland on the ferry. He asks Eumaeus who the stranger is. He says the poor man looks like a king, but the gods send trouble even to kings. He goes to Odysseus and shakes his hand. He wishes him better luck in the future. Then he complains to Zeus. He says Zeus is the cruellest of gods, because he creates men and then shows them no mercy. When he saw the stranger, he started to sweat and his eyes filled with tears, because the man reminded him of Odysseus. Perhaps his master also wanders in rags like this, if he is still alive.

Philoetius explains that Odysseus made him cowherd when he was young, and the cattle have multiplied greatly under his care. But now he must keep bringing the best cows for strangers to eat, men who ignore Telemachus and do not fear the gods, and who want to divide Odysseus's property. He has often thought of running away with the cattle to another country, but that would be wrong while Telemachus lives. Staying is terrible, but he stays because he still hopes his master will come home and drive the suitors out.

Odysseus says the cowherd seems a good and sensible man. He swears by Zeus and by the hearth of Odysseus that Odysseus will return before Philoetius leaves this place, and that he will see the suitors killed. Philoetius says that if this happens, he will help Odysseus with all his strength. Eumaeus also prays for his master's return. So Odysseus now knows that he has two loyal men, Eumaeus and Philoetius.

Meanwhile the suitors are again planning to kill Telemachus. But an eagle flies past on their left, carrying a dove. This is a bad sign. Amphinomus says their plot will fail, and they should go and eat instead. They agree.

They go inside and sacrifice sheep, goats, pigs, and the cow. Eumaeus gives out the cups, Philoetius hands round the bread, and Melanthius pours the wine. Telemachus places Odysseus near the stone doorway, on a poor stool at a small table. He gives him a fair portion of meat and wine in a gold cup. He tells him to sit and drink among the great men. Then he warns the suitors. This is not a public tavern. It is the house of Odysseus, and now it belongs to Telemachus. They must keep their hands and their insults to themselves. The suitors bite their lips, surprised at his boldness. Antinous says they do not like his words but will accept them, because he seems serious. If Zeus had let them, they would have silenced him already. Telemachus ignores him. Meanwhile, in the town, the heralds are leading animals for a great sacrifice to Apollo in his sacred grove.

The servants give Odysseus the same portion as the others, as Telemachus ordered. But Athena keeps the suitors behaving badly, because she wants Odysseus's anger to grow even more. Among the suitors is a rude man named Ctesippus, from Same, who is very rich. He says, mockingly, that the stranger has received a fair share, and it is right to treat Telemachus's guests well. So he will give him a gift too, something to give to the bath woman or another servant. Then he grabs a cow's foot from a basket and throws it at Odysseus. Odysseus moves his head a little and the foot hits the wall. He smiles a grim, bitter smile.

Telemachus speaks fiercely to Ctesippus. He says it is lucky for him that he missed. If he had hit the stranger, Telemachus would have killed him with his spear, and his father would be holding a funeral instead of a wedding. He says he is no longer a child. He understands good and evil now. He has watched them kill his sheep and drink his wine, because one man cannot fight many. But they must not use violence. If they want to kill him, they can. He would rather die than keep seeing such shameful things: guests insulted and women servants dragged around the house.

Everyone is silent. Then a suitor named Agelaus speaks calmly. He says Telemachus is right, and they should stop mistreating the stranger and the servants. But he gives Telemachus some advice. While there was hope that Odysseus would return, it was reasonable to wait. But it is now clear that Odysseus will never come home. So Telemachus should tell his mother to marry the best man, the one who offers the most. Then Telemachus can keep his property and eat and drink in peace. Telemachus answers that he does not stand in his mother's way. He urges her to marry whoever she wants, and he will give her many gifts. But he cannot force her out of the house against her will. The gods forbid it.

Now Athena does something terrible and strange. She makes the suitors laugh uncontrollably, and she confuses their minds. They laugh with faces that are not their own. Their meat looks covered in blood. Their eyes fill with tears, and their hearts feel heavy with fear of what is coming. The prophet Theoclymenus sees this and cries out. He asks what is wrong with them. Darkness covers them from head to foot. Their cheeks are wet with tears. He hears voices crying. The walls and roof beams drip with blood. The porch and courtyard are full of ghosts going down into the darkness of the underworld. The sun has disappeared from the sky, and a terrible mist covers everything.

The suitors laugh at him. Eurymachus says the newcomer has lost his mind and tells the servants to throw him out into the street, since he finds it so dark inside. Theoclymenus answers that he needs no one to guide him. He has eyes, ears, feet, and a good mind. He will leave, because he sees disaster coming, and not one of the men who insult others and plot evil in the house of Odysseus will escape. He leaves and goes to the house of Piraeus.

The suitors look at each other and laugh at Telemachus's guests. One says Telemachus has bad luck with guests. First this useless beggar who cannot work or fight, and now a man pretending to be a prophet. He should put them on a ship and sell them as slaves in Sicily. Telemachus does not answer. He silently watches his father, waiting for the moment when he will attack.

Penelope has placed her chair where she can hear everything said in the hall. The suitors have had a large and happy meal at midday, with many animals sacrificed. But the poem warns that their evening meal will be the most terrible one imaginable. A goddess and a brave man will soon serve it to them, and they have brought it on themselves.

## Book 21: The Contest of the Bow

Athena now puts it into Penelope's mind to set up the contest of the bow and the axes, which will lead to the suitors' death. Penelope takes the bronze key with the ivory handle and goes with her maids to the far storeroom, where her husband's treasures of gold, bronze, and iron are kept. Here also hangs his great bow, with a quiver full of deadly arrows.

The poem tells the history of this bow. Odysseus received it as a young man from a friend named Iphitus. Odysseus had been sent by his father and the other chiefs to the land of Messene, to recover three hundred sheep that the Messenians had stolen from Ithaca along with their shepherds. Iphitus was there too, searching for twelve lost horses. The two young men met and became friends. Iphitus gave Odysseus the bow of his father Eurytus, a famous archer, and Odysseus gave him a sword and a spear. But they never visited each other's homes, because Heracles later killed Iphitus while he was a guest in his house, in order to keep the horses. Heracles broke the sacred law of hospitality. Odysseus never took this bow to Troy. He kept it at home as a memory of his friend. So the bow itself carries a lesson about guests and hosts, which is the theme of the whole revenge.

Penelope opens the storeroom doors, which roar like a bull in a meadow. She climbs up, takes the bow in its case down from a peg, sits with it on her knees, and weeps. When she has cried, she carries the bow and the quiver to the hall. Her maids follow with a chest of iron and bronze axe heads that her husband won as prizes. Standing by a pillar, with her veil before her face, she speaks to the suitors. She says they have abused the hospitality of this house for a long time, on the excuse that they want to marry her. So here is their prize. Whoever can string the great bow of Odysseus most easily, and shoot an arrow through all twelve axes, she will follow him and leave this house, though she will remember it in her dreams.

She tells Eumaeus, the loyal pig-keeper, to set the bow and the axes before the suitors. Eumaeus weeps as he does it. Philoetius, the cowherd, also weeps when he sees his master's bow. Antinous scolds them. He calls them foolish country fellows who upset their mistress with their tears. He says the bow will be very hard to string, because no one here is like Odysseus. He saw him once when he was a child. But secretly Antinous hopes he will string the bow himself. The poem tells us that he will instead be the first man to taste an arrow from Odysseus's hand.

Telemachus then speaks. He says Zeus must have taken away his sense, because his mother says she will leave and marry again, and yet he is laughing. He praises his mother. No woman in all Greece is her equal. He tells the suitors to begin, without delay. He will try the bow too. If he can string it and shoot through the iron, he will not let his mother leave with a stranger. He will have proved himself his father's equal.

He throws off his red cloak and puts down his sword. He digs a long straight trench in the floor and sets the axes in it in a perfect line, pressing the earth around them. Everyone is surprised, because he has never seen this done before. The axes are set up so that the holes in their heads form a line, and the arrow must pass through all of them. Then Telemachus tries to string the bow. Three times he pulls with all his strength, and three times he fails. On the fourth try he would have done it, but Odysseus signals him with a look to stop. So Telemachus says he must be too weak, or too young. He puts the bow down, leaning it against the door, and sits again.

Antinous says the suitors will try in order, from left to right, in the order the wine is served. The first is Leiodes, the suitors' priest, who sits in a corner by the wine bowl. He is the only suitor who disapproves of their wicked behaviour. He tries but cannot string the bow, because his hands are soft and not used to work. He warns the others that this bow will take the life and spirit of many of them. It is better to die than to keep living after failing to win the prize they have waited for so long. He says each man should now go and court another woman with gifts, and let Penelope marry whoever offers most.

Antinous is angry. He says Leiodes is talking nonsense. Just because he is no archer, the bow will not destroy the rest of them. Others will string it. He orders Melanthius, the disloyal goatherd, to light a fire, bring a seat with a sheepskin, and bring a large lump of fat from the kitchen. They will warm the bow and rub it with fat to make it softer. Melanthius does this. The suitors warm and grease the bow and try again and again, but none of them is strong enough. Only Antinous and Eurymachus, their two leaders, have not yet tried.

Meanwhile Eumaeus and Philoetius walk out of the hall together. Odysseus follows them outside the gates. He speaks to them quietly. He asks what they would do if a god suddenly brought Odysseus back. Would they fight for the suitors or for Odysseus? Philoetius says he wishes Zeus would bring his master back, and he would show how hard he would fight for him. Eumaeus prays the same to all the gods.

Now Odysseus is sure of their loyalty, and he reveals himself. He says that he is Odysseus. After much suffering, he has come home in the twentieth year. He has found that of all his servants, only these two want him back. He promises that if the gods let him kill the suitors, he will give each of them a wife, a house, and land near his own, and they will be like brothers to Telemachus. Then he shows them proof: the scar from the boar's tusk, from the hunt on Mount Parnassus with the sons of Autolycus.

When they see the scar, both men weep. They throw their arms around Odysseus and kiss his head and shoulders, and he kisses their heads and hands. They might have cried until sunset, but Odysseus stops them. Someone might come out and see them. He gives them orders. They must go back inside separately, not together. He will go first. The suitors will try to stop him from taking the bow, but Eumaeus must bring the bow to him anyway. Eumaeus must also tell the women to lock the doors of their rooms and stay inside quietly, even if they hear men groaning and fighting. Philoetius must lock and tie the gates of the outer courtyard. Then Odysseus goes back to his seat, and the two men follow.

At this moment Eurymachus has the bow. He is warming it by the fire, but he still cannot string it. He sighs deeply. He says he is not so sad about losing the marriage, because there are many other women in Ithaca and elsewhere. What hurts him is that they are so much weaker than Odysseus that they cannot string his bow. Future generations will speak of their shame.

Antinous says it will not be so. Today is the feast of Apollo, the archer god. No one should string a bow on such a holy day. They should put the bow aside, leave the axes standing, pour drink offerings, and rest. Tomorrow Melanthius can bring the best goats, they will sacrifice to Apollo, and then try again and finish the contest. Everyone agrees. They pour wine and drink.

Then Odysseus speaks, cunningly. He praises Eurymachus and Antinous for their sensible words. He agrees they should stop for today and leave the result to the gods. But in the meantime, he asks to hold the bow, just to see if his hands still have their old strength, or if wandering and hardship have destroyed it.

The suitors are furious, because they are afraid he might succeed. Antinous attacks him with harsh words. He says the beggar has no sense at all. He should be grateful to eat with his betters and listen to their conversation, which no other beggar has been allowed to do. The wine must have gone to his head. He tells the story of the Centaur Eurytion, a creature who was half man and half horse. At a wedding feast he got drunk and behaved violently, so the heroes cut off his ears and nose and threw him out. That started a war between men and Centaurs. Antinous warns that if the beggar strings the bow, they will send him to the cruel King Echetus, who kills everyone. He tells him to drink quietly and not quarrel with younger men. There is deep irony here. Antinous warns against violence at a feast, while violence is about to fall on him.

Penelope speaks up. She says it is wrong to treat Telemachus's guest so badly. Does Antinous really think that if the stranger strings the bow, he will take her home as his wife? Not even the stranger himself expects that. Eurymachus answers that they do not fear that. They fear the gossip. People will say the suitors are weak men who court the wife of a great hero but cannot string his bow, while a poor beggar strung it at once and shot through the iron. That would shame them.

Penelope replies that men who eat a great man's property and dishonour his house cannot expect a good name anyway. So why care about gossip? The stranger is tall and strong and says he comes from a good family. Give him the bow. If Apollo lets him string it, she will give him a cloak, a shirt, a spear to protect himself from dogs and robbers, a sword, sandals, and safe passage wherever he wants to go.

Then Telemachus speaks firmly. He says that he alone has the right to give the bow or refuse it. No one can force him, even if he decides to give the bow to the stranger as a gift. He tells his mother to go upstairs to her room, her loom and her spinning, and her maids. The bow is men's business, and especially his, because he is the master of the house. Penelope is surprised, but she takes his words to heart. She goes upstairs and cries for her husband until Athena sends her sleep. Telemachus wants her out of the hall before the killing begins, to protect her.

Eumaeus picks up the bow to carry it to Odysseus. The suitors shout at him from all sides. One says he must be mad, and if Apollo hears their prayers, his own dogs will tear him apart one day out on his farm. Eumaeus is frightened and puts the bow down. But Telemachus shouts at him from across the hall. He tells Eumaeus to bring the bow anyway, or he will chase him back to the country with stones, because he is stronger. He wishes he were as much stronger than all the suitors as he is than Eumaeus. Then he would quickly send some of them home in pain. The suitors laugh at this, and their anger toward Telemachus softens. So Eumaeus carries the bow across the hall and puts it into Odysseus's hands.

Then Eumaeus quietly tells Eurycleia that Telemachus wants the doors of the women's rooms locked. If the women hear groaning or fighting, they must stay inside and keep working. Eurycleia locks the doors. Meanwhile Philoetius slips out and ties the courtyard gates shut with a ship's rope he finds in the gatehouse. Then he comes back and watches Odysseus.

Odysseus is turning the bow in his hands, examining it carefully on every side, to see whether worms have eaten into the horn while he was away. The suitors make jokes. One says the old man must be an expert in bows. Perhaps he has one like it at home, or wants to make one. Another says he hopes the beggar has as little success in life as he will have with this bow.

But when Odysseus has finished examining the bow, he strings it as easily as a skilled musician puts a new string on his lyre. He tests the string with his right hand, and it sings sweetly, like the voice of a swallow. The suitors turn pale. At that moment Zeus thunders loudly as a sign, and Odysseus's heart rejoices.

He takes an arrow lying on the table. The other arrows, the ones the suitors will soon taste, are still in the quiver. He places it on the bow, pulls back the string while still sitting on his stool, aims, and shoots. The arrow flies through the holes of all twelve axes, from the first to the last, and out into the courtyard.

Then Odysseus speaks to Telemachus. He says the guest has not shamed him. He did not miss, and he strung the bow quickly. He is still strong, not weak as the suitors mocked him. Now, he says, it is time for the suitors to have their supper while it is still light, and then music and dance, which make a feast complete. These words have a terrible double meaning. The supper he is about to serve them is death.

He signals to Telemachus with his eyebrows. Telemachus straps on his sword, takes his spear, and stands beside his father's seat, armed with shining bronze. The contest is over, and the battle is about to begin.

## Book 22: The Killing of the Suitors

Odysseus throws off his rags and jumps onto the wide stone doorstep, holding the bow and the quiver full of arrows. He pours the arrows onto the ground at his feet. He announces that the contest is over, and now he will aim at another target that no man has hit before, if Apollo grants it.

His first arrow is for Antinous, the most arrogant of the suitors. Antinous is lifting a golden two-handled cup of wine to his lips. He has no thought of death. Who would expect one man to attack so many? The arrow hits him in the throat and goes through his neck. He falls backwards, drops the cup, and blood pours from his nose. As he dies, he kicks the table over, and the bread and meat fall into the dirt. The suitor who mocked the beggar for his hungry belly dies over his own food.

The suitors jump up in confusion. They look at the walls for weapons, but there are no shields or spears, because Telemachus and Odysseus removed them the night before. The suitors shout angrily at the beggar. They think he killed Antinous by accident. They tell him he will never compete again. He has killed the best young man in Ithaca, and vultures will eat his body. They do not understand that death is hanging over all of them.

Odysseus looks at them fiercely and reveals himself. He calls them dogs. He asks if they thought he would never come back from Troy. They have eaten his property, forced his women servants to sleep with them, and tried to marry his wife while he was still alive. They have feared neither the gods nor people's judgement. Now they will all die.

The suitors turn pale. Only Eurymachus speaks. If he really is Odysseus, he says, then his accusations are fair. The suitors have done much wrong. But Antinous, who lies dead, was to blame for everything. He did not really care about marrying Penelope. He wanted to kill Telemachus and become king of Ithaca. Now that he has received his punishment, Odysseus should spare the others, his own people. They will repay everything they have eaten and drunk. Each of them will pay a fine of twenty oxen, and more gold and bronze, until his anger is satisfied.

Odysseus refuses. Even if they gave him everything they own now and everything they will ever own, he would not stop until they had all paid in full. They can fight or run, but he says none of them will escape. Revenge, in this world, is not something that can be bought.

Eurymachus tells the others that the man will show no mercy. They must fight. They should draw their swords, hold up the tables as shields against the arrows, and rush at him all together. If they can push him from the doorway, they can escape into the town and call for help. Eurymachus draws his sword and charges with a loud cry. Odysseus shoots him in the chest. He falls over his table, and his cup and food crash to the floor. He hits the ground with his forehead and kicks the stool until darkness covers his eyes.

Then Amphinomus, the kindest of the suitors, rushes at Odysseus with his sword, to drive him from the door. But Telemachus strikes him from behind with his spear, between the shoulders, and kills him. Earlier, Odysseus warned Amphinomus to go home, but he stayed. Telemachus leaves his spear in the body, because he is afraid someone will attack him while he pulls it out, and he runs to his father's side.

Telemachus offers to bring shields, spears, and helmets for his father, for himself, and for the two loyal herdsmen. Odysseus tells him to hurry while the arrows last, so that he is not pushed away from the door when he is alone. Telemachus runs to the storeroom and brings four shields, eight spears, and four bronze helmets with horsehair crests. He arms himself, and Eumaeus and Philoetius put on armour and stand beside Odysseus. Meanwhile, as long as his arrows last, Odysseus shoots the suitors one by one, and they fall on top of each other. When the arrows are finished, he leans the bow against the doorpost, puts on a thick shield and a helmet with a nodding crest, and takes two strong spears.

There is a small side door high in the wall, and a narrow passage leading out of the hall. Odysseus tells Philoetius to guard the passage door, where only one man can attack at a time. Agelaus, one of the suitors, calls for someone to climb up to the side opening and raise the alarm in the town. But Melanthius, the disloyal goatherd, says this is impossible, because the passage is too close to the main entrance. One brave man could stop many. Instead, Melanthius has another idea. He goes by back passages to the storeroom and brings twelve shields, twelve helmets, and twelve spears for the suitors.

When Odysseus sees the suitors putting on armour and waving spears, his heart sinks. The danger is real now. He tells Telemachus that one of the women, or Melanthius, must be helping them. Telemachus admits that it is his own fault. He left the storeroom door open. He sends Eumaeus to close the door and see who is doing it.

Eumaeus sees Melanthius going back for more armour. He asks Odysseus whether to kill him or bring him back for punishment. Odysseus says that he and Telemachus will hold off the suitors. Eumaeus and Philoetius must tie Melanthius's hands and feet behind his back, throw him into the storeroom, and hang him by a rope from a high beam, so that he suffers there alive. The two herdsmen wait on either side of the storeroom door. Melanthius comes out carrying a helmet and an old rotten shield that once belonged to Laertes. They grab him by the hair, throw him to the ground, tie him painfully, and pull him up to the rafters. Here the poet again speaks directly to Eumaeus, saying that he mocked Melanthius. Eumaeus tells him he will have a comfortable night on a soft bed, and he will be sure to wake at dawn, when it is time to bring goats for the suitors' dinner. Then the two lock the door and return to Odysseus's side.

Now four men stand at the door, full of fury, against many suitors who are still brave. Then Athena appears, in the form and voice of old Mentor. Odysseus is glad, though he suspects it is really the goddess. He asks Mentor, his old friend of the same age, to help him. The suitors shout at Mentor. Agelaus threatens him. He says that if Mentor helps Odysseus, then after they kill father and son, they will kill Mentor too, take his property, and drive his wife and children out of Ithaca.

This makes Athena even angrier. She scolds Odysseus to test him. She says his strength is not what it was at Troy, where he fought for nine years for Helen, killed many men, and took the city by his clever plan. Why is he weaker now, in his own house, facing the suitors? She tells him to stand beside her and see how Mentor repays his kindness. But Athena does not yet give him full victory. She wants to test the courage of Odysseus and his son a little more. So she flies up and sits on a roof beam in the form of a swallow.

The bravest suitors still alive are Agelaus, Eurynomus, Amphimedon, Demoptolemus, Pisander, and Polybus. Agelaus says that Mentor has gone after empty boasting, and the four men are alone at the door. He tells six of them to throw their spears together at Odysseus. If Odysseus falls, the others will not matter. But Athena makes their spears useless. One hits the doorpost, one hits the door, and one hits the wall. Then Odysseus tells his men to throw. Odysseus kills Demoptolemus, Telemachus kills Euryades, Eumaeus kills Elatus, and Philoetius kills Pisander. The suitors move back, and Odysseus's men pull their spears from the bodies.

The suitors throw again, and again Athena turns their spears aside. Amphimedon only scratches the skin of Telemachus's wrist, and Ctesippus grazes Eumaeus's shoulder above his shield. Then Odysseus and his men throw again. Odysseus kills Eurydamas, Telemachus kills Amphimedon, and Eumaeus kills Polybus. Philoetius hits Ctesippus in the chest. He mocks him and says this is his gift in return for the cow's foot that Ctesippus threw at Odysseus when he was begging in his own house. Odysseus kills Agelaus in close fighting, and Telemachus kills Leiocritus, the suitor who mocked Mentor at the assembly long ago.

Then Athena, from the roof, raises her aegis, a terrible magic shield or goatskin that fills enemies with panic. The suitors' hearts fail. They run to the other end of the hall like cattle driven mad by stinging flies on a summer day. Odysseus and his men fall on them like vultures with hooked claws diving on small birds that cannot fight or fly. The hall is full of the sound of dying men, and the floor runs with blood.

Leiodes, the suitors' priest, falls at Odysseus's knees. He begs for mercy. He says he never harmed any woman in the house, never spoke or acted badly, and tried to stop the others. He was only their priest, and he will die without deserving it. But Odysseus answers coldly. If he was their priest, he must have prayed many times that Odysseus would never return, and that he could marry Penelope and have children with her. So he must die. Odysseus picks up the sword that Agelaus dropped and strikes Leiodes on the neck. His head falls into the dust while he is still speaking.

Next is Phemius, the singer, whom the suitors forced to sing for them. He is standing near the side door, holding his lyre. He thinks about running to the altar of Zeus in the courtyard, where a person can ask for the god's protection. Instead he decides to beg Odysseus directly. He puts his lyre down carefully and holds Odysseus's knees. He says that Odysseus will regret it if he kills a singer who sings for gods and men. The gods have taught him his songs. He will sing for Odysseus as if he were a god. Telemachus can tell him that Phemius never wanted to sing for the suitors. They forced him, because they were many and strong.

Telemachus hears this and tells his father to stop. This man is innocent. He also asks to spare Medon, the herald, who was always kind to him when he was a boy, if he is still alive. Medon has been hiding under a chair, covered by a fresh cowhide. He throws off the hide, runs to Telemachus, and holds his knees. He asks Telemachus to protect him from his father's anger. Odysseus smiles at him. He tells Medon not to fear. Telemachus has saved him, so that he may know and tell others how much better good deeds are than bad ones. He sends Medon and Phemius out to the courtyard, away from the killing. They sit by the altar of Zeus, looking around in fear, still expecting to die.

Odysseus searches the hall to see if anyone is hiding and still alive. But all the suitors lie dead in the dust and blood, heaped together like fish that fishermen have pulled from the sea in a net and thrown on the sand, where they die in the hot sun.

Odysseus asks Telemachus to call Eurycleia. When the old nurse comes out, she finds Odysseus covered in blood and dirt, like a lion that has just eaten an ox. When she sees the bodies and the blood, she starts to shout with joy. But Odysseus stops her. He tells her to rejoice silently. It is not holy to boast over dead men. The gods' judgement and their own evil deeds destroyed them, because they respected no one, rich or poor, who came to them. This is an important moment. Odysseus presents his revenge as justice, not as a personal triumph.

Then he asks which women in the house have been disloyal. Eurycleia says there are fifty women servants. Twelve of them behaved shamefully and disrespected both her and Penelope. Telemachus was too young to give orders to the women servants, so they did not disrespect him. She offers to go and tell Penelope, whom a god has put to sleep. Odysseus tells her not to wake Penelope yet, but to send the twelve disloyal women to him.

Odysseus orders Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius to make the women carry out the dead bodies, then wash the tables and chairs with sponges and water. After that, the men should take the women outside, between the round building and the courtyard wall, and kill them with swords.

The women come down, weeping and crying aloud. They carry out the bodies and lay them in the gatehouse. They clean the tables and chairs, while the men scrape the blood and dirt from the floor, and the women carry it outside. When the house is clean, the men lead the women out and trap them in a narrow space. But Telemachus decides that they will not die a clean death by the sword, because they insulted him and his mother and slept with the suitors. He ties a ship's rope high between pillars and hangs them all, side by side, like birds caught in a hunter's net. Their feet move for a little while, and then they are still. Then the men take Melanthius out into the courtyard. In their anger they cut off his nose and ears, his hands and feet, and throw parts of his body to the dogs.

These punishments are very harsh, even by the standards of the poem. Modern readers often find the hanging of the maids especially troubling, because they were servants with little power. The poem presents it as punishment for disloyalty, but many readers see it as one of the darkest moments in the story.

When it is over, the men wash and go back into the house. Odysseus asks Eurycleia for sulphur and fire, to purify the house. In the ancient world, burning sulphur was used to clean a place of the pollution of death. He also asks her to tell Penelope and all the loyal women servants to come. Eurycleia offers him clean clothes, saying it is not right for him to stand in rags. But Odysseus says the fire comes first. She brings fire and sulphur, and he purifies the hall and the courtyards.

Then the loyal women come out with torches. They crowd around Odysseus, welcome him home, kiss his head and shoulders, and hold his hands. Odysseus feels a strong wish to cry, because he remembers every one of them. After all the violence, the book ends with this moment of tender homecoming.

## Book 23: Penelope's Test and the Secret of the Bed

Eurycleia, the old nurse, runs upstairs to tell Penelope the news. She laughs with joy, and her old legs feel young again. She bends over Penelope and wakes her. She tells her to come and see with her own eyes what she has wanted for so long. Odysseus has come home and killed the suitors who were eating his property and mistreating his son.

Penelope does not believe her. She says the gods must have made the nurse mad. The gods can make wise people foolish and foolish people wise. Why is she mocking her and waking her from the sweetest sleep she has had since her husband left for Troy? If any other woman had woken her with such nonsense, she would have scolded her harshly. Only Eurycleia's age protects her.

Eurycleia insists that it is true. The stranger whom everyone mistreated in the hall was Odysseus. Telemachus knew, but kept the secret so that his father could take revenge. Penelope jumps up, hugs the old woman, and cries with joy. But then she asks how one man could kill so many suitors. Eurycleia says she did not see it. She and the women sat locked in their room and only heard men groaning. Then Telemachus came to fetch her, and she found Odysseus standing among piles of bodies, covered in blood like a lion. Now the bodies are in the gatehouse, and Odysseus is purifying the house with sulphur and fire. He has sent for Penelope, so they can be happy together.

Penelope again warns her not to celebrate too soon. Everyone would be glad to see Odysseus return, especially she and her son. But this story cannot be true. She thinks a god must have killed the suitors, angry at their wickedness, because they respected no one, rich or poor. Odysseus himself, she says, died far away and will never come home.

Eurycleia scolds her for being so hard to convince. She gives another proof. When she was washing the stranger's feet, she saw the scar from the wild boar. She wanted to tell Penelope, but Odysseus put his hand over her mouth. She says that if she is lying, Penelope may kill her in the most cruel way. Penelope answers that even a wise woman cannot understand the plans of the gods. But she agrees to go down and see the dead suitors and the man who killed them.

As she walks downstairs, Penelope wonders what to do. Should she stand at a distance and question him? Or should she run to him and kiss him? She crosses the hall and sits down opposite Odysseus, on the other side of the fire. Odysseus sits by a pillar, looking at the ground, waiting for his wife to speak. For a long time she sits in silence, amazed. Sometimes she looks straight at his face and almost knows him. But then his dirty, ragged clothes confuse her again.

Telemachus loses patience. He scolds his mother. He says she is so hard that he can hardly call her mother. Why does she keep away from his father and not talk to him? No other woman could stay away from her husband after twenty years and so much suffering. Her heart, he says, is harder than stone.

Penelope answers that she is so amazed that she cannot speak, ask questions, or even look at him directly. But if he really is Odysseus, they will soon know each other. There are secret signs that only the two of them know. Odysseus smiles at this. He tells Telemachus to let his mother test him. She does not accept him now because he is dirty and in rags, but she will soon decide. Penelope is acting like her husband. She, too, is careful and does not trust appearances.

Then Odysseus turns to a serious problem. When a man kills even one person who has few friends, he must leave his country to escape revenge. But they have killed the best young men of Ithaca and the nearby islands, the strength of the city. What should they do now? Telemachus says his father is famous as the wisest of men, so he should decide, and they will follow him bravely.

Odysseus makes a clever plan. Everyone should wash and dress in clean clothes, and the women should dress too. Then Phemius, the singer, should play dance music on his lyre. If anyone outside, a neighbour or a passer-by, hears the noise, they will think there is a wedding in the house. So the news of the suitors' death will not spread in the town until Odysseus and his men can escape to his country estate and decide what to do next.

They do as he says. Phemius plays, and men and women dance. The whole house echoes with music. People outside say that the queen has finally married one of her suitors. They think she should be ashamed, because she did not wait to protect her husband's house until he came home. This is a bitter irony, because Penelope's husband is indeed home.

Meanwhile, the housekeeper Eurynome bathes Odysseus, rubs him with oil, and dresses him in a fine shirt and cloak. Athena makes him look taller and stronger, with thick curly hair like hyacinth flowers. She makes him shine like silver covered with gold by a skilled craftsman. He comes out of the bath looking like a god and sits opposite his wife again.

Now Odysseus speaks to her. He says the gods have given her a harder heart than any other woman. No other wife could stay away from a husband who returned after twenty years of suffering. Then he tells the nurse to prepare a bed for him alone, because his wife's heart is made of iron.

Penelope answers carefully. She says she is not proud, and she does not look down on him. She is not overwhelmed by his appearance, because she remembers very well what he looked like when he sailed from Ithaca. Then she sets her test. She tells Eurycleia to take the bed out of the bedroom that Odysseus built himself and put it outside, with fleeces, covers, and blankets.

This is a trick, and Odysseus falls into it. He becomes very angry. He asks who has moved his bed from where he left it. No man could move it, however strong, unless a god helped him. Then he explains the secret. When he built the house, a large young olive tree was growing in the courtyard, as thick as a pillar. He built the bedroom around this tree, with stone walls, a roof, and strong doors. Then he cut off the top of the tree, smoothed the trunk, and shaped it into the main post of the bed. He built the rest of the bed around it and decorated it with gold, silver, and ivory, and stretched red leather straps across it. The bed is part of a living tree, rooted in the earth. So he wants to know: is the bed still there, or has someone cut through the olive tree at its roots?

When Penelope hears this, she knows the truth at once. Her knees and heart give way. She runs to him in tears, throws her arms around his neck, and kisses him. She asks him not to be angry, because he is the wisest of men. The gods have been cruel to them. They did not let them enjoy their youth together and grow old together. She asks him to forgive her for not embracing him at once. All this time she was afraid that someone would trick her with a false story, because there are many wicked people in the world. She mentions Helen. Helen would never have gone with a foreign man if she had known that the Greeks would come to bring her back. A god put the idea in her mind, and that wrong began all their sorrows. Penelope seems to say that she had to be careful not to make a similar mistake. Now he has given her proof by describing the bed, which no one has ever seen except the two of them and one maid, Actoris, who was given to her by her father and who guards their bedroom door. So, hard as she has been to convince, she now believes him completely.

The bed is the perfect symbol of their marriage. It is fixed and rooted, and it cannot be moved. And the test shows that Penelope is as clever as Odysseus. She tricks the master trickster.

Now Odysseus also weeps, holding his dear and faithful wife. The poem gives a beautiful comparison. Just as land is welcome to sailors whose ship Poseidon has destroyed, and who swim to shore covered with salt, glad to reach safety, so Penelope is glad to see her husband. She cannot take her white arms from around his neck. This comparison is special, because it gives Penelope the experience of a shipwrecked swimmer, like Odysseus himself in Book 5. Her long wait has been her own journey.

They would have cried until dawn, but Athena does something wonderful. She holds back the night in the west and keeps Dawn waiting at the edge of the sea. She does not let Dawn harness her horses to bring the light. So the night becomes longer for the husband and wife.

Odysseus tells Penelope that their troubles are not completely over. He still has a long and hard task, as the ghost of Tiresias told him in the land of the dead. But now they should go to bed. Penelope says he can go to bed whenever he likes, now that the gods have brought him home. But first she wants to hear about this task. She will learn about it later anyway, so it is better to know now.

Odysseus says he does not like it, but he will tell her. Tiresias told him to travel far, carrying an oar on his shoulder, until he reaches people who have never heard of the sea, who do not put salt in their food and know nothing about ships. When a traveller asks him why he carries a winnowing shovel, he must plant the oar in the ground and sacrifice a ram, a bull, and a boar to Poseidon. Then he must go home and make sacrifices to all the gods. Death will come to him gently, away from the sea, when he is old and at peace, with his people prospering around him. Penelope says that if the gods will give him a happy old age, then there is hope for an end to their troubles.

Meanwhile Eurynome and Eurycleia prepare the bed by torchlight. Eurycleia goes to bed, and Eurynome leads Odysseus and Penelope to their room. They come gladly to their old bed. Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius stop the dancing, send the women to bed, and lie down to sleep in the hall.

After making love, Odysseus and Penelope talk. She tells him what she suffered, watching the wicked suitors fill the house and kill so many sheep and cattle and drink so much wine for her sake. He tells her about his own suffering, and also about the trouble he caused to others. He tells her everything, and she listens so eagerly that she does not sleep until he finishes. He tells her about the Cicones, the Lotus-Eaters, and the Cyclops, whom he punished for eating his men. He tells about Aeolus, who helped him, and the storm that blew him back. He tells about the Laestrygonians who destroyed all his ships but one, about Circe, about his journey to the land of the dead, where he saw Tiresias, his old friends, and his mother. He tells about the Sirens, the Wandering Rocks, Charybdis, and Scylla. He tells how his men ate the cattle of the Sun, and how Zeus destroyed the ship and all the men died. He tells about Calypso, who kept him in her cave and wanted to make him immortal, and about the Phaeacians, who honoured him like a god and sent him home with rich gifts. That is the last thing he tells her, and then sweet sleep takes him. In a few lines, the whole adventure is retold, now as a private story between husband and wife.

When Athena decides that Odysseus has had enough love and rest, she lets Dawn rise from the ocean. Odysseus gets up and speaks to Penelope. He says they have both had their full share of suffering, she by mourning his absence and he by being kept from home. Now she must take care of the property in the house. As for the sheep and goats the suitors ate, he will take many from other people by raids, and the Greeks will have to give the rest, until his yards are full again. Now he will go to the country to see his old father, who has grieved for him so long. He warns her that as soon as the sun rises, the news of the suitors' death will spread. She must go upstairs with her women, see no one, and ask no questions.

Then he puts on his armour and wakes Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius, and they put on their armour too. They open the gates and go out, with Odysseus leading the way. It is daylight now, but Athena hides them in darkness and leads them quickly out of the town. The husband and wife are reunited, but the danger from the suitors' families is still waiting.

## Book 24: Laertes, and Peace at Last

The last book begins in the underworld. Hermes, the guide of souls, raises the ghosts of the dead suitors with his golden wand and leads them down to the house of Hades. They follow him squeaking and crying, like bats squealing in a dark cave when one of them falls from the rock. They pass the river Ocean, the White Rock, the gates of the Sun, and the land of dreams, and they reach the meadow of asphodel flowers, where the spirits of the dead live.

There they find the ghosts of the great heroes of Troy: Achilles, Patroclus, Antilochus, and Ajax. The ghost of Agamemnon comes too, with the men who died with him. Before the suitors arrive, Achilles and Agamemnon are talking. Achilles says that everyone thought Zeus loved Agamemnon most, because he led so many brave men. Yet he died a miserable death. It would have been better for him to die at Troy, at the height of his fame, with a great grave and glory for his son.

Agamemnon answers that Achilles was the lucky one. He describes the funeral of Achilles at Troy. The Greeks fought all day to rescue his body. They washed him and mourned him. His mother, the sea goddess Thetis, came from the sea with her nymphs, and the sound of their crying frightened the Greeks so much that they almost ran to their ships, until wise Nestor told them who it was. The sea nymphs dressed his body in divine clothes. The nine Muses sang songs of mourning. For seventeen days gods and men wept, and on the eighteenth day they burned his body. They put his bones in a golden jar with the bones of his friend Patroclus, and built a great tomb on a cliff above the sea, so that sailors would see it for all time. Thetis offered wonderful prizes for funeral games. So Achilles's fame will never die. But Agamemnon, he says, had no such comfort. Zeus planned his death at the hands of Aegisthus and his wicked wife.

Then Hermes arrives with the ghosts of the suitors. Agamemnon recognizes one of them, Amphimedon, who once hosted him in Ithaca when Agamemnon and Menelaus came to persuade Odysseus to go to Troy. Agamemnon asks how so many fine young men, all of the same age, died together. Was it a storm at sea, or a battle while stealing cattle, or a war?

Amphimedon tells the story from the suitors' point of view. They were courting the wife of Odysseus, who would neither refuse nor accept them, because she was planning their destruction. He tells the story of the weaving and the burial cloth for Laertes, which she undid each night for three years, until a maid betrayed her. When she finally finished it and washed it, it shone like the sun or the moon. Then, he says, some evil god brought Odysseus back to the farm of the swineherd. His son came home from Pylos, and together they planned the suitors' death. Odysseus came to town dressed as a beggar, leaning on a stick, and no one recognized him, not even the older men. The suitors insulted him and threw things at him, and he bore it all in silence. Then he and Telemachus hid the weapons. Then Odysseus cunningly had his wife offer the bow and the axes as a contest. None of the suitors could string the bow. They tried to stop the beggar from getting it, but Telemachus insisted. Odysseus strung it easily, shot through the axes, and then began shooting the suitors, starting with Antinous. A god was clearly helping him. Now, Amphimedon says, their bodies are still lying unburied in the house of Odysseus, because their families do not yet know.

Interestingly, Amphimedon believes that Odysseus planned the contest with Penelope. The poem does not say this happened. It is the suitors' interpretation, and it shows how the story looks from the losing side.

Agamemnon's ghost then praises Penelope. He calls Odysseus happy and blessed, because he has a wife of such great wisdom and loyalty. Her fame will never die, and the gods will make a beautiful song about her faithfulness for all people. He compares her to his own wife, Clytemnestra, who murdered her husband. Her song will be hateful and will bring shame on all women, even the good ones. So the poem ends the story of Agamemnon, which began in Book 1, with a clear comparison. Penelope is the faithful wife, and Clytemnestra is the traitor.

Now the story returns to Ithaca. Odysseus, Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius leave the town and reach the farm of Laertes, which the old man made from wild land with great effort. There is a house with rooms around it where the slaves live and eat. An old Sicilian woman takes care of Laertes. Odysseus tells his son and the herdsmen to go into the house and kill the best pig for dinner. He wants to see if his father will recognize him after so long.

He gives his armour to Eumaeus and Philoetius and goes down into the vineyard alone. The old servant Dolius and his sons are away, gathering thorns to build a fence around the vineyard. So Odysseus finds his father alone, digging around a vine. Laertes wears a dirty, patched shirt. His legs are wrapped in leather to protect them from thorns, he wears leather sleeves on his arms, and he has a goatskin cap on his head. He looks deeply sad. When Odysseus sees his father so old, worn, and full of grief, he stops under a tall pear tree and weeps.

He wonders whether to run to him, kiss him, and tell him everything, or to test him first with questions. He decides to test him. This is a moment many readers find hard to understand. Perhaps Odysseus simply cannot stop being careful. Perhaps he wants to wake his father slowly from his long grief.

He walks up to his father and praises his garden. Every plant, fig tree, vine, olive, and pear tree shows careful work. But, he says politely, he hopes the old man will not be offended. He takes better care of his garden than of himself. He is old, dirty, and badly dressed. He does not look like a slave. He looks like a man of noble birth who should bathe, eat well, and sleep softly. So whose servant is he? And is this really Ithaca? A man he met said so, but that man did not listen to his story. He is looking for an old friend. Once, in his own country, he hosted a man from Ithaca, the son of Laertes. He liked him more than any other guest. He gave him rich gifts: seven talents of gold, a silver bowl, twelve cloaks, twelve rugs, twelve shirts, and four skilled women servants.

Laertes weeps. He says this is indeed Ithaca, but it is now in the hands of wicked men. All those gifts were given for nothing. If the stranger had found his friend alive, he would have repaid him generously. He asks how many years ago the stranger hosted this guest, his unhappy son. He believes his son is dead far away, eaten by fish or by wild animals and birds. His mother and father could not wrap his body for burial, and his wife Penelope could not close his eyes and mourn him. Then he asks who the stranger is, and where his ship is.

Odysseus tells another invented story. He says he comes from a place called Alybas, the son of a king, and his name is Eperitus. A god blew him off course on his way from Sicily. His ship is waiting outside the town. It is now the fifth year since Odysseus left his country. When they parted, the birds flew on the right, a good sign, and they hoped to meet again and exchange gifts.

When Laertes hears this, a dark cloud of grief falls over him. He takes dust from the ground in both hands and pours it over his grey head, groaning. This is a traditional act of deep mourning. Odysseus's heart is moved. His nostrils tremble with strong feeling. He cannot continue the test. He jumps forward, throws his arms around his father, and kisses him. He says he is the son the old man is asking about. He has come home in the twentieth year. But there is no time for crying, because he has just killed the suitors in his house to punish their crimes.

Laertes asks for clear proof. Odysseus shows him the scar from the boar's tusk on Mount Parnassus, where his parents sent him to visit his grandfather Autolycus. Then he gives a second, very personal proof. When he was a little boy, he followed his father around this same garden and asked about each tree. Laertes told him their names and gave him thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees, forty fig trees, and fifty rows of vines, with grain planted between them. As with Penelope and the bed, the final proof is a private memory connected to something living and rooted, here the trees of the family orchard.

When Laertes hears this, his knees give way and his heart melts. He throws his arms around his son, and he almost faints. Odysseus holds him up. When he recovers, Laertes cries out to Zeus. He says the gods must still be on Olympus, if the suitors have truly been punished for their pride. But then he worries. The people of Ithaca will come here soon to take revenge, and they will send messages to all the cities of the region. Odysseus tells him not to worry and invites him into the house, where Telemachus and the herdsmen are preparing dinner.

At the house, the old Sicilian woman bathes Laertes, rubs him with oil, and gives him a fine cloak. Athena makes him taller and stronger. When he comes out, his son is amazed and says a god must have made him look so fine. Laertes wishes he were as strong as when he was young and captured the fortress of Nericus. If he had been in the house yesterday, he would have killed many suitors, and his son would have been proud.

They sit down to eat. Old Dolius and his sons come in from the fields, called by their mother, the Sicilian woman. When they see Odysseus and realize who he is, they stand frozen in surprise. Odysseus tells them kindly to sit down and eat, since everyone has been waiting for them. Dolius takes his master's hand, kisses his wrist, and welcomes him home, saying they had given up hope. He asks if Penelope knows, or if someone should tell her. Odysseus says she already knows. Dolius's sons greet Odysseus one by one, and they all eat together. Dolius is the father of the disloyal Melanthius and Melantho, but he and these sons remain faithful.

Meanwhile, Rumour spreads through the town, telling everyone the terrible fate of the suitors. People come crying and shouting to the house of Odysseus. They take the bodies away and bury their own dead. They send the bodies of men from other islands home on fishing boats. Then they gather angrily in the assembly.

Eupeithes, the father of Antinous, speaks first, weeping for his son, who was the first man Odysseus killed. He says Odysseus has done great harm to the people. First he took many of their best men to Troy and lost all his ships and men. Now he has come back and killed the best men of the islands. They must act before he escapes to Pylos or Elis. If they do not take revenge for their sons and brothers, they will be shamed forever. He would rather die than live without revenge. Everyone pities him.

But then Medon the herald and Phemius the singer arrive from the house of Odysseus. Everyone is surprised to see them alive. Medon tells the assembly that Odysseus did not act against the will of the gods. He himself saw a god in the shape of Mentor standing beside Odysseus, sometimes encouraging him and sometimes rushing through the hall attacking the suitors, so that they fell in heaps.

The people turn pale with fear. Then the old prophet Halitherses speaks, the man who reads omens. He tells them it is their own fault. They did not listen to him or to Mentor when they told them to stop their sons from wasting the property and insulting the wife of a man they thought would never return. He tells them not to attack Odysseus, or they will bring more disaster on themselves.

More than half the people shout and leave the assembly, agreeing with Halitherses. But the rest side with Eupeithes. They put on armour and gather outside the town, and Eupeithes leads them in their foolishness. He thinks he will avenge his son, but he will never come home.

On Olympus, Athena asks Zeus what he plans. Will there be more war, or will he make peace? Zeus reminds her that she herself arranged Odysseus's return and revenge. She may do as she likes, but he gives his opinion. Now that Odysseus has his revenge, they should make a solemn agreement. Odysseus will remain king, and the gods will make the families forget the killing of their sons and brothers. Then everyone will be friends again as before, and there will be peace and plenty. This is what Athena already wants, so she flies down from Olympus.

At Laertes's farm, they finish dinner. Odysseus sends one of Dolius's sons to look out. He sees the angry crowd coming close and calls for weapons. Odysseus, his three companions, and the six sons of Dolius put on armour. Laertes and Dolius also arm themselves, old as they are, warriors because they must be. They open the gate and march out, with Odysseus in front.

Athena joins them in the form and voice of Mentor. Odysseus is glad. He tells Telemachus that in this battle, where every man shows his courage, he must not shame his ancestors, who were famous for their strength and bravery. Telemachus answers that his father will see he will not shame the family. Old Laertes is overjoyed. He says what a day this is, when his son and his grandson compete with each other in courage. Three generations of the family stand together.

Athena comes close to Laertes. She tells him to pray to her and to Zeus, then throw his spear. She fills him with new strength. He prays and throws. The spear hits Eupeithes in the helmet and goes through it, and Eupeithes falls dead with a crash of armour. The father who wanted revenge for Antinous is killed by the father of Odysseus.

Odysseus and Telemachus attack the front line of the enemy with swords and spears. They would have killed every one of them. But Athena shouts in a loud voice. She tells the men of Ithaca to stop this terrible war and settle the matter at once, without more bloodshed. Pale fear takes hold of them all. Their weapons fall from their hands, and they run back to the city to save their lives.

But Odysseus, full of battle rage, gives a great cry and rushes after them like an eagle diving from the sky. Then Zeus throws a thunderbolt of fire, which lands right in front of Athena. Athena tells Odysseus to stop the fighting, or Zeus will be angry with him. Odysseus obeys gladly.

Then Athena, still in the form and voice of Mentor, makes a solemn peace agreement between the two sides. And so the poem ends. The cycle of revenge, which could have gone on for generations, is stopped by the gods. After twenty years of war, wandering, and suffering, Odysseus is home with his wife, his son, and his father, and Ithaca finally has peace.

# The Art of War

Sun Tzu

## Before You Begin

The Art of War is one of the oldest books about war in the world. According to Chinese tradition, it was written by a man called Sun Tzu, whose personal name was Wu. The word "Tzu" is a title of respect, a little like "Master". Tradition says that Sun Tzu came from the state of Ch'i, and that he served as a general for King Ho Lu of Wu, one of many small states in China at that time. This would place him in the late sixth century BC, around 500 BC.

The most famous story about Sun Tzu comes from the great Chinese historian Ssu-ma Ch'ien, and Giles retells it in his introduction. King Ho Lu had read Sun Tzu's thirteen chapters, and he wanted to test his ideas. He asked whether Sun Tzu could train women as soldiers. Sun Tzu agreed. One hundred and eighty women from the palace were brought out, and Sun Tzu divided them into two companies. He put one of the king's two favorite concubines at the head of each company. A concubine was a woman who lived with a king as a kind of second wife. Sun Tzu explained the commands clearly and then gave the order to turn right. The women only laughed. Sun Tzu said that if orders are not clear, the general is to blame. He explained again and gave the order to turn left. Again the women laughed. This time Sun Tzu said that if the orders are clear and the soldiers still do not obey, it is the fault of their officers. He ordered the two leaders to be executed. The king, watching from a high platform, begged him to spare them. Sun Tzu replied that as general, there were some of the king's commands he could not accept. The two women were killed, and new leaders were chosen. After that, the women followed every command perfectly and in silence. The story is harsh, but it shows the book's view that clear orders and strict discipline are the base of any army. According to the history, the king then made Sun Tzu a general, and Wu went on to defeat the powerful state of Ch'u.

Lionel Giles, who translated the book into English in 1910, explains that scholars have long doubted parts of this story. The most important early Chinese record of the period, a book called the Tso Chuan, does not mention Sun Tzu at all, either as a general or as a writer. Because of this, some Chinese scholars questioned whether he really existed, and one called the story of the women "utterly preposterous and incredible". Giles himself takes a middle position. He believes the thirteen chapters are very old and were written by a real soldier with practical experience. But he thinks the book was probably written somewhat later than the traditional story says, during the wars between Wu and its neighbor Yüeh, and that its author was not a very famous man in his own time. Giles also suggests that the story of the women may have grown out of some real event. So, even in Giles's account, the date and the author of the book remain uncertain. It is also worth knowing a later view that does not come from Giles. Many modern scholars date the text later still, to the Warring States period, which lasted from about the fifth to the third century BC, and they think it may have been shaped by more than one person over time. This later scholarly view is not found in the source used for this summary.

The book itself is short. It has thirteen chapters, and each chapter is made of short numbered sayings. Some sayings are only one line long. Sun Tzu does not tell stories or describe battles in detail. Instead he gives brief rules and memorable images, such as water flowing downhill or a snake that fights back with its head and its tail. Over the centuries, many Chinese commentators wrote notes to explain these sayings. They added examples from Chinese history. Giles's translation includes a large number of these notes, together with his own comments, which often compare Sun Tzu's ideas with European generals such as Napoleon and Wellington. In this summary, Sun Tzu's own teaching always comes first. When an example or explanation comes from a commentator or from Giles, the summary says so clearly.

Today, The Art of War is read far outside the army. Business leaders, sports coaches, lawyers, and politicians often quote it. They like its focus on planning, knowing your opponent, avoiding costly conflict, and winning without a fight. Some of its lines, such as "All warfare is based on deception" and "If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles", are known all over the world. However, it is important to remember that applying this advice to business or daily life is a modern use of the book. It was not Sun Tzu's own purpose. He wrote about real war between states, where the cost was measured in lives, and some of his advice, such as executing soldiers or deceiving one's own men, belongs clearly to that ancient world. This summary will sometimes mention how an idea is read today, but it will always mark this as a modern reading.

A short note on names. Giles writes Chinese names in an old spelling system, so he writes the author's name as Sun Tzŭ, and the state as Wu. Other books may use different spellings, such as Sunzi. This summary uses simple spellings like Sun Tzu, and it keeps Giles's spelling for other names so that readers can find them in his translation. The chapter titles follow Giles's English titles.

This summary is based on [Project Gutenberg's edition of Lionel Giles's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/132), which is in the public domain.

## Chapter 1: Laying Plans

The first chapter is about planning before a war begins. The Chinese title, as the commentator Ts'ao Kung explains it, refers to the careful thinking a general did in a temple that was set aside for him before a campaign. Today we might say he made his plans in his tent or in his office.

Sun Tzu opens with a serious warning. War is of vital importance to the State. It is a matter of life and death, and a road either to safety or to ruin. For this reason, no ruler can ignore the study of it.

He then says that war is governed by five constant factors. A leader must think about all five when he judges a situation. They are the Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, the Commander, and Method and discipline.

The Moral Law is the first factor. Sun Tzu says it makes the people agree completely with their ruler. Then they will follow him even at the risk of their lives, and they will not be afraid of danger. Giles explains in a note that this "Moral Law" is a kind of harmony between ruler and people. It is close to the idea of the Tao, or "the Way", in Chinese thought. Giles says he almost translated it as "morale", which means the spirit and confidence of a group. But he did not, because Sun Tzu treats it as a quality of the ruler.

Heaven is the second factor. It means night and day, cold and heat, and the times and seasons. In simple words, it is weather and timing. Earth is the third factor. It means distances, large and small. It also means danger and safety, open ground and narrow passes, and the chances of life and death. In simple words, it is the land where the fighting happens.

The Commander is the fourth factor. A good general must have five qualities: wisdom, sincerity, kindness, courage, and strictness. Giles notes that Chinese tradition had its own list of five main virtues. Sun Tzu changes that list. He puts wisdom and sincerity first, and he adds the two military virtues of courage and strictness.

Method and discipline is the fifth factor. It means organizing the army into proper units and giving officers clear ranks. It also means keeping the roads open so that supplies can reach the army, and controlling military spending.

Sun Tzu says every general should know these five things well. The one who knows them will win. The one who does not know them will fail.

Next, Sun Tzu turns the five factors into seven questions. A leader should use them to compare his side with the enemy's side. First, which of the two rulers has the Moral Law, meaning which one is in harmony with his people? Second, which general is more able? Third, which side has the advantages of Heaven and Earth, meaning weather, timing, and ground? Fourth, which side enforces discipline more strictly? Fifth, which army is stronger? Sixth, which side has better trained officers and soldiers? Seventh, which side is more consistent in giving rewards and punishments?

The commentator Tu Mu tells a famous story about discipline. The great general Ts'ao Ts'ao had made a strict rule that no one could damage the crops in the fields. One day his own horse jumped into a field of corn. Ts'ao Ts'ao judged himself guilty under his own law. His officers persuaded him not to die, so he cut off his hair instead as a punishment. The story shows that a leader's rules must apply to the leader too.

With these seven questions, Sun Tzu says, he can predict victory or defeat. Then he speaks directly to a ruler. If a general listens to his advice and uses it, that general will win and should be kept in command. If a general does not listen, he will lose and should be dismissed. Giles points out that this sentence suggests the book was written for a particular ruler, King Ho Lu of Wu.

Sun Tzu then adds an important point. The rules are a base, but a leader must also use any helpful conditions beyond the ordinary rules. When circumstances are good, plans should change to fit them. Giles says that Sun Tzu was a practical soldier who did not trust theory alone. Giles gives a story about the Duke of Wellington before the battle of Waterloo. One of his officers asked him for his plans. Wellington asked who would attack first the next day, he or Napoleon. The officer said Napoleon. Wellington replied that Napoleon had not told him his plans, and since his own plans depended on Napoleon's, he could not say what they were.

Then comes one of the most famous lines in the book: "All warfare is based on deception." Deception means making someone believe something that is not true. Sun Tzu explains what this means in practice. When you are able to attack, you must seem unable. When you are using your forces, you must seem inactive. When you are near, make the enemy think you are far away. When you are far away, make him think you are near.

He continues with more ways to trick the enemy. Offer baits, small rewards that tempt the enemy into a trap. Pretend to be in disorder, and then crush him. If the enemy is well prepared everywhere, be ready for him. If he is stronger, avoid him. If he has a quick temper, try to make him angry. Pretend to be weak, so that he becomes proud and careless. A commentator quoted by Tu Yu compares this to a cat playing with a mouse. The cat first seems weak and still, and then it suddenly jumps.

If the enemy is resting, give him no rest. If his forces are united, separate them. Attack where he is not prepared, and appear where he does not expect you. Sun Tzu adds that these methods lead to victory, but they must not be told to anyone in advance.

The chapter ends by returning to planning. The general who wins makes many calculations in the temple before the battle is fought. The general who loses makes only a few calculations. Many calculations lead to victory and few calculations lead to defeat. No calculation at all is even worse. By looking at how much planning each side has done, Sun Tzu says, he can see who is likely to win or lose.

In a modern reading, many people use this chapter as a guide to business planning. They compare their company with a competitor on a few key points before acting. This is a later use of the text, not Sun Tzu's own purpose, which was the survival of a State in war.

## Chapter 2: Waging War

The title of this chapter sounds as if it will be about fighting. In fact, it is mostly about cost. The commentator Ts'ao Kung sums it up with one line: whoever wants to fight must first count the cost.

Sun Tzu begins with a picture of a large army. Imagine a thousand fast war chariots, a thousand heavy chariots, and a hundred thousand soldiers in armor. They need food for a march of a thousand li. A li is an old Chinese unit of distance, and Giles says that about 2.78 li make one mile. Sun Tzu lists the costs at home and at the front. There are payments for guests and visitors, small things like glue and paint, and large sums for chariots and armor. All together, the army costs a thousand ounces of silver every day. That, he says, is the price of raising an army of one hundred thousand men.

Giles adds some background in his notes. In early Chinese war, the chariot was the center of the army, and foot soldiers were grouped around it. According to the commentators, each fast chariot had 75 foot soldiers with it and each heavy chariot had 25. So the whole army was divided into a thousand groups, each with two chariots and a hundred men.

Then Sun Tzu explains why a long war is dangerous. If victory takes a long time, the soldiers' weapons become dull and their eagerness to fight fades. If you attack a walled town and stay there for a long time, your strength will be used up. If the campaign goes on and on, the State will not have enough resources to support it.

He describes what happens next. When your weapons are dull, your spirit is low, your strength is gone, and your money is spent, other rulers will rise up to take advantage of your weakness. At that point, no one, however wise, will be able to prevent the disaster that follows.

This leads to a famous and difficult line. Giles translates it this way: "though we have heard of stupid haste in war, cleverness has never been seen associated with long delays." Giles says the commentators do not explain this sentence well. Several of them say that even a foolish general can win through speed. The commentator Chang Yu goes further and says that stupid haste is better than clever slowness. But Giles thinks Sun Tzu says something more careful. Speed may sometimes be unwise, but slowness is always foolish, if only because a long war makes the nation poor. Giles also mentions the Roman general Fabius, who won fame by delaying, and he says it is still an open question whether that method would have worked in the long run.

Sun Tzu states the lesson plainly: no country has ever benefited from a long war. Only someone who fully understands the harm of war can understand the profitable way to carry it on. Giles explains that this means fighting quickly and ending the war fast.

Next come practical rules about supplies. A skilled soldier does not call up a second group of soldiers, and he does not load his supply wagons more than twice. Giles explains that the general should not waste time waiting for more troops or going back home for fresh supplies. Instead he should cross into enemy land without delay. Sun Tzu adds that the army should bring its equipment from home but find its food in enemy land. This is called foraging, which means collecting food and animal feed from the land where the army is. In this way, the army will have enough to eat.

Sun Tzu then describes the economic damage that war does at home. When the State's treasury is poor, the army must be supplied from far away. Sending supplies over long distances makes the people poor. Also, when an army is nearby, prices rise, and high prices drain away people's wealth. When their wealth is gone, the farmers suffer from heavy taxes and demands.

He even gives numbers. Homes are stripped bare, and three tenths of the people's income is lost. The government also loses four tenths of its total income on broken chariots, tired horses, armor, helmets, bows and arrows, spears and shields, protective covers, oxen, and heavy wagons. Giles notes that two commentators say the people actually lose seven tenths, but he does not think the text says this.

For this reason, a wise general makes a point of taking food from the enemy. One cartload of the enemy's food is worth twenty cartloads of your own. Giles explains why. Twenty cartloads would be eaten up on the long road just to bring one cartload to the front.

Sun Tzu then speaks about the soldiers' motivation. To kill the enemy, the men must be stirred to anger. To gain from defeating the enemy, they must receive rewards. The commentator Tu Mu adds that captured goods should be given as rewards. Then every soldier will want to fight for his own gain.

He gives an example from chariot fighting. When ten or more enemy chariots are captured, the men who took the first one should be rewarded. The army should replace the enemy's flags with its own and use the captured chariots together with its own. Captured soldiers should be treated kindly and kept. Sun Tzu calls this using the defeated enemy to increase your own strength.

The chapter ends with two short conclusions. In war, the great goal should be victory, not long campaigns. And the leader of armies holds the fate of the people in his hands. Whether the nation lives in peace or in danger depends on him.

In a modern reading, managers often quote this chapter to warn against long, costly projects that drain money and energy. This is a later application, not part of Sun Tzu's text, which is about the real economic cost of war for farmers and the State.

## Chapter 3: Attack by Stratagem

A stratagem is a clever plan or trick used to defeat an enemy. In this chapter Sun Tzu explains that the best victory is the one won with the least fighting.

He begins with a clear rule. In war, the best thing is to take the enemy's country whole and undamaged. To smash and destroy it is not as good. In the same way, it is better to capture a whole army than to destroy it. The same is true for smaller units, from a regiment down to a small company of soldiers.

Then he gives one of his most famous ideas. To fight and win every battle is not the highest skill. The highest skill is to break the enemy's resistance without fighting at all. Giles supports this with a modern example from his own time. At Sedan in 1870, the Prussian general Moltke forced a huge French army to surrender almost without bloodshed.

Sun Tzu then ranks four ways of dealing with an enemy, from best to worst. The best is to block the enemy's plans. Giles says the Chinese word means more than simple defense. It means an active response, and the commentator Ho Shih says that when the enemy plans an attack, we should attack first. The second best is to stop the enemy's forces from joining together, in other words to cut him off from his allies. Giles reminds readers that China at this time was divided into many states, so allies mattered a great deal. The third choice is to attack the enemy's army in the field. The worst of all is to attack walled cities.

Sun Tzu explains why sieges are so bad. A siege is a long attack on a city with walls. Preparing the large shields, moving shelters, and other machines takes three whole months. Building earth mounds up to the height of the walls takes three more months. The commentators describe these moving shelters as wooden boxes on four wheels, covered with raw animal skins, that carried men up to the walls.

Then the general loses patience. He cannot control his anger, so he sends his men to climb the walls like swarming ants. The commentator Ts'ao Kung uses this picture of ants climbing a wall. The result is that one third of the men are killed, and the town is still not taken. These, Sun Tzu says, are the terrible effects of a siege. Giles adds an example from his own day, the heavy Japanese losses at the siege of Port Arthur.

So the skilled leader defeats the enemy's troops without fighting. He takes their cities without a siege. He overthrows their kingdom without long campaigns. With his forces still whole, he competes for control of the empire, and he wins completely without losing a man. Sun Tzu calls this the method of attacking by stratagem.

Next he gives rules based on numbers. If your forces are ten times the enemy's, surround him. If they are five times larger, attack him. If they are twice as large, divide your army into two parts. Giles explains, using Ts'ao Kung and Chang Yu, what this last rule means. One part attacks the enemy in the front, and the other attacks from behind. If the enemy turns to face one part, the other part can crush him.

If the two sides are equal, you can offer battle. If you are a little weaker, you can avoid the enemy. If you are much weaker in every way, you can run away. Chang Yu adds a caution here. This only holds if all other things are equal, because a small difference in numbers can be outweighed by better energy and discipline. Sun Tzu concludes that a small force may fight bravely, but in the end a larger force will capture it.

Sun Tzu then talks about the general and the ruler. The general is the protective wall of the State. If this wall is complete, the State is strong. If it has gaps, the State is weak.

There are three ways a ruler can bring disaster on his army. First, he orders the army to advance or retreat without knowing that it cannot obey. Sun Tzu calls this "hobbling the army", which means tying its legs so it cannot move. The commentator Li Ch'uan compares it to tying the legs of a racehorse so it cannot run. Second, the ruler tries to manage an army the same way he manages a kingdom, without understanding army conditions. This makes the soldiers restless. Ts'ao Kung explains that military life and civil life are completely different, and an army cannot be handled gently in the same way. Third, the ruler gives officers jobs without choosing carefully, because he does not understand that war requires adapting to circumstances. This shakes the soldiers' confidence.

When an army is restless and does not trust its leaders, Sun Tzu warns, other princes will cause trouble. This brings chaos into the army and throws victory away.

Next Sun Tzu lists five essentials for victory. First, the one who knows when to fight and when not to fight will win. Second, the one who knows how to use both large and small forces will win. Third, the one whose army has the same spirit in every rank will win. Fourth, the one who is prepared and waits for an unprepared enemy will win. Fifth, the one who has military ability and is not interfered with by the ruler will win. Victory, Sun Tzu says, lies in knowing these five points. A commentator quoted by Tu Yu explains the last point simply: the ruler gives broad instructions, but the general decides when to fight.

The chapter ends with Sun Tzu's most famous saying. In Giles's words: "If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles." If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will lose every battle.

The commentator Li Ch'uan gives an example of not knowing the enemy. In the year 383, Fu Chien, a prince of Ch'in, marched against his enemy with a huge army. When people warned him that the enemy had very able leaders, he boasted that his soldiers could stop the Yangtze River just by throwing their whips into it. Soon after, his army was badly defeated at the Fei River, and he had to flee.

In a modern reading, the idea of winning without fighting is often applied to negotiation, competition, and diplomacy. "Know the enemy and know yourself" is widely quoted in business and sports. These are later uses of Sun Tzu's words, not his original subject.

## Chapter 4: Tactical Dispositions

Dispositions means the way an army is arranged and positioned. Tactics are the methods used in a battle. So this chapter is about putting your army in the right position before you fight. The commentator Tu Mu explains the idea simply. If you hide your dispositions, your real condition stays secret, and that leads to victory. If you show them, your condition becomes clear to the enemy, and that leads to defeat.

Sun Tzu begins with the good fighters of old times. First they made themselves safe from defeat. Then they waited for a chance to defeat the enemy. He explains the logic. Protecting yourself against defeat is in your own hands. But the chance to defeat the enemy is given by the enemy himself. Giles explains that this means the enemy must make a mistake.

So a good fighter can make himself safe from defeat, but he cannot be sure of defeating the enemy. Hence the saying that one may know how to win without being able to do it. Chang Yu says a general makes himself safe by hiding where his troops are, covering his tracks, and taking care at all times.

Sun Tzu then links this to defense and attack. Being safe from defeat means using defense. Being able to defeat the enemy means attacking. Standing on defense shows that your strength is not enough. Attacking shows that you have more than enough strength. Giles notes that the commentators read one of these lines a little differently, but the general meaning is the same.

He uses two strong pictures. The general who is skilled in defense hides in the deepest secret places of the earth. Giles explains that the Chinese literally says "under the ninth earth", which means total secrecy, so the enemy cannot know where he is. The general who is skilled in attack strikes from the highest heights of heaven, like a sudden bolt of lightning that leaves no time to prepare. In this way, one side has the power to protect itself, and the other wins a complete victory.

Next Sun Tzu says that true skill is often invisible. To see a victory only when everyone else can also see it is not the highest skill. And it is not the highest skill to fight, win, and have the whole empire say "Well done!" Ts'ao Kung explains that the point is to see the plant before it has started to grow, which means to see the result before the action begins.

The commentator Li Ch'uan tells the story of Han Hsin, who was about to attack a much larger army that was well protected in a city. He told his officers that they would destroy the enemy and then meet again for dinner. The officers did not take him seriously. But Han Hsin had already worked out a clever plan in his mind, and he won a crushing victory, just as he had foreseen.

Sun Tzu gives three simple comparisons. To lift an "autumn hair" is no sign of great strength. Giles explains that this means the very fine fur of a hare in autumn. To see the sun and moon is no sign of sharp eyes. To hear thunder is no sign of good ears. In the same way, winning an obvious victory proves nothing special.

Sun Tzu says the clever fighter not only wins, but wins easily. Because of this, his victories bring him no fame for wisdom and no praise for courage. Tu Mu explains why. His victories are won over problems that the world never saw, so no one thinks he is wise. And the enemy gives up before any blood is spilled, so no one praises his bravery.

He wins his battles by making no mistakes. Making no mistakes is what makes victory certain, because it means defeating an enemy who is already beaten. So the skilled fighter puts himself in a position where defeat is impossible, and he does not miss the moment to defeat the enemy. Giles adds that "position" here means not only the ground the army stands on, but all the preparations that keep the army safe.

This leads to a famous paradox, which is a statement that seems strange but is true. Giles words it like this: "in war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory." The commentator Ho Shih explains it. First make plans that will guarantee victory, and then lead the army to battle. If you rely only on force and not on planning, victory is no longer certain.

Sun Tzu then says that the excellent leader develops the Moral Law and holds firmly to method and discipline. These are two of the five factors from Chapter 1. In this way he controls success.

He then lists five steps of military method. First is Measurement. Second is Estimation of quantity. Third is Calculation. Fourth is Balancing of chances. Fifth is Victory. Each step comes from the one before it. Measurement comes from Earth, meaning the ground. Estimation comes from Measurement. Calculation comes from Estimation. Balancing of chances comes from Calculation. And Victory comes from Balancing of chances. Giles admits that these Chinese terms are hard to separate clearly. He suggests that the process starts with measuring the ground, moves to judging the enemy's strength and numbers, then to weighing your chances against his, and finally, if the balance is in your favor, to victory.

Sun Tzu ends with two images of power. A winning army facing a beaten army is like a heavy weight on a scale against a single grain. Giles explains that this shows the huge advantage a disciplined, confident army has over one that has lost its spirit after defeat. And the rush of a winning army is like water held back and then suddenly released into a valley a thousand fathoms deep. A fathom is an old measure of depth, about the height of a person.

In a modern reading, the idea of "first make yourself safe from defeat" is often applied to managing risk in business or investing. This is a later use, not Sun Tzu's own subject.

## Chapter 5: Energy

This chapter is about energy, meaning the force and momentum of an army. Sun Tzu explains how a leader can organize his troops, combine different kinds of attack, and release their power at the right moment.

He begins with organization. Controlling a large army works in the same way as controlling a few men. It is only a matter of dividing them into groups. Giles explains that this means breaking the army into regiments and companies, each with its own officer. The commentator Tu Mu tells a story about the general Han Hsin. The first Han Emperor once asked him how large an army the Emperor himself could lead. Han Hsin said no more than one hundred thousand men. The Emperor then asked how many Han Hsin could lead. Han Hsin answered, "the more the better."

In the same way, Sun Tzu says, fighting with a large army is no different from fighting with a small one. It is only a question of setting up signs and signals, such as flags and drums, so that everyone knows what to do.

Next Sun Tzu introduces one of his most important ideas. To make sure the whole army can stand against an enemy attack and not break, a general uses direct and indirect moves. In Chinese these are called cheng and ch'i. Cheng is the direct, normal, or expected force. Ch'i is the indirect, unusual, or surprising force.

Giles spends a long note on these two words, because they are hard to translate. He collects the commentators' views. Li Ch'uan says facing the enemy is cheng, and making a move to the side is ch'i. Ts'ao Kung says going straight into battle is direct, and appearing behind the enemy is indirect. Ho Shih adds that each can become the other. We should make the enemy think our open attack is a secret one, and the reverse. Ho Shih gives the example of Han Hsin, who seemed to march toward one city, but then suddenly sent a large force across the Yellow River in wooden tubs. The open march was cheng, and the surprise crossing was ch'i. Giles sums it up simply. Any move that the enemy is watching is cheng. Any move that surprises him or comes from an unexpected place is ch'i. And if the enemy sees a move that was meant to surprise him, it becomes cheng.

Sun Tzu then mentions the next chapter's subject. To make your army strike the enemy like a grindstone thrown against an egg, you need the science of weak points and strong. In all fighting, he says, the direct method may be used to start the battle, but indirect methods are needed to win.

He says that indirect tactics, used well, never run out. They are as endless as Heaven and Earth and as unending as rivers. Like the sun and moon, they end only to begin again. Like the four seasons, they pass away only to return.

Sun Tzu explains this with three beautiful comparisons. There are only five musical notes, yet they make more melodies than anyone can ever hear. There are only five basic colors, which he names as blue, yellow, red, white, and black, yet they make more shades than anyone can ever see. There are only five basic tastes, sour, sharp, salty, sweet, and bitter, yet they make more flavors than anyone can ever taste. In the same way, there are only two methods of attack in battle, the direct and the indirect. Yet together they create an endless series of moves.

The direct and the indirect lead into each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle, where you never reach an end. Sun Tzu asks who could ever use up all their possible combinations.

Next he describes force and timing. Troops attacking are like a rushing flood that can even roll stones along. The quality of decision is like the well timed dive of a falcon, which lets it strike and kill its prey. Giles admits that a key Chinese word here is hard to translate. He thinks it means the falcon's self control, which keeps it from diving until the right moment. Giles compares this to Nelson's ship at the battle of Trafalgar, which did not fire a single gun while it was under heavy fire, until it was close enough for its guns to do terrible damage.

So the good fighter is terrible in his attack and quick in his decision. Sun Tzu gives a clear image: energy is like bending a crossbow, and decision is like pulling the trigger. The power is stored first and then released at once.

Sun Tzu then talks about order inside apparent chaos. In the noise and confusion of battle, there may seem to be disorder, but there is no real disorder. The army's lines may look as if they have no head or tail, but it still cannot be beaten. The commentator Mei Yao-ch'en explains that the army's units and signals were all fixed in advance. So even when units separate, join, scatter, and gather, the army only looks disordered.

This leads to a deep point about pretending. To pretend disorder, you need perfect discipline. To pretend fear, you need courage. To pretend weakness, you need strength. Tu Mu explains it clearly. If you want to fake confusion to lure the enemy, you must first be very well disciplined. If you want to look timid to trap him, you must be very brave. If you want to look weak to make him overconfident, you must be very strong.

Sun Tzu adds how each is done. Hiding order behind disorder depends on how the army is divided into units. Hiding courage behind fear needs a store of hidden energy. Hiding strength behind weakness is done through the way the army is arranged.

The commentator Chang Yu gives an example of hidden strength. The first Han Emperor wanted to attack a northern people called the Hsiung-nu, and he sent spies to look at them. The Hsiung-nu knew this, so they hid their strong men and good horses and showed only weak soldiers and thin cattle. The spies all told the Emperor to attack. Only one adviser, Lou Ching, disagreed. He said that countries at war usually show off their strength, so showing only weakness must be a trick. The Emperor ignored him, fell into the trap, and was surrounded.

Sun Tzu says that a leader who is skilled at keeping the enemy moving shows false appearances, and the enemy acts according to them. He gives up something so that the enemy will grab it. He uses baits to keep the enemy marching, and then he waits for him with a group of chosen men.

Tu Mu tells a famous story here about Sun Pin, a descendant of Sun Tzu. In 341 BC, Sun Pin was fighting against the general P'ang Chuan, his personal enemy. Sun Pin's state had a reputation for cowardice. So as his army entered enemy land, he ordered his men to light one hundred thousand cooking fires on the first night, fifty thousand on the second, and only twenty thousand on the third. P'ang Chuan thought that more than half of Sun Pin's soldiers had run away, and he chased them fast. Sun Pin waited in a narrow pass that the enemy would reach after dark. He removed the bark from a tree and wrote on it, "Under this tree shall P'ang Chuan die." He hid archers nearby and told them to shoot if they saw a light. When P'ang Chuan arrived, he lit a light to read the words, and he was killed by arrows. Giles notes that this is Tu Mu's version. The older history says, less dramatically, that P'ang Chuan killed himself after his army was defeated.

The chapter ends with combined energy. The clever fighter looks at the power of the whole army and does not demand too much from individual men. Tu Mu adds that such a leader uses each man according to his abilities and does not expect perfection from those with less talent. This lets him choose the right men and use their combined energy.

When he uses combined energy, his soldiers become like rolling logs or stones. A log or stone stays still on flat ground but moves on a slope. If it is square, it stops. If it is round, it rolls down. Ts'ao Kung calls this using natural power. So the energy of good fighting men is like the force of a round stone rolling down a mountain thousands of feet high. Tu Mu says the main lesson of this chapter is the great value of fast movement and sudden attacks, through which small forces can achieve great results.

In a modern reading, people often compare direct and indirect force to a company's normal products and its surprising new ideas. This is a later interpretation, not something Sun Tzu wrote.

## Chapter 6: Weak Points and Strong

This chapter explains how to find the enemy's weak points and avoid his strong ones. The commentator Chang Yu says it follows the chapter on energy for a reason. A general first learns attack and defense, then direct and indirect methods, and only then can he see where the enemy is weak or strong.

Sun Tzu begins with timing. Whoever arrives first at the battlefield and waits for the enemy will be fresh. Whoever arrives second and must hurry into battle will be tired. So the clever fighter forces his will on the enemy and does not let the enemy force his will on him. Giles adds that a great soldier fights on his own terms or does not fight at all.

Sun Tzu explains how to control the enemy's movements. By offering him some advantage, you can make him come to you. By causing him damage, you can make it impossible for him to come near. Giles explains the two cases. In the first, you tempt him with a bait. In the second, you attack something important that he must stay to defend.

If the enemy is resting, you can disturb him. If he has plenty of food, you can starve him. If he is quietly in camp, you can force him to move. Appear at places the enemy must rush to defend. March quickly to places where he does not expect you.

An army can march long distances without trouble if it goes through land where the enemy is not. Ts'ao Kung sums this up well: come out of nowhere, strike weak points, avoid defended places, and attack where you are not expected.

You can be sure your attacks will succeed if you attack only places that are not defended. The commentator Wang Hsi explains what "undefended places" means. They are weak points, where the general lacks skill or the soldiers lack spirit, where walls are weak or care is poor, where help comes too late, where food is short, or where the defenders argue among themselves. In the same way, you can be sure your defense is safe if you hold only positions that cannot be attacked.

This leads to a key saying. A general is skilled in attack when his enemy does not know what to defend. He is skilled in defense when his enemy does not know what to attack. Giles calls this a saying that puts the whole art of war in a nutshell.

Sun Tzu then praises the art of subtlety and secrecy, almost like a prayer. Through it, he says, we learn to be invisible and silent to the enemy, and so we can hold the enemy's fate in our hands.

You can advance and be impossible to stop if you aim at the enemy's weak points. You can retreat safely if you move faster than the enemy. If you want to fight, you can force the enemy into battle even if he is behind high walls and deep ditches. You only need to attack some other place that he must go to save. Tu Mu explains that if the enemy has invaded, you can cut his supply roads and the roads home. If you are the invader, you can attack the enemy's ruler directly.

If you do not want to fight, you can stop the enemy from attacking you, even if your camp is only a line drawn on the ground. You only need to put something strange and puzzling in his way. Tu Mu gives a story about the famous strategist Chu-ko Liang. When he was about to be attacked by Ssu-ma I, he suddenly lowered his flags, stopped the drums, and opened the city gates. The enemy saw only a few men sweeping the ground. Ssu-ma I suspected a trap and led his army away. Giles says that what Sun Tzu recommends here is simply a well timed bluff, which means pretending to have more strength or a plan you do not really have.

Next comes the idea of concentration, which means bringing your forces together in one place. If we discover the enemy's arrangement but stay hidden ourselves, we can keep our forces together while the enemy must divide his. We will be one united body, and he will be broken into parts. So we will be many against his few. When we attack a weaker force with a stronger one, the enemy is in serious trouble.

The place where we plan to fight must stay secret. Then the enemy must prepare for attacks at many different points. His forces will be spread in many directions, and the number we meet at any one place will be small. If he strengthens his front, his rear becomes weak. If he strengthens his rear, his front becomes weak. The same is true for his left and his right. If he sends extra troops everywhere, he will be weak everywhere.

Sun Tzu sums this up. Weakness in numbers comes from having to prepare against possible attacks. Strength in numbers comes from forcing the enemy to prepare against us. Giles quotes a British military writer, Colonel Henderson, who said the highest generalship is to make the enemy spread out his army and then to attack each part in turn with a larger force.

If you know the place and time of the coming battle, you can bring your forces together from great distances to fight. But if you know neither the time nor the place, your left wing cannot help your right, your right cannot help your left, your front cannot help your rear, and your rear cannot help your front. This is even worse when parts of the army are many miles apart.

Then Sun Tzu makes one of his rare references to real events of his time. He says that even if the soldiers of Yüeh, Wu's enemy state, are more numerous, this will not help them win. Victory, he says, can be achieved. Giles adds a sad note. The long war between the two states ended in 473 BC with the total defeat of Wu by Yüeh, probably long after Sun Tzu's death. Chang Yu notices that this line seems to contradict Chapter 4, which said one may know how to win without being able to do it. He explains that Chapter 4 talked about an enemy who is fully prepared, while here Sun Tzu expects Yüeh not to know the time and place of the battle.

Even if the enemy has more men, Sun Tzu continues, you can stop him from fighting. Plan so that you discover his plans and their chances of success. Disturb him, and learn the pattern of when he acts and when he waits. Chang Yu explains that by watching whether the enemy reacts with joy or anger, we can learn his real plan. Force him to show himself so that you find his weak spots. Compare his army carefully with your own, so you know where you have too much strength and where you have too little.

The highest point of skill in arranging your forces is to hide them. If you hide your arrangement, you will be safe from the cleverest spies and the wisest planners. Giles explains that hiding here means not giving any sign of the plans in your mind.

Most people cannot understand how victory can be made out of the enemy's own tactics. Everyone can see the tactics by which a battle is won, but no one sees the strategy that created the victory. Giles explains that people see how a battle is won on the surface, but not the long series of plans before it.

So do not repeat the tactics that won you one victory. Let your methods follow the endless variety of circumstances. Wang Hsi says there is one root principle behind victory, but the tactics that lead to it are without number.

The chapter ends with Sun Tzu's famous image of water. Military tactics are like water. Water flows away from high places and hurries downward. In the same way, in war you avoid what is strong and strike what is weak. Water shapes its path to fit the ground. The soldier shapes his victory to fit the enemy he is facing. Just as water has no fixed shape, war has no fixed conditions. A leader who can change his tactics to fit his opponent and so win may be called a heaven-born captain, meaning a natural genius.

Sun Tzu finishes with examples from nature. The five elements, water, fire, wood, metal, and earth, are not always equally strong. The four seasons give way to each other. Days are short and long, and the moon grows and shrinks. Giles notes that the comparison is not perfect, because these changes in nature are regular, while war is not.

In a modern reading, "avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak" is often used in business to mean finding a market gap that competitors are not serving. This is a later use of the idea, not Sun Tzu's own subject.

## Chapter 7: Maneuvering

To maneuver means to move an army skillfully in order to gain an advantage over the enemy. In this chapter Sun Tzu explains why maneuvering is so hard, how to move troops, how to signal to them, and how to deal with the spirit of soldiers and the mind of a general.

He starts with the order of events. In war, the general receives his orders from the ruler. He gathers an army and brings his forces together. Then, before making camp, he must blend the different parts of the army into one harmonious whole. The commentator Chang Yu explains that this means building harmony and trust between high and low ranks before going to war.

After this comes maneuvering, and Sun Tzu says nothing is more difficult. The difficulty is to turn the indirect into the direct, and bad luck into gain. Giles calls this one of Sun Tzu's very short and puzzling sayings. Ts'ao Kung explains it like this. Make it look as if you are far away, then cover the distance quickly and arrive before your enemy. Tu Mu adds that you should trick the enemy so that he relaxes while you are moving at full speed. Giles mentions Hannibal and Napoleon, who both made surprising marches across the Alps.

So, Sun Tzu says, to take a long and winding road, to lure the enemy out of the way, and to arrive first even though you started after him, shows that you understand the art of deviation. Deviation here means taking an unexpected path.

Tu Mu tells a story that fits this idea well. In 270 BC, a town called O-yu was surrounded by an army of Ch'in. The king of Chao asked his general Chao She to rescue it. Chao She marched only a short distance from the capital and then stopped. For 28 days he built strong defenses, and he made sure enemy spies saw this and reported it. The Ch'in general was delighted and thought Chao She was afraid to come. But as soon as the spies left, Chao She made a fast march of two days and one night. He took a high hill before the enemy knew he was there. The Ch'in army was badly beaten and had to leave.

Sun Tzu then warns about the dangers of moving too fast. Maneuvering with a trained army brings advantage, but with an undisciplined crowd it is very dangerous. If you send the whole army with all its equipment to grab an advantage, you will probably arrive too late. If you send a fast group ahead without its baggage, it will lose its supplies.

He gives exact examples. Suppose you order your men to march day and night without stopping, covering twice the normal distance, a hundred li, to win an advantage. Then the leaders of all three divisions will be captured. The strong men will be in front, the tired ones will fall behind, and only one tenth of the army will arrive. If you march fifty li, you will lose the leader of your first division, and only half the army will arrive. If you march thirty li, two thirds will arrive. Tu Mu notes that a normal day's march was thirty li.

From this Sun Tzu concludes that an army without its baggage train is lost. Without food it is lost. Without supply bases it is lost.

Then he lists three kinds of knowledge a general needs. We cannot make alliances until we know the plans of our neighbors. We cannot lead an army on the march unless we know the land well, with its mountains and forests, dangerous holes and cliffs, marshes and swamps. And we cannot use the natural advantages of the land unless we use local guides. Giles notes that these three sayings are repeated in Chapter 11.

Sun Tzu returns to deception. In war, practice dissimulation, which means hiding your true intentions, and you will succeed. Move only if there is a real advantage to gain. Whether to bring your troops together or to divide them must depend on the situation.

Then comes a famous set of comparisons from nature. Let your speed be like the wind. Mei Yao-ch'en notes that wind is not only fast but invisible and leaves no tracks. Let your closeness be like a forest. When raiding and taking goods, be like fire. When holding your position, be unmovable like a mountain. Let your plans be dark and hidden like the night, and when you move, strike like a thunderbolt.

When you take goods from the countryside, Sun Tzu says, share them among your men. When you capture new land, divide it for the benefit of the soldiers. Giles explains that Sun Tzu wanted to reduce uncontrolled stealing by putting all captured goods together and sharing them fairly. Think carefully before you make a move. The one who has learned the art of deviation will win. This, Sun Tzu says, is the art of maneuvering.

Giles points out that the chapter could end here. But what follows is a long passage quoted from an older lost book called the Book of Army Management. No commentator doubts that it belongs here.

This old book says that on the battlefield the spoken voice does not carry far enough, so gongs and drums are used. Ordinary objects cannot be seen clearly enough, so banners and flags are used. Gongs, drums, banners, and flags focus the ears and eyes of the whole army on one point. Chang Yu says that when all eyes and ears focus on the same thing, even a million soldiers can move like one man.

When the army becomes one body like this, the brave cannot advance alone, and the cowardly cannot retreat alone. This is the art of handling large numbers of men. Tu Mu tells a hard story about the general Wu Ch'i. Before a battle, one very brave soldier ran out alone, killed two enemies, and came back with their heads. Wu Ch'i had him executed at once. When an officer protested that the man was a good soldier, Wu Ch'i said he believed that, but the man had acted without orders.

So in night fighting, use many signal fires and drums. In day fighting, use flags and banners. These guide the ears and eyes of your army.

Next Sun Tzu speaks about spirit, which we might call morale. A whole army can be robbed of its spirit. A commander can be robbed of his presence of mind, which means his ability to stay calm and think clearly.

Li Ch'uan and other commentators tell a story about spirit. The small state of Lu was attacked by the state of Ch'i. The Duke of Lu was ready to attack after the enemy's first drum roll, but his adviser Ts'ao Kuei said, "Not just yet." Only after the enemy's drums had sounded three times did he give the order to attack, and Lu won. Later he explained: the first drum roll creates courage, by the second it is already fading, and after the third it is gone. He attacked when the enemy's spirit was gone and his own was at its highest.

Sun Tzu explains how spirit changes during a day. A soldier's spirit is strongest in the morning. By noon it begins to weaken. In the evening, he only wants to go back to camp. So a clever general avoids the enemy when its spirit is strong and attacks when it is tired and thinking of returning. Sun Tzu calls this the art of studying moods.

He then names three more arts. To wait, disciplined and calm, for disorder and noise in the enemy's ranks is the art of keeping self-control. To be near the goal while the enemy is far away, to rest while the enemy struggles, and to be well fed while the enemy is hungry, is the art of saving your strength. Not to block an enemy whose flags are in perfect order, and not to attack an army that stands calm and confident, is the art of studying circumstances.

The chapter ends with a list of military rules. Do not advance uphill against the enemy, and do not fight him when he comes downhill. Do not chase an enemy who is only pretending to run away. Do not attack soldiers whose spirit is strong. Do not take a bait offered by the enemy. Do not block an army that is returning home. The commentators explain that soldiers who want to go home will fight to the death against anyone in their way.

When you surround an army, leave a way out. Tu Mu explains that the aim is not to let the enemy escape. The aim is to make him believe there is a road to safety, so that he does not fight with the courage of despair. Then, Tu Mu adds, you may crush him. Do not push a desperate enemy too hard. Ch'en Hao quotes a saying that birds and animals use their claws and teeth when they are trapped.

Ho Shih gives a story that shows why. In the year 945, a small Chinese force led by Fu Yen-ch'ing was surrounded by a much larger army in a dry, desert-like land. The men had so little water that they squeezed mud to suck out the moisture. In despair, they decided it was better to die fighting than to become prisoners. A strong sandstorm was blowing. One officer saw that in the dust the enemy could not see how few they were. The force made a sudden attack with its horsemen, defeated the enemy, and broke out to safety.

Sun Tzu closes simply: such is the art of warfare.

In a modern reading, the advice about morale, energy during the day, and not pushing an opponent into a corner is often applied to team management and negotiation. This is a later use, not part of Sun Tzu's text.

## Chapter 8: Variation of Tactics

This is a short chapter about flexibility. The Chinese title literally means "The Nine Variations." Giles explains that Sun Tzu does not actually list nine variations. Following the commentator Wang Hsi, Giles thinks that "nine" simply means a very large number. The message is that a general must change his methods as much as the situation requires. Giles also suggests that part of the chapter may have been lost, because it is unusually short.

The chapter begins with the same sentence as Chapter 7: the general receives his orders from the ruler, gathers his army, and brings his forces together. Giles thinks this sentence may have been added here just to give the chapter a beginning.

Sun Tzu then gives a set of rules for different kinds of ground. In difficult country, do not make camp. Where main roads cross, join with your allies. Do not stay long in dangerously isolated places. The commentators describe such places as land with no wells, no animals, no food plants, and no firewood, or land full of cliffs and deep valleys with no road. In places where you are closed in, you must use a clever plan. In a desperate position, you must fight.

Then comes a list of things a general should not always do. There are roads that must not be followed. Li Ch'uan says this especially means narrow passes where an ambush may be waiting. There are armies that must not be attacked. There are towns that must not be besieged. There are positions that must not be fought for. And there are commands of the ruler that must not be obeyed.

The commentators give examples. Ts'ao Kung tells how, when he invaded one region, he ignored a city that lay directly in his path and pushed on into the heart of the country. As a result he later captured fourteen important cities. Chang Yu says no town should be attacked if it cannot be held once taken, or if it will cause no trouble when left alone. Giles notes that the last rule, about not obeying the ruler, was hard for Chinese readers, who greatly respected authority. But in his view, even the ruler's wishes must sometimes give way to military necessity.

Sun Tzu explains why this matters. The general who fully understands the advantages of varying his tactics knows how to handle his troops. A general who does not understand them may know the land well, but he will not be able to use that knowledge in practice. And a student of war who cannot vary his plans will fail to use his men well, even if he knows what Sun Tzu calls the Five Advantages.

Sun Tzu does not explain these Five Advantages. The commentator Chia Lin suggests they are five normal good actions. Follow a short road. Attack an isolated army. Besiege a town in trouble. Storm a position that can be taken. Obey the ruler's commands. But sometimes the situation forbids even these. For example, a short road may hide an ambush, or an isolated army may be so desperate that it will fight to the death.

Next Sun Tzu gives one of his wisest ideas. In the plans of a wise leader, thoughts of advantage and disadvantage are always mixed together. If we balance our hopes of gain with awareness of risk, we can achieve the main part of our plans. And if, in the middle of trouble, we stay ready to seize an advantage, we can free ourselves from misfortune. Ts'ao Kung puts it simply: whether you are in a good position or a bad one, always keep the opposite in mind.

Tu Mu gives an example for the second idea. If you are surrounded and think only of escaping, your weak attitude will invite the enemy to chase and crush you. It is better to make a bold counterattack and use that advantage to break free.

Sun Tzu then explains how to wear down enemy leaders. Weaken them by causing them damage. Make trouble for them. Keep them constantly busy. Offer them false attractions and make them rush to any point you choose. The commentator Chia Lin lists many ways to cause such damage, such as drawing away the enemy's wisest advisers, sending traitors into his country, and creating conflict between the ruler and his ministers.

The next saying is one of the most practical in the book. The art of war teaches us not to rely on the hope that the enemy will not come, but on our own readiness to meet him. Do not rely on the chance that he will not attack, but on the fact that we have made our position impossible to attack.

The chapter ends with the five dangerous faults of a general. First is recklessness, which leads to destruction. Ts'ao Kung describes it as bravery without thought, which makes a man fight blindly like a mad bull. Such an enemy can be lured into a trap.

Second is cowardice, which leads to capture. The commentators describe this as a general who is too afraid to seize an advantage, or who only wants to come home alive. Giles notes that nothing is achieved in war without risk. The commentators give an example from the year 404. A rebel leader named Huan Hsuan kept a small boat tied to his warship so he could escape quickly if he lost. His soldiers saw this and lost their fighting spirit, and his much larger force was defeated.

Third is a hot temper that can be provoked by insults. Tu Mu tells of a leader who stayed behind his walls and refused to fight. His enemies knew he was quick to anger, so they kept attacking his walls until he came out in rage. They then pretended to run away, led him into a trap, and killed him.

Fourth is a sense of honor that is too sensitive to shame. Giles explains that Sun Tzu does not mean honor itself is bad. The danger is a general who is easily hurt by unfair criticism and reacts to it. Mei Yao-ch'en says that a person seeking real success should not care too much about public opinion.

Fifth is too much worry for his men, which brings him trouble and anxiety. Giles explains that Sun Tzu does not want generals to ignore their soldiers' welfare. The danger is giving up an important military advantage for the men's short-term comfort. In the end, the soldiers will suffer more from a defeat or a longer war.

Sun Tzu calls these five faults the constant sins of a general, and he says they ruin the conduct of war. When an army is destroyed and its leader is killed, the cause will surely be found among these five faults. He tells his reader to think about them deeply.

In a modern reading, these five faults are often used as a checklist for leaders in any field, for example a manager who reacts too quickly to criticism or who avoids every risk. This is a later application, not Sun Tzu's own purpose.

## Chapter 9: The Army on the March

Sun Tzu opens this chapter by stating its real subject: where to set up camp, and how to read signs of the enemy. Giles says this first sentence describes the chapter better than its title. The chapter has three parts. First, Sun Tzu gives advice for four kinds of land. Then he gives a long list of signs that reveal what the enemy is doing. Finally, he talks about how to treat soldiers.

He begins with mountains. Pass quickly over mountains, and stay near valleys. Giles explains that the idea is to avoid empty high land and stay close to water and grass. The commentator Chang Yu tells a story. A robber leader named Wu-tu Ch'iang hid in the hills. The general Ma Yuan did not try to force a battle. Instead he took all the places near water and animal feed. Soon the robbers had no food and had to surrender. Sun Tzu adds that you should camp in high places, facing the sun, and you should not climb up hills to fight. That is mountain warfare.

Next, rivers. After you cross a river, move far away from it. When an invading army crosses a river toward you, do not meet it in the middle of the water. It is best to let half the army cross and then attack. The commentator Li Ch'uan points to a famous battle won by Han Hsin at the Wei River. Han Hsin secretly built a dam upstream with about ten thousand bags of sand. He crossed with half his army, attacked, and then pretended to fail and ran back. The enemy general, Lung Chu, followed him into the river. Han Hsin's men then cut open the sandbags. A great wave of water stopped most of the enemy army from crossing, and Han Hsin destroyed the part that was cut off.

Sun Tzu adds more river rules. If you want to fight, do not wait for the invader right next to the river he must cross, or he may not cross at all. Keep your boats higher up the river than the enemy, and face the sun. Do not move upstream to meet the enemy. Tu Mu explains that an enemy upstream could open the water gates and sweep you away in a flood.

Third, salt marshes. A marsh is low, wet land. When crossing salt marshes, your only goal should be to get across quickly. Giles explains that marshes have no fresh water, poor grass, and are low and open to attack. If you are forced to fight in one, keep water and grass near you and put your back to a group of trees.

Fourth, flat, dry country. Here, take a position that is easy to reach, with higher ground on your right and behind you. Then danger will be in front and safety behind.

Sun Tzu says that these four kinds of knowledge, about mountains, rivers, marshes, and plains, helped the legendary Yellow Emperor defeat four other rulers. Giles notes that commentators were unsure about this story, because older histories do not clearly mention four such victories.

Then Sun Tzu gives general advice about camps. All armies prefer high ground to low ground, and sunny places to dark ones. If you take care of your men and camp on hard, dry ground, the army will be free from disease, and this means victory. When you come to a hill or a river bank, take the sunny side, with the slope behind you on your right. This both helps your soldiers and uses the natural advantages of the ground. If heavy rain has made a river high and foamy, wait until it goes down before crossing.

Some places must be left as quickly as possible and never approached. Sun Tzu names steep cliffs with rushing water between them, deep natural hollows, closed-in places that are easy to enter but hard to leave, thick bushes where spears cannot be used, deep mud where chariots and horses cannot pass, and narrow cracks between cliffs. While you stay away from such places, try to make the enemy go near them. While you face them, let the enemy have them at his back.

If there are hills, ponds with tall water grass, hollows full of reeds, or thick woods near your camp, search them carefully. Enemy soldiers hiding in ambush, or spies, may be there.

Now comes the long list of signs. Giles says it is so good that it could almost belong in a modern scouting handbook. An ambush, by the way, is a surprise attack by hidden soldiers.

If the enemy is close but stays quiet, he trusts the natural strength of his position. If he stays far away but tries to provoke a battle, he wants you to come forward. If his camp is easy to reach, he is offering a bait.

Movement among the trees of a forest means the enemy is coming. Chang Yu explains that the trees are being cut to clear a path. Many screens of grass in thick vegetation mean the enemy wants to make you suspicious. Ts'ao Kung explains that a retreating enemy builds them so you will fear an ambush and not follow. Birds suddenly rising in flight are a sign of an ambush below. Frightened animals mean a sudden attack is coming.

Dust also tells a story. A high, narrow column of dust means chariots are coming. Low dust spread over a wide area means foot soldiers. Dust going in different directions means groups are collecting firewood. A few small clouds of dust moving back and forth mean the army is setting up camp.

Words and actions are also signs. Humble words together with more preparations mean the enemy is about to attack. Tu Mu explains that the enemy wants you to become careless.

Chang Yu tells a long story about the general T'ien Tan, who defended the city of Chi-mo. He let the enemy hear his "fears" that they might cut off prisoners' noses or dig up the town's ancestral graves. The enemy did both, and this made the people of the city furious and eager to fight. Then T'ien Tan offered to surrender and sent the enemy general gifts of silver, so the enemy became careless. At night he released a thousand oxen with blades tied to their horns and burning reeds tied to their tails. Five thousand chosen soldiers followed them, and the people in the city made a huge noise with drums and bronze pots. The enemy army fled in terror, and its general was killed.

Sun Tzu continues. Violent words and pushing forward as if to attack are signs that the enemy will retreat. When light chariots come out first and take places on the sides, the enemy is forming for battle. Offers of peace without a firm sworn agreement mean a plot. Much running about, with soldiers forming lines, means the decisive moment has come. When some advance and some retreat, it is a lure.

The soldiers' bodies also show their condition. When they lean on their spears, they are weak from hunger. If the men sent to fetch water drink first, the army is thirsty. Tu Mu remarks that you can know the condition of a whole army from the behavior of a single man. If the enemy sees an advantage and does not try to take it, the soldiers are exhausted.

If birds gather on a spot, no one is there. Noise at night shows nervousness. Disorder in the camp shows the general's authority is weak. If flags are moved around, rebellion is starting. If the officers are angry, the men are tired.

When an army feeds its horses with grain and kills its cattle for food, and the men do not hang their cooking pots over the fires, they will not return to their tents. They are determined to fight to the death. Men whispering together in small groups shows discontent among the soldiers. Too many rewards mean the enemy is running out of resources. Too many punishments mean he is in serious trouble. To start with loud threats and then become afraid of the enemy's numbers shows a great lack of intelligence. When messengers come with friendly words, the enemy wants a truce, which is a pause in fighting. If enemy troops come up angrily and stand facing you for a long time without fighting or leaving, you must be very watchful. Ts'ao Kung says this may be a trick to gain time for a surprise attack from the side.

Sun Tzu then comments on numbers. If your army is no larger than the enemy's, that is enough. It only means you cannot make a direct attack. You can bring all your strength together, watch the enemy closely, and gain more men. Giles admits this sentence is unclear. Chang Yu explains it as finding extra recruits among the people who follow the camp, but not borrowing foreign soldiers.

He who does not think ahead and does not take his enemies seriously will surely be captured by them. Ch'en Hao quotes an old saying: if bees and scorpions carry poison, how much more does a hostile state. Even a small enemy deserves respect.

The chapter ends with how to treat soldiers. If soldiers are punished before they feel loyal to you, they will not obey, and if they do not obey, they are almost useless. But if they feel loyal and punishments are not carried out, they are still useless. So soldiers must first be treated with kindness, but kept under control with iron discipline. This is a sure road to victory. If orders are always enforced in training, the army will be well disciplined. If a general shows trust in his men but always insists that his orders are obeyed, both sides gain. Chang Yu explains that the general trusts the men, and the men trust the general.

In a modern reading, the many "signs" in this chapter are often compared to reading the behavior of competitors or the mood of a team. This is a later use of the text, not Sun Tzu's purpose.

## Chapter 10: Terrain

Terrain means the shape and type of land, such as hills, passes, and open plains. Giles notes that only the first third of this chapter is really about terrain. The middle part is about six disasters caused by a general's mistakes, and the last part is a set of separate but interesting remarks.

Sun Tzu begins by naming six kinds of terrain. They are accessible ground, entangling ground, temporizing ground, narrow passes, steep heights, and positions far away from the enemy. Giles comments that this list mixes different kinds of ideas and is not very logical. Still, Sun Tzu explains each one.

Accessible ground is land that both sides can cross freely. Here you should get to the high, sunny places before the enemy, and you should guard your supply line carefully. Then you can fight with an advantage. Tu Yu explains that this means not letting the enemy cut your connection to your supplies. Giles adds that he wishes Sun Tzu had said more on this subject, since Napoleon believed that the secret of war lies in communications.

Entangling ground is land that you can leave but that is hard to return to. From here, if the enemy is not prepared, you can go out and defeat him. But if the enemy is ready for you and you fail, you cannot return, and disaster follows.

Temporizing ground is land where neither side gains by making the first move. To temporize means to wait and delay. Tu Mu says each side finds it hard to move, so there is a deadlock. In this position, even if the enemy offers an attractive bait, you should not go out. Instead, pull back and tempt the enemy to come out. When part of his army has come out, you can attack with advantage.

Narrow passes are the fourth kind. If you reach them first, fill them with a strong force and wait for the enemy. Tu Yu explains that then you hold the initiative, meaning you decide what happens. If the enemy reaches a pass first, do not follow him in if it is strongly held. Go after him only if it is weakly held.

Steep heights are the fifth kind. If you get there first, take the high, sunny places and wait for the enemy to come up. If the enemy is there first, do not follow him. Pull back and try to draw him away.

Chang Yu tells a story about high ground. A general named P'ei Hsing-chien had set up a fully protected camp for the night. Then he suddenly ordered the army to move to a nearby hill. His officers complained loudly about the extra work. That same night a terrible storm came, and the old camp was flooded more than twelve feet deep. The officers were amazed and asked how he knew. He told them that from now on they should obey orders without asking unnecessary questions. Chang Yu concludes that high places are good not only for fighting but also for safety from floods.

The sixth kind is a position far from the enemy. If the two armies are equally strong, it is hard to provoke a battle, and fighting will be to your disadvantage. Tu Yu explains that after a long march you would be tired while the enemy would be fresh.

Sun Tzu says these six are the principles connected with Earth, and a general in a responsible position must study them carefully.

Next he lists six calamities, or disasters, that can strike an army. He stresses that they do not come from nature. They come from faults for which the general is responsible. They are flight, insubordination, collapse, ruin, disorganization, and rout.

Flight happens when, other things being equal, a force is thrown against an enemy ten times its size. The smaller force will run away.

Insubordination means refusing to obey. It happens when the ordinary soldiers are too strong and their officers too weak. Tu Mu tells the sad story of T'ien Pu, a commander in the year 821. His soldiers had no respect for him. They rode around the camp on donkeys by the thousands, and he could not stop them. When he finally tried to fight, his troops ran away in every direction, and he took his own life.

Collapse is the opposite case. It happens when the officers are too strong and the soldiers too weak. Ts'ao Kung explains that the officers want to push forward, but the weak soldiers suddenly give way.

Ruin happens when senior officers are angry and disobedient. When they meet the enemy, they start fighting on their own out of resentment, before the commander can judge whether he is ready to fight.

Disorganization happens when the general is weak and has no authority, when his orders are unclear, when officers and men have no fixed duties, and when the lines are formed in a careless, random way. Giles quotes the British general Baden-Powell, who said the secret of getting good work from trained men lies in the clearness of their instructions.

Rout means a total, chaotic defeat. It happens when a general cannot judge the enemy's strength. He lets a smaller force fight a larger one, or throws a weak group against a strong one, and he does not put his best soldiers in the front line.

Sun Tzu says these are six ways of inviting defeat, and a responsible general must study them carefully.

He then returns to the ground. The natural shape of the land is the soldier's best ally. But the real test of a great general is his power to judge the enemy, to control the forces that lead to victory, and to calculate difficulties, dangers, and distances wisely. The one who knows these things and uses them in battle will win. The one who does not will surely lose.

Next comes a bold statement about the ruler. If fighting is sure to bring victory, you must fight, even if the ruler forbids it. If fighting will not bring victory, you must not fight, even if the ruler orders it. Chang Yu quotes a saying that the emperor's orders do not pass through the walls of a camp.

Sun Tzu then describes the ideal general. He advances without wanting fame, and he retreats without fearing shame. His only thought is to protect his country and serve his ruler well. Such a man is the jewel of the kingdom. Giles adds that Wellington, he believes, said the hardest thing for a soldier is to retreat.

He then speaks about care for soldiers. Treat your soldiers as your children, and they will follow you into the deepest valleys. Look on them as your own beloved sons, and they will stand by you even to death.

Tu Mu tells a moving story about the general Wu Ch'i. He wore the same clothes and ate the same food as his lowest soldiers. He did not ride a horse and did not sleep on a mat. Once a soldier had an infected sore, and Wu Ch'i himself sucked the poison out. When the soldier's mother heard this, she began to cry. People asked her why, since this was a great honor. She said that years before, Wu Ch'i had done the same for her husband, who then never left him and finally died in battle. Now she knew her son would also die fighting for him.

But Sun Tzu gives a warning. If you are kind but cannot make your authority felt, soft-hearted but unable to enforce your orders, and unable to stop disorder, then your soldiers are like spoiled children. They are useless for any practical purpose. Tu Mu gives an example of strict discipline. In the year 219, the general Lu Meng ordered his army not to take anything from the people of a town. One officer, who came from the same home town as Lu Meng, took a bamboo hat from a local person to keep the rain off his helmet. Lu Meng had him executed, crying as he gave the order. After that, the commentator says, no one in the army even picked up things dropped on the road.

Near the end, Sun Tzu explains three kinds of half knowledge. If we know our men are ready to attack, but we do not know that the enemy cannot be attacked, we are only halfway to victory. If we know the enemy can be attacked, but we do not know our own men are not ready, we are only halfway. If we know both, but we do not know that the ground makes fighting impossible, we are still only halfway.

So the experienced soldier, once he starts moving, is never confused. Once he leaves camp, he is never at a loss. The chapter ends with a longer version of the famous saying from Chapter 3: "If you know the enemy and know yourself, your victory will not stand in doubt; if you know Heaven and know Earth, you may make your victory complete." Here Heaven means weather and seasons, and Earth means the ground.

In a modern reading, the six calamities are often compared to problems in organizations, such as weak managers, angry senior staff, or unclear instructions. This is a later use of the text, not Sun Tzu's own subject.

## Chapter 11: The Nine Situations

This is the longest chapter in the book. It is about nine kinds of ground, or nine situations an army can find itself in, especially when it invades another country. Sun Tzu explains what each situation means and what a general should do in each one. Then he talks at length about the minds of soldiers: how danger makes them brave, how a general can unite them, and why he must keep his plans secret, even from his own men.

Sun Tzu begins by naming the nine kinds of ground. They are dispersive ground, facile ground, contentious ground, open ground, ground of intersecting highways, serious ground, difficult ground, hemmed-in ground, and desperate ground. He then defines each one.

Dispersive ground is when a leader is fighting in his own territory. To disperse means to scatter. Giles explains that soldiers near their homes want to see their wives and children, so they may use a battle as a chance to run away in all directions.

Facile ground is when the army has gone into enemy land, but not very far. Facile means easy. The commentators say it is called this because it is easy to retreat from. Tu Mu adds that once the army has crossed the border, the general should burn his boats and bridges, to show everyone that there is no thought of going home.

Contentious ground is ground that gives a great advantage to whichever side holds it. It is ground to be fought over. Ts'ao Kung says it is ground where a few weak soldiers can defeat many strong ones, such as the entrance to a mountain pass. Giles compares it to Thermopylae, the narrow pass where a small Greek force held back a huge invading army.

Open ground is land where both sides can move freely. Ts'ao Kung describes it as land covered with a network of roads, like a chessboard.

Ground of intersecting highways is land that is the key to three neighboring states. Whoever takes it first can control most of the empire, because he can force most of the states to become his allies. Meng Shih gives the example of the small state of Cheng, which had three large states around it.

Serious ground is when the army has gone deep into enemy country and has left many walled cities behind it. Wang Hsi says the name comes from the fact that the army's situation is now serious.

Difficult ground is land that is hard to cross, such as mountain forests, rough slopes, marshes, and wet lowlands.

Hemmed-in ground is land that you reach through narrow valleys and can only leave by winding paths. Here a small enemy force could crush a large army.

Desperate ground is ground where the army can only be saved from destruction by fighting at once. Ts'ao Kung pictures it as a high mountain in front, a large river behind, no way forward, and no way back. Ch'en Hao says that being on desperate ground is like sitting in a leaking boat or hiding in a burning house.

Next Sun Tzu gives a short rule for each kind of ground. On dispersive ground, do not fight. On facile ground, do not stop. On contentious ground, do not attack. Ts'ao Kung explains that you should try to take the position first, rather than attacking an enemy who already holds it. On open ground, do not try to block the enemy's way. On ground of intersecting highways, join with your allies. On serious ground, gather supplies by taking them from the land. On difficult ground, keep marching. On hemmed-in ground, use a clever trick. On desperate ground, fight.

The commentators add examples. For hemmed-in ground, Giles tells how Hannibal was trapped in the mountains by the Roman general Fabius. At night Hannibal tied burning bundles of sticks to the horns of about two thousand oxen and drove them toward the passes. The Romans were frightened by the strange moving lights and left their positions, and Hannibal's army escaped. For desperate ground, Chia Lin explains the rule simply. If you fight with all your strength, there is a chance of life. If you stay in your corner, death is certain.

Sun Tzu then describes what the skilled leaders of old times did to the enemy. They cut off his front from his rear. They stopped his large and small units from working together. They stopped good troops from rescuing weak ones, and officers from gathering their men. When the enemy's men were scattered, they kept them from coming together. Even when the enemy was united, they kept him in disorder. When it was useful, they moved forward. When it was not, they stayed still.

He imagines a question. How do you deal with a large enemy army that is in good order and about to attack? Sun Tzu answers: "Begin by seizing something which your opponent holds dear; then he will be amenable to your will." In other words, take something he cares about, and he will have to do what you want. Tu Mu suggests three things an enemy usually cares about: taking good positions, destroying your farmland, and protecting his own supply lines.

Then comes a key principle: "Rapidity is the essence of war." Sun Tzu adds that you should take advantage of the enemy's lack of readiness, travel by unexpected routes, and attack places that are not guarded.

The commentator Ho Shih gives two stories about speed. In the year 227, an official named Meng Ta planned to rebel. He wrote to an ally that the general Ssu-ma I was far away and that it would take a month before any army came. But Ssu-ma I marched at once and reached Meng Ta's city in eight days. Meng Ta wrote in shock about this "miraculous rapidity", and two weeks later his city had fallen. In the second story, in the year 621, the general Li Ching sailed down the flooded Yangtze River in autumn, when his enemy thought no army would dare travel. His enemy was completely unprepared and had to surrender.

Sun Tzu now turns to the principles for an invading army. The deeper you go into a country, the more united your troops become, and the defenders will not be able to beat you. Raid rich farmland to feed your army. Take good care of your men and do not overwork them. Gather your energy and save your strength. Keep your army moving, and make plans that no one can understand.

Ch'en Hao tells the story of the general Wang Chien, who invaded the state of Ch'u in 224 BC. He was not sure of his soldiers' spirit, so he refused every challenge to fight and stayed behind his walls. He spent his time winning his men's loyalty. He made sure they ate well, shared his own meals with them, and gave them places to bathe. After some time, he asked what the men were doing for fun. He heard that they were competing in throwing weights and jumping. Then he knew they were ready. By this time the Ch'u army had given up and marched away. Wang Chien followed them and won a great victory.

Sun Tzu continues with one of his central ideas about human nature. Put your soldiers in positions where there is no escape, and they will choose death over running away. If they are ready to face death, there is nothing they cannot do. Officers and men alike will give their very best. Soldiers in desperate situations lose their fear. If there is no place to escape to, they will stand firm. If they are deep inside enemy country, they will hold together. If there is no other way, they will fight hard.

So, without being organized, the soldiers will always be alert. Without being asked, they will do what you want. Without rules, they will be loyal. Without orders, they can be trusted.

Sun Tzu also says to forbid fortune-telling and remove superstitious doubts. Then, until death comes, there is no disaster to fear. Tu Mu quotes an older writer who said that magic spells and predicting the army's fortune should be forbidden, so the soldiers' minds will not be disturbed.

He then makes a careful point about soldiers' feelings. If our soldiers are not loaded with money, it is not because they dislike wealth. If their lives are not long, it is not because they do not want to live long. Chang Yu explains that all people naturally want wealth and long life. If soldiers throw away their valuables and give up their lives, it is only because they have no choice. On the day they are ordered into battle, Sun Tzu says, soldiers may cry, some wetting their clothes with tears as they sit, others with tears running down their faces as they lie down. Ts'ao Kung explains that this is not fear, but the firm decision to do or die. But once they are trapped with no way out, they will show the courage of Chu or Kuei. Giles explains that these were two famous brave men from Chinese history, each known for one bold act done at great risk to his life.

Now comes one of the most famous images in the book. A skilled tactician is like the shuai-jan, a snake found in the Ch'ang mountains. If you strike its head, its tail attacks you. If you strike its tail, its head attacks you. If you strike its middle, both head and tail attack you. Sun Tzu asks whether an army can be made to act like this snake, with every part helping the others. He answers yes. He gives an example from his own time: the men of Wu and the men of Yüeh are enemies, but if they are crossing a river in the same boat and a storm hits, they will help each other just as the left hand helps the right. Giles notes that this is one of the passages he uses to date the book, because it shows Wu and Yüeh as enemies.

So, Sun Tzu says, it is not enough to stop an army from running away by tying up the horses and burying the chariot wheels in the ground. Giles explains that such mechanical tricks are not enough. The men need firm purpose and a spirit of working together. The principle for managing an army is to set one standard of courage that everyone must reach. How to get the best out of both strong and weak soldiers is a question of using the ground well. Mei Yao-ch'en explains that weaker troops in a strong position can hold out as long as better troops in an exposed one. In this way, the skilled general leads his whole army as easily as if he were leading one man by the hand.

Next Sun Tzu discusses the general's character and his secrecy. A general must be calm, so that he keeps secrets, and fair and honest, so that he keeps order. He must be able to confuse his own officers and men with false reports and appearances and keep them completely in the dark. Ts'ao Kung gives a short saying: the troops must not share your plans at the start; they may only celebrate with you when things turn out well.

Giles knows this may sound strange, so he gives examples. He mentions the American general Stonewall Jackson, who hid his plans even from his closest officers. He also tells, from a Chinese history, how the general Pan Ch'ao in the year 88 told his officers he would divide and retreat. He secretly let prisoners escape so that the enemy would hear this. The enemy sent large forces away to block his retreat. Then Pan Ch'ao gathered his troops and attacked at dawn, and the enemy army fled.

Sun Tzu continues. By changing his arrangements and plans, the general keeps the enemy uncertain. By moving his camp and taking roundabout routes, he stops the enemy from guessing his purpose. Chang Yu quotes another work saying that the rule "war is based on deception" applies not only to the enemy but even to one's own soldiers.

At the critical moment, the leader acts like someone who has climbed up high and then kicks away the ladder behind him. He takes his men deep into enemy land before he shows his hand. He burns his boats and breaks his cooking pots. Like a shepherd driving sheep, he drives his men this way and that, and no one knows where they are going. To gather the army and bring it into danger, Sun Tzu says, is the business of the general. Giles explains that he means that after the army is gathered, there should be no delay in striking at the enemy's heart.

Sun Tzu says a general must study three things: the different measures for the nine kinds of ground, when to attack and when to defend, and the basic laws of human nature.

He then goes over the nine kinds of ground a second time, with some new definitions. When invading, going deep brings unity, and going only a short way brings scattering. When you leave your country and cross a neighbor's land, you are on what he calls critical ground. Giles notes that this is a tenth kind of ground, which is mentioned in Chapter 8 but is not part of the main list. When roads lead in all four directions, it is ground of intersecting highways. Going deep is serious ground, and going a little way is facile ground. When enemy strongholds are behind you and narrow passes are in front, it is hemmed-in ground. When there is no place of escape at all, it is desperate ground.

Then he gives a second list of what he himself would do on each kind of ground. On dispersive ground, he would give his men unity of purpose. On facile ground, he would keep all parts of the army closely connected. On contentious ground, he would hurry up his rear troops. On open ground, he would watch his defenses closely. On ground of intersecting highways, he would strengthen his alliances. On serious ground, he would make sure supplies keep coming. On difficult ground, he would keep pushing along the road. On hemmed-in ground, he would block every way of retreat. On desperate ground, he would tell his soldiers that there is no hope of saving their lives.

The commentary gives an example of blocking a retreat. In the year 532, Kao Huan was surrounded by a large army, but there were gaps in the enemy lines. Instead of escaping, he blocked the gaps himself with oxen and donkeys tied together. When his soldiers saw that they must win or die, their spirit rose so high that they broke through the enemy. For desperate ground, Tu Yu says to burn the baggage, throw away supplies, fill in the wells, and destroy the cooking stoves, so that the men understand they must fight to the death.

Giles adds an important note here about the state of the text. He points out that Sun Tzu's ideas about different kinds of ground are spread in a messy way across Chapters 8, 9, 10, and 11. Chapter 8 promises nine variations but gives only five, and it is very short. Chapter 11 defines some grounds twice and gives two different lists of what to do. Giles concludes that the book has not come down to us in exactly the form its author wrote it. Chapter 8 seems incomplete, and Chapter 11 seems to contain material that was added later or belongs elsewhere.

Sun Tzu explains the reason behind all this. It is a soldier's nature to resist stubbornly when surrounded, to fight hard when he has no other choice, and to obey quickly when he is in danger.

He then repeats three sayings from Chapter 7. You cannot make alliances with neighboring rulers until you know their plans. You cannot lead an army on the march unless you know the land. And you cannot use the land's natural advantages without local guides. Giles adds a warning that guides can also make mistakes. He tells how Hannibal's guide misunderstood the name of a town because of Hannibal's accent and led the army to the wrong place.

Sun Tzu then speaks about a powerful ruler at war. To be ignorant of any of these principles is not fitting for a warlike prince. When such a prince attacks a powerful state, he shows his skill by stopping the enemy from gathering its forces. He frightens his opponents, and their allies are prevented from joining against him. So he does not try to make alliances with everyone, and he does not help other states grow strong. He follows his own secret plans and keeps his enemies in fear. In this way he can capture their cities and overthrow their kingdoms. Giles notes that the commentators read this passage in different ways, and that Chang Yu thinks Sun Tzu may actually be criticizing this cold and proud attitude.

Next come rules about orders and rewards. Give rewards without following fixed rules, and give orders without following earlier arrangements. Then you can handle a whole army as if it were one man. Giles explains that this means not revealing plans in advance, and being ready to change them at the last moment. Give your soldiers tasks to do, but never tell them your plan. When things look good, show them. When things look bad, tell them nothing.

Then Sun Tzu repeats his bold idea about danger. Put your army in deadly danger, and it will survive. Plunge it into desperate trouble, and it will come out safely. For it is exactly when a force is in harm's way that it can strike a winning blow.

Giles connects this with a famous battle fought by Han Hsin in 204 BC. He placed ten thousand men with their backs to a river, which the enemy laughed at as a beginner's mistake. Earlier, he had secretly sent two thousand horsemen, each carrying a red flag, to hide near the enemy camp. Han Hsin attacked, then pretended to run away toward the river. The enemy left its camp to chase him. Han Hsin's men, with the river behind them, had no way to escape, so they fought with total desperation. Meanwhile the hidden horsemen rode into the empty enemy camp and replaced all its flags with red ones. When the enemy soldiers looked back and saw the red flags, they thought their camp was lost and fled in panic. After the battle, his officers asked why he had broken the rule against having a river behind him. Han Hsin quoted Sun Tzu's words about placing an army in deadly danger so that it survives.

Sun Tzu adds a few final rules. Success in war comes from carefully going along with the enemy's purpose. Chang Yu explains that if the enemy wants to advance, you encourage him, and if he wants to retreat, you let him, so that he becomes careless before you strike. By staying close to the enemy's side, you will in the end kill his commander. Sun Tzu calls this the ability to achieve something through pure cleverness.

On the day you take command, close the border passes, destroy the official passes used for travel, and stop all messengers. Giles explains that these official passes were wooden or bamboo tablets that allowed travelers through the gates. Be strict and firm in the council room so that you control the situation. If the enemy leaves a door open, rush in. Act before your opponent by seizing what he values, and cleverly arrange the time when he arrives at the battlefield. Follow the rules, and adjust to the enemy until you can fight a decisive battle.

The chapter ends with a memorable picture. At first, be shy like a young girl, until the enemy gives you an opening. Then be as fast as a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to stop you. Giles notes that a hare is known for being timid, so the comparison seems odd, but Sun Tzu was thinking only of its speed.

In a modern reading, the idea of "burning your boats" is often used for a person or team that removes all ways back so that they must succeed. This is a later use of Sun Tzu's image. His own subject was soldiers deep in enemy land, and some of his advice, such as deceiving one's own men, belongs clearly to ancient warfare.

## Chapter 12: The Attack by Fire

Giles notes that a little more than half of this chapter is about using fire in war. After that, Sun Tzu moves on to other subjects. The chapter ends with some of the most thoughtful and peaceful lines in the whole book.

Sun Tzu begins by listing five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp. The second is to burn supplies, such as food, fuel, and animal feed. The third is to burn the baggage trains, meaning the wagons that carry the army's equipment. The fourth is to burn arsenals and storehouses, where weapons, money, and clothing are kept. The fifth is to throw falling fire among the enemy. The commentator Tu Yu explains that this means lighting the tips of arrows and shooting them into the enemy's camp with strong crossbows.

The commentators give examples. For burning a camp, the commentary tells the story of Pan Ch'ao, a Chinese envoy who was visiting a foreign king. Suddenly a messenger from the Hsiung-nu, the enemies of the Chinese, arrived at the same court, and Pan Ch'ao's small group was in great danger. Pan Ch'ao decided to act at once. One night, when a strong wind was blowing, he and his small band set fire to the Hsiung-nu camp from the side the wind was blowing from. Some of his men beat drums and shouted behind the camp, while others waited at the gate with bows. The enemy rushed out in panic and was destroyed. For burning supplies, Tu Mu mentions an adviser who told an emperor to make regular raids and burn a rebellious region's stores of grain, a plan that in the end worked.

Sun Tzu then explains what a fire attack needs. You must have the means, and the materials for making fire should always be ready. Tu Mu lists such materials as dry plants, reeds, small branches, straw, grease, and oil. Chia Lin adds that you must also use wind and dry weather.

There is a right season and there are special days for fire attacks. The right season is when the weather is very dry. The special days are when the moon is in four particular groups of stars, which Sun Tzu names the Sieve, the Wall, the Wing, and the Cross-bar. He says these are all days when the wind rises.

Next, Sun Tzu explains five possible developments in a fire attack, and how to respond to each. First, when fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, attack at once from outside. Second, if fire breaks out but the enemy's soldiers stay calm, wait and do not attack. Giles explains that the main purpose of fire is to throw the enemy into confusion. If the enemy stays calm, it means he is ready for you, so you must be careful. Third, when the flames are at their strongest, follow with an attack if you can. If you cannot, stay where you are. Fourth, if you can start a fire from outside, do not wait for one to start inside. Attack at a good moment. Fifth, when you start a fire, stay upwind of it, meaning on the side the wind is coming from. Do not attack from downwind.

Tu Mu explains the fourth point. If the enemy has camped in a place full of dry grass, you should use fire against him when you can. If you wait, he may burn the grass around his camp himself, and your chance will be gone. Tu Mu gives the example of the general Huang-fu Sung, who was defending a city with a very small force in the year 184. His officers were nervous. But the rebels had camped among thick grass. One night a strong wind came up. Huang-fu Sung sent brave men to set fire to the grass while his soldiers on the walls raised torches. Then he charged out with drums beating, and the rebels fled in panic.

Tu Mu also explains the fifth point in a simple way. If the wind blows from the east, start the fire on the east side and attack from the east too. If you start it on the east side and attack from the west, you will suffer from the fire just like the enemy.

Sun Tzu adds a note about wind. A wind that rises in the daytime lasts long, but a night wind soon dies down. In every army, he says, the five developments connected with fire must be known, the movements of the stars calculated, and a watch kept for the right days. Chang Yu adds that you must not only know how to attack with fire, but also guard against fire attacks from the enemy.

Sun Tzu then compares fire with water. Those who use fire to help their attack show intelligence. Those who use water to help their attack gain extra strength. Water can cut off an enemy, but it cannot take away all his belongings. Ts'ao Kung explains that water can block the enemy's road or divide his army, but it cannot destroy his stored supplies. Chang Yu says this is why Sun Tzu speaks about fire at length and about water in only a few lines.

Next comes a sentence that Giles calls one of the most puzzling in the book. Sun Tzu says it is a sad fate for someone who tries to win battles and succeed in attacks without developing a spirit of enterprise, because the result is wasted time and a general lack of progress. Here, enterprise means boldness and readiness to act. Many commentators, including Ts'ao Kung and Tu Mu, think the line is about giving rewards quickly to those who deserve them. Giles prefers the reading of Mei Yao-ch'en, who says that a leader must seize good moments when they come and use bold means such as fire and water. What he must not do is sit still and only hold on to what he already has.

Sun Tzu continues with a saying. The wise ruler makes his plans well in advance, and the good general develops his resources.

Then the tone of the chapter changes. Sun Tzu gives strong warnings against using war for the wrong reasons. Do not move unless you see an advantage. Do not use your troops unless there is something to gain. Do not fight unless the situation is critical. No ruler should send troops into the field just because he is angry. No general should fight a battle just because he feels insulted. If it is to your advantage, move forward. If not, stay where you are.

Anger may in time turn into happiness, and annoyance may be followed by contentment. But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come back into being. And the dead can never be brought back to life. Giles adds a sad note: Sun Tzu's own state of Wu would later become an example of this saying, since it was destroyed.

So, Sun Tzu concludes, the wise ruler is careful and the good general is full of caution. This is the way to keep a country at peace and an army whole.

In a modern reading, the closing lines of this chapter are often quoted as advice against making big decisions in anger, in business or in personal life. This is a later use of the words. For Sun Tzu, the point was that a ruler's anger could destroy a whole kingdom.

## Chapter 13: The Use of Spies

The last chapter is about spies, people who secretly gather information about an enemy. Sun Tzu explains why spies are worth their cost, names five kinds of spies, and describes how a wise leader should manage them.

He begins by returning to the great cost of war, which he discussed in Chapter 2. Raising an army of a hundred thousand men and marching them far away causes heavy losses for the people and drains the State's wealth. The cost is a thousand ounces of silver every day. There is disturbance at home and abroad, and men fall down exhausted on the roads. As many as seven hundred thousand families are kept from their work.

Giles explains this number with a note from the commentators. In old China, land was divided into groups of eight families who farmed around a shared central field. In time of war, one family in each group sent a soldier, and the other seven helped support him. So an army of a hundred thousand men affected the farming of seven hundred thousand families.

Then Sun Tzu makes a strong argument. Armies may face each other for years, fighting for a victory that is decided in a single day. So if a leader stays ignorant of the enemy's condition because he does not want to spend a hundred ounces of silver on honors and payments, that is the height of inhumanity. Such a person is no leader of men, no help to his ruler, and no master of victory.

Giles explains the logic of this argument. War brings terrible suffering and huge costs. Without good information about the enemy, a war can drag on for years. The only way to get this information is through spies, and good spies must be well paid. So it is foolish to save a small amount on spies when every day of war costs far more, and that cost falls mainly on the poor. That is why Sun Tzu calls it a crime against humanity not to use spies.

Next Sun Tzu explains what spies provide. What lets a wise ruler and a good general strike and win, and achieve things beyond ordinary men, is foreknowledge. Foreknowledge means knowing something before it happens. Here it means knowing the enemy's positions and plans.

He says where this knowledge cannot come from. It cannot be obtained from spirits. It cannot be worked out from past experience. And it cannot be found by calculation. The commentator Li Ch'uan explains that things like length, distance, and size can be measured exactly, but human actions cannot be calculated in that way. Sun Tzu concludes that knowledge of the enemy's positions can only be obtained from other men.

So he turns to the use of spies, of whom there are five kinds: local spies, inward spies, converted spies, doomed spies, and surviving spies. When all five kinds are working, no one can discover the secret system. Sun Tzu calls this "divine manipulation of the threads", like a person who secretly controls many threads at once, and he says it is a ruler's most precious skill.

He then describes each kind. Local spies are ordinary people who live in the enemy's district. Tu Mu says you should win them over by treating them kindly.

Inward spies are officials of the enemy. Tu Mu lists the kinds of officials who might be persuaded: able men who lost their positions, people who feel passed over for promotion, and people who always try to stay on both sides. They should be won over secretly with rich gifts. But Ho Shih warns that inward spies can be dangerous. He tells how, in the year 303, a rebel leader had his own man beaten until he bled and then sent him to the enemy, pretending to be a traitor. The man promised to help the enemy enter the city. When the enemy soldiers climbed in, they were all killed, and the rebel then defeated the rest of the army.

Converted spies are enemy spies whom we capture and use for our own purposes. Giles explains that this usually means winning them over with large payments and promises, so that they carry back false information and spy on their own people. Ho Shih gives an example from 260 BC. Spies secretly working for the state of Ch'in told the king of Chao that Ch'in only feared one man, a young officer named Chao Kua. Chao Kua had studied war all his life and believed no one could beat him. Even his own father, the famous general Chao She, had warned that his son would bring ruin on the army if he ever became general. The king believed the spies and gave Chao Kua command anyway. Chao Kua fell into a trap, and his army was completely destroyed.

Doomed spies are our own spies who are deliberately given false information. We do certain things openly to deceive them, and they report these false things to the enemy. Tu Yu explains that when the enemy acts on the false report and then finds that we do something quite different, these spies will be killed. That is why they are called doomed.

Surviving spies are those who go to the enemy's camp and come back with news. Giles explains that these are the ordinary spies who form a regular part of the army. Tu Mu describes the ideal surviving spy. He should be very intelligent but look like a fool, poorly dressed but with a will of iron. He should be strong, brave, able to bear hunger and cold, and able to put up with shame.

Ho Shih tells a lively story about such a spy, Ta-hsi Wu. He and two companions rode to the enemy camp at night wearing the enemy's uniforms. They crept close and listened until they learned the passwords. Then they rode boldly through the camp pretending to be night guards. More than once they even stopped to punish enemy soldiers who were breaking the rules. They came back with full information, and their emperor won a great victory.

Sun Tzu then says how a leader should treat spies. No one in the whole army should be closer to the general than his spies. Tu Mu and Mei Yao-ch'en note that a spy may even enter the general's private sleeping tent. No one should be rewarded more generously. And in no other business should there be greater secrecy. Tu Mu says all messages with spies should go "mouth to ear".

To use spies, a leader needs certain qualities. Spies cannot be used well without a natural wisdom and insight. They cannot be managed well without kindness and honesty. And without a very sharp mind, a leader cannot be sure their reports are true. Mei Yao-ch'en warns that spies may switch to the enemy's side. Sun Tzu says: "Be subtle! be subtle! and use your spies for every kind of business." Subtle here means careful, clever, and quiet.

He adds a harsh rule. If a spy reveals secret news before the right time, he must be put to death, together with the person he told. Giles notes that the second death is meant to stop the news from spreading further, and he admits that Sun Tzu can be accused of inhumanity here.

Sun Tzu explains how spies prepare for any operation. Whether the aim is to destroy an army, attack a city, or kill a particular person, you must first learn the names of the commanding general's helpers, assistants, door-keepers, and guards. Our spies must find these out. Giles suggests that the first purpose is to see whether any of them can be bribed.

Next Sun Tzu explains why the converted spy is the most important. The enemy's spies who come to spy on us must be found, tempted with payments, taken aside, and given comfortable housing. In this way they become converted spies who work for us. Through the converted spy, we learn which local people and which officials can be used as local and inward spies. Through him, we know how to send doomed spies with false news to the enemy. And through him, surviving spies can be used at the right times. The aim of all five kinds of spying is knowledge of the enemy, and this knowledge comes first of all from the converted spy. So the converted spy must be treated with the greatest generosity.

Sun Tzu then gives two examples from ancient history. The rise of the Yin dynasty was due to I Chih, who had served the earlier Hsia dynasty. The rise of the Chou dynasty was due to Lü Ya, who had served the Yin. Giles believes Sun Tzu is presenting these two famous men as examples of something like converted spies. They knew the weaknesses of the old rulers and helped the new ones. Giles notes that some commentators, such as Mei Yao-ch'en and Ho Shih, were offended by the idea that such great heroes were spies, but Giles finds their objections weak.

The book ends with these thoughts. Only the wise ruler and the wise general will use the most intelligent people in the army as spies, and in this way they achieve great results. Spies are a most important part of war, because an army's ability to move depends on them. The commentator Chia Lin says that an army without spies is like a man without ears or eyes. Tu Mu gives a final warning: just as water can carry a boat across a river but can also sink it, spies can bring great success, but relying on them can also lead to total destruction.

In a modern reading, this chapter is often linked to the value of good information, research, and intelligence in business and government. This is a later use of Sun Tzu's ideas. His own methods, such as bribery, deception, and killing spies, belong clearly to ancient warfare.

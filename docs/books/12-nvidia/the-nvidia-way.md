# The Nvidia Way: Jensen Huang and the Making of a Tech Giant

Tae Kim

## Introduction

Tae Kim begins with a whiteboard. At Nvidia, Jensen Huang uses it to question engineers, explain problems, and expose whether an argument actually works. A presentation can conceal gaps behind polished slides; a diagram built in front of colleagues makes the reasoning visible. Erasing that diagram also suggests something about the company: yesterday's successful idea cannot be protected indefinitely from a better one.

Kim's central argument is that Nvidia's rise cannot be explained by semiconductor designs alone. Its hardware advantages grew out of an organization that combined technical ambition, fast decisions, unusually direct communication, and sustained investment before new markets were commercially attractive. Gaming financed capabilities that later proved useful for scientific computing and artificial intelligence. The AI boom rewarded a platform assembled over many years, not a company that suddenly discovered the opportunity when ChatGPT arrived.

The opening historical marker is June 18, 2024, when Nvidia briefly became the world's most valuable company, at about \$3.3 trillion. This summary follows the supplied book's reporting horizon, not Nvidia's current valuation or a prediction about future investment returns. It is an original chapter-by-chapter synthesis, including the conclusion and appendix, rather than a replacement for Kim's reporting.

Kim had followed Nvidia as an analyst and journalist and interviewed more than a hundred people for the book. They included Huang, cofounders Curtis Priem and Chris Malachowsky, employees, investors, customers, manufacturing partners, and competitors. Those perspectives let him reconstruct failures that the company itself is not especially eager to revisit.

In their interview after Huang's 2024 Caltech commencement speech, the CEO resisted a nostalgic account of the early years. The first products were serious mistakes, not hidden masterpieces. Nvidia became capable by repeatedly repairing a company that its own decisions had nearly destroyed. Kim gives more credit to the founders' early judgment than Huang does, preserving a useful disagreement about how much was avoidable failure and how much was learning under uncertainty.

The operating culture is similarly double-edged. Flattening the organization and making reasoning public can reduce internal politics and prevent middle managers from filtering away bad news. But the mechanism includes embarrassing people in front of colleagues, demanding exceptional effort, and placing the founder at the center of an enormous number of decisions. Employees Kim interviewed often preferred that clarity to the evasiveness they found elsewhere; that testimony does not make the environment easy.

The book's unresolved question is succession. If the company's coherence depends so heavily on Huang's technical understanding, attention, and will, can its methods remain effective without him? Kim presents the Nvidia Way as something worth studying, while leaving open whether it can be separated from the person who created it.

## 1. Pain and Suffering

Part I, The Early Years, follows the founders before 1993. Huang's story starts with his parents' determination to give their children opportunities in America. Born in Taiwan and raised partly in Thailand, he learned English through words his mother selected from a dictionary. When political unrest made the family's situation less secure, the boys went ahead to relatives in Washington while their parents prepared to follow.

His parents then spent much of what they owned on a Kentucky boarding school, believing they were buying a strong education. Oneida Baptist Institute was not the preparatory school they imagined. It served many troubled children, and the young Huang encountered bullying, daily chores, and an intimidating older roommate. Cleaning dormitory bathrooms became part of his education. The relationship with his roommate eventually changed: Huang helped him learn to read, and the older boy introduced Huang to weightlifting.

Huang later described the experience as reducing his fear of unfamiliar situations and increasing his tolerance for discomfort. Kim connects that interpretation to his later willingness to confront powerful competitors. It remains a retrospective explanation of one person's development, not evidence that mistreatment is necessary for leadership.

After his parents settled in Oregon, Huang returned to family life and public school. At Portland's Paddle Palace he practiced table tennis and cleaned floors to help pay for tournament travel. The owner recognized his promise. A disastrous tournament visit to Las Vegas, where he let the city's distractions interfere with preparation, stayed with him as a lesson about focus. Later he placed third in a junior doubles event. Talent, in this telling, was less decisive than what he did with his attention.

Working at Denny's supplied another kind of training. Huang moved from dishes and cleaning to clearing tables and serving customers. The restaurant pushed a shy teenager to communicate, handle pressure, and deal with other people's mistakes without stopping the flow of service. He took pride in doing ordinary tasks exceptionally well. Yet efficiency mattered too: he disliked laborious milkshake orders and tried to steer customers toward simpler drinks. Kim sees an early version of the tension between uncompromising standards and limited time.

At school, games and programming gave Huang reasons to seek out computers he could not afford at home. He entered Oregon State University young, chose electrical engineering, and met Lori Mills, whom he married after graduation. He accepted a chip-design job at AMD rather than LSI Logic, initially preferring the company whose reputation he knew.

His professional education continued at Stanford while he worked and raised a family. Completing the master's degree took eight years. That experience illustrates a distinction Kim returns to: urgency about today's work can coexist with patience about an important long-term objective.

Eventually, manually designing chips at AMD felt too slow and repetitive. LSI's software tools promised a different future, and Huang moved there despite leaving a familiar position. Assigned to support Sun Microsystems, he met Priem and Malachowsky. Kim ends the chapter with Huang's own belief that character and resilience matter more than intelligence alone. The biography supplies experiences that shaped that belief; it does not establish suffering as a reliable recipe for success.

## 2. The Graphics Revolution

Curtis Priem's route into graphics began with games written through a slow terminal connected to a distant mainframe. A billiards simulation required a large roll of punched paper tape. A supportive mathematics teacher, Elmer Kress, gave him access to the equipment and encouraged experiments with digitized images. Graphics was not initially a market opportunity to him; it was a way to make something fascinating work.

At Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, access to a powerful IBM computer reinforced that interest. When his father lost his job, a General Motors work-study program helped Priem finish his degree. He could have pursued funded graduate study and a corporate career, but the example of Apple's founders suggested that a young engineer could combine inventive work with ownership in a growing business.

He joined Vermont Microsystems and unexpectedly became the principal designer of IBM's Professional Graphics Controller. The card improved color and resolution and performed graphics operations independently of the main processor. It also preserved compatibility with an older graphics mode. That balance between specialized acceleration and the existing software environment would become important again at Nvidia.

The company did not offer the equity participation Priem had hoped for. He looked for work in Silicon Valley, first joining testing-equipment maker GenRad. Management missteps and instability there soon sent him looking again. Wayne Rosing recruited him to Sun Microsystems.

Rosing had learned from Apple's Lisa that a powerful machine could still disappoint if its graphical experience was slow or inadequate. At Sun, he wanted dedicated graphics acceleration for the new SPARCstation. His superior favored relying on the central processor instead and instructed him not to pursue the graphics project. Rosing quietly created room for Priem to build it anyway, within the physical and memory constraints of the workstation.

Chris Malachowsky supplied a different strength. He had originally prepared for medicine, then realized while taking the entrance examination that engineering interested him more. At Hewlett-Packard, manufacturing work taught him how designs became real computers, at scale and at a profit. Later he developed processors and control software. A supposedly practice interview with Sun turned into the opportunity to work with Priem.

Priem conceived the architecture; Malachowsky figured out how to implement it. Their accelerator shifted much of the graphics workload away from the CPU to dedicated chips. LSI Logic agreed to manufacture unusually demanding designs and assigned Huang to the account. The future Nvidia founders learned to work together by solving problems that stretched the available tools and fabrication methods.

Their collaboration was not serene. Priem could jump directly from an idea to a conclusion, leaving others unable to follow. Malachowsky had to draw out the missing reasoning, sometimes through arguments loud enough to frighten teammates. Huang interpreted productive disagreement as a way to sharpen ideas. Kim also shows the interpersonal strain behind that later management principle.

The resulting GX accelerator, completed in 1989, made Sun's display operations dramatically faster. Priem's flight simulator, Aviator, gave customers an especially persuasive demonstration. It combined detailed terrain, responsive flight, and networked competition at a level ordinary PCs could not match. Giving copies to Sun's sales offices helped sell the hardware. The software demonstrated what the chip enabled, rather than asking customers to infer value from specifications.

Aviator also taught Priem about piracy and software protection, lessons he carried into later designs. GX moved from an optional upgrade to a standard part of Sun's workstations, and the project's success advanced all three men's careers.

Yet Priem and Malachowsky felt that Sun was becoming harder to innovate within. In their recollections, internal sponsorship and presentations increasingly mattered more than buildable technology. Projects were approved and canceled without shipping. A competing plan to return more graphics work to the CPU defeated their proposal for an accelerator using new Samsung memory. Priem experienced the treatment as both technically misguided and personally brutal.

The two considered leaving to demonstrate the memory technology as consultants. They asked Huang, now experienced in licensing and customer relationships, to help with a contract. He supplied the question that changed the plan: why create the opportunity for somebody else rather than build a company around it? Kim presents Nvidia's origins as the convergence of architectural imagination, practical engineering, business judgment, and dissatisfaction with an organization that no longer let those abilities work together.

## 3. The Birth of Nvidia

Two developments made the early 1990s attractive for a graphics startup. PCI gave expansion cards a faster connection to the rest of the computer, while Windows 3.1 made a richer visual interface familiar to millions of PC users. Priem and Malachowsky already knew how to accelerate demanding windowed displays. They believed those skills could move from expensive workstations into the much larger personal-computer market.

They would design chips, not build factories or assemble every graphics card themselves. Outsourcing those activities would concentrate scarce money and attention on their distinctive expertise. Their aim was not simply to transplant a workstation product: consumers and games would create different requirements.

The founders developed the business over long conversations at a Denny's in East San Jose. Huang did not immediately abandon a job he enjoyed. He pressed his friends about the opportunity and wanted to believe the company could eventually generate fifty million dollars in annual revenue. Their confidence in graphics was not yet a complete business model.

Priem broke the stalemate by leaving Sun first. Huang and Malachowsky followed after handing over responsibilities at their existing companies. Kim distinguishes these commitments from formal incorporation, which came on April 5, 1993. The company did not spring into existence fully staffed and financed in a single restaurant conversation.

There were attractive ways not to take the risk. Rosing offered Priem work accelerating the programming language that became Java. Later, he proposed licensing Sun's patents to the new team if they made chips compatible with both Sun workstations and PCs. That could provide a major customer and reduce legal uncertainty. But dual compatibility would consume resources and might not even be technically practical. The founders chose the PC opportunity rather than let a reassuring early customer dictate their direction.

Their initial workplace was Priem's cleared-out townhouse. They recruited former colleagues, some of whom worked without salary until financing arrived. The experienced workstation engineers were surprisingly unfamiliar with their newly purchased Gateway PC. Making an older Sun graphics chip work with Windows took substantial software effort. A version of Aviator, renamed Zone5, became their demonstration of what better PC graphics could do.

Even choosing a name required abandoning ideas. Combinations of the founders' names sounded awkward, and several ordinary words containing the letters NV were already taken. A reference to the Latin word for envy survived as Nvidia, also echoing the NV1 chip they intended to develop. Each founder initially received an equal stake for a small cash contribution.

Raising capital revealed the limits of technical credibility. Wilfred Corrigan, Huang's former CEO at LSI, doubted parts of the market argument but strongly endorsed Huang himself. His introduction to Sequoia's Don Valentine mattered enormously. The founders were not pitching from the same position as unknown engineers without industry relationships.

At Kleiner Perkins, a partner pushed them to manufacture circuit boards in-house. Malachowsky objected that this would force them to compete in an area where they had no special advantage. They walked away rather than accept money tied to a distracting operating model. Sutter Hill's Tench Coxe was more receptive, recognizing the team's depth despite skepticism elsewhere in his partnership about another graphics company.

The Sequoia meeting went badly. Nvidia prepared an impressive demonstration; Valentine wanted clear answers about customers, positioning, and competition. Asked what kind of company they were, Priem effectively answered that their chip could do everything. A broad technical vision sounded like an inability to choose. The proposed manufacturing partner, SGS-Thomson, also worried the investors, who preferred TSMC.

Further meetings nevertheless produced funding. Sequoia and Sutter Hill each invested a million dollars in June 1993. The team, the opportunity, existing relationships, and the investors' own experience with graphics and game companies outweighed the poor pitch.

Kim's lesson is not that planning or explanation can be skipped. Huang recognized how badly he had articulated the business. Rather, a reputation built through earlier work can buy the opportunity to learn skills a new founder lacks. Nvidia began with outstanding engineers and a plausible opening, but its first financing did not prove that it already knew how to build a successful product.

## 4. All In

Part II, Near-Death Experiences, covers 1993 to 2003. It begins with a mistake that almost ended Nvidia: treating an ingenious design as proof that customers would want it. Once financing arrived, the founders divided responsibility. Huang ran the business, Priem designed the architecture, and Malachowsky handled engineering and implementation. They hired staff, moved into a real office, and started building the NV1.

The design addressed genuine constraints. Memory initially cost about fifty dollars per megabyte, and ordinary personal computers struggled with the mathematics needed for advanced graphics. Priem used forward texture mapping and quadrilateral shapes rather than the triangle-based approach that would become standard. His method reduced resource requirements, but developers would have to rewrite games to benefit from it. He also combined graphics with a proprietary audio system. The card attempted to improve several things at once, asking the market to accommodate its inventions.

Huang obtained useful support. He pushed Intel to make its PCI expansion standard more capable and open. A manufacturing agreement with SGS-Thomson helped pay for Nvidia's software team. Sega invested and promised games adapted for NV1, while commissioning the NV2 for a future console. An initial order for 250,000 chips suggested a promising launch.

Then the assumptions collapsed. Memory became far cheaper, weakening the argument for the unfamiliar graphics approach. Most developers did not rewrite their games. The hugely popular DOOM ran badly, because NV1 did not fully implement the older graphics capabilities it needed. Its audio was incompatible with the widely used SoundBlaster standard, and software workarounds kept breaking. Customers wanted their favorite games to run well, not an explanation of why those games should have been written differently. Nearly all of that large initial chip order came back.

Kim links the failure to a boardroom question that Huang initially did not understand: how would he position the product? Harvey Jones was asking what would make customers choose it over an alternative, not requesting a feature list. Huang studied Al Ries and Jack Trout's book on positioning and recognized how little Nvidia's complicated story mattered beside the simple fact that competing cards played DOOM properly.

Sega also abandoned NV2. Here Kim describes a specific financial lifeline rather than a clean rescue: a contract promised Nvidia a million dollars if it delivered a working prototype on a suitably sized motherboard. Engineer Wayne Kogachi completed that isolated assignment, releasing money for the next design. It was still not enough to maintain the workforce. Nvidia cut its staff from more than a hundred people to forty.

Meanwhile, 3dfx demonstrated what a clear proposition could achieve. Its Voodoo Graphics brought capabilities associated with expensive Silicon Graphics workstations to affordable PCs. Hardware-accelerated GLQuake gave gamers a compelling reason to buy it. Its executives considered acquiring Nvidia but expected the company to collapse without their help. They also tried to recruit driver specialist Dwight Diercks. His decision to stay preserved expertise crucial to Nvidia's recovery.

With roughly three million dollars and nine months of operating time left, Nvidia changed direction. NV3, sold as RIVA 128, would support established graphics standards and compete by being faster, not by requiring a different ecosystem. Huang approved an unusually large chip and a wide memory interface. Yet Nvidia could not afford the normal sequence of manufacturing prototypes, discovering faults, and manufacturing again.

The answer was a million-dollar Ikos emulator. Buying it reduced the cash runway to about six months, so it was a concentrated bet on learning faster rather than surviving longer without a competitive product. Engineers could test the design before manufacturing it, although the simulation was painfully slow. Loading Windows took minutes; tests ran overnight. An apparent emulator error turned out to be a real hardware bug. The team also reused workable parts of earlier designs and licensed a proper VGA core from Weitek, bringing in designer Gopal Solanki. Survival required practical borrowing as well as invention.

The first demonstration in April 1997 was not effortless vindication. Software remained fragile, and a benchmark could crash. When a carefully restarted system nevertheless produced exceptional results, rivals recognized the threat. A Rendition architect asked about a job. The working chip also gave Huang a stronger basis for raising further capital.

A major order from STB Systems finally changed the company's prospects. RIVA 128 shipped more than a million units within four months, won major PC customers, and helped Nvidia reach its first profitable quarter. Huang celebrated by handing employees single dollar bills, giving shipping worker Kathleen Buffington a second for her exceptional effort. Kim's account makes the turnaround collective: customer compatibility, software, manufacturing, testing, financing, and execution all mattered. The lesson is not that desperation guarantees invention, but that Nvidia survived by correcting its own theory of what a useful product was.

## 5. Ultra-Aggressive

RIVA 128 made Nvidia attractive to people who wanted to build something exceptional. Caroline Landry, a designer at Matrox, realized that Nvidia had already shipped a chip far ahead of the unfinished product she was developing. She joined as Nvidia's first female engineer. The opportunity was real, but so was the price. Eleven o'clock finishes and full weekends were ordinary. When she mentioned complaints about the hours, Huang compared employees with Olympic athletes who disliked early training but still needed it to excel.

Kim presents this expectation as foundational, not a temporary response to a deadline. Employees were supposed to put the company's survival ahead of personal comfort and professional ego. Landry initially envied the recognition given to the young engineer Jonah Alben, whom Huang identified as a future leader. She came to see unusually capable colleagues as allies rather than threats. Working beside someone smarter increased everyone's chances of succeeding.

Newcomers received an unusually blunt introduction. Marketing director Michael Hara urged them to question established methods, propose ideas, take risks, and accept mistakes. Anyone expecting an undemanding job should leave. Yet permission to fail had a sharp boundary: repeating mistakes could cost someone their position. Nvidia hired quickly and dismissed unsuitable recruits quickly, too. Its early expectation that employees advance or make way for others meant even experienced people could feel replaceable.

The organizing principle behind the urgency was Huang's Speed of Light standard. Instead of asking how long a project had taken before, or how quickly competitors worked, teams were to break it into tasks and estimate the minimum physically possible time for each. Queues, waiting, and bureaucratic delays were problems to remove, not allowances to build into a schedule. Huang believed comfortable targets encouraged employees to manage appearances and careers rather than maximize the company's progress.

RIVA 128 supplied the practical model. Dwight Diercks identified software drivers, which let applications and operating systems use a graphics chip, as a central bottleneck. Traditionally, programmers waited for physical hardware before completing and testing them. Nvidia's emulator allowed driver development before manufactured chips arrived, removing nearly a year from the usual sequence. Licensing existing technology also avoided rebuilding less important components.

Nvidia retained these advantages after the crisis. Drivers were prepared early, tested against important applications, and made compatible across chip generations. The company took responsibility for distributing monthly updates rather than leaving customers dependent on separate computer makers' schedules. A new game or software update could break graphics behavior; centralized maintenance made compatibility an ongoing service. Speed meant changing the order and organization of work, not simply spending more hours on the old process.

Nevertheless, the hours were extraordinary. CFO Geoff Ribar saw Huang work from morning until close to midnight. Marketing staff commonly worked sixty to eighty hours a week. Colleagues joked that leaving for a late-night movie amounted to taking half a day. Employees brought sleeping bags or children to the office. Some remembered deep friendship and shared purpose, but even a bathroom encounter with Huang could trigger an anxious recital of unfinished work. Ribar learned that being the first person the CEO encountered could mean absorbing anger about problems outside finance.

Huang continually warned that Nvidia could disappear within thirty days. Kim distinguishes an atmosphere of permanent emergency from the company's actual finances: bankruptcy was not always immediately imminent. But a sequence of wrong decisions could still start an irreversible decline. The warning made hypothetical threats emotionally immediate.

Intel's entry into graphics made that anxiety concrete. Its i740 offered an eight-megabyte frame buffer against RIVA 128's four. Intel's influence with computer manufacturers could turn its specification into a purchasing requirement and strand Nvidia's successful product. Orders began to weaken. Huang framed the contest as an existential battle against a business roughly 860 times Nvidia's size by revenue. Landry recalled dragging herself through exhausting days with the conviction that defeating Intel justified the effort.

Chris Malachowsky led the response. He had left spare capacity in the original design and found a way to rewire it for the larger buffer. Engineers used a focused ion beam, a tool that modifies tiny features on prototype chips, to test the changes. The resulting RIVA 128ZX arrived eleven days after Intel's product, matching its memory capacity and offering better performance for a modestly higher price.

During this effort, Malachowsky also helped newcomer Sanford Russell understand Nvidia's technology. Russell appeared at the lab late at night and asked questions between stretches of debugging. The same person racing to rescue a product made time to explain its workings. Kim's account includes this patient transfer of knowledge alongside the CEO's confrontational motivation.

Urgency did not remove tradeoffs. A magazine's comparison showed that RIVA's treatment of certain images looked worse than competing products. Deliberate visual noise used to mask rendering irregularities could leave smoke or clouds looking muddy. Chief scientist David Kirk explained that compromises had helped get the chip finished in time. Huang refused to accept winning on speed while losing on image quality. Walt Donovan promised the next generation would correct the weakness, but even that assurance failed to calm him.

Competitiveness also escaped any useful business purpose. Huang overwhelmed colleagues at table tennis, but could not defeat Ribar, who had been a highly ranked young chess player. Memorized openings did not overcome Ribar's skill. After losses, Huang sometimes scattered the pieces and sought a table-tennis rematch on more favorable ground. Kim does not portray every expression of his drive as admirable.

Manufacturing then exposed a different vulnerability. SGS-Thomson could not reliably supply enough usable chips. Jeff Fisher's trip to its French factory with Gateway became an exercise in explaining why only about half the expected allocation would arrive. Thanksgiving became a succession of calls to disappointed customers.

Huang had already opened a relationship with TSMC after earlier approaches went unanswered. A personal letter to Morris Chang in 1996 led to capacity commitments and repeated visits. Nvidia made TSMC its main supplier in February 1998. Yet choosing a better partner did not instantly solve production. Residual titanium caused unpredictable defects in RIVA 128ZX chips, forcing Nvidia to test every part before shipment.

The company turned a building into a large testing operation. Even the tests needed careful control: a faulty chip could appear functional if a rig was not properly powered down between runs. Exhausted engineers were eventually relieved by hundreds of contract workers. Their help exposed class divisions inside the supposedly united company. Engineers avoided sitting with the less-educated, often immigrant testers and complained when they took large quantities of free food. Landry challenged the exclusion by eating with them. Huang told employees to share their meals, insisting that the testers' contribution mattered far more than depleted snacks.

The disruption still nearly exhausted Nvidia's money. Between the quarters ending in April and July 1998, revenue fell from 28.3 million dollars to 12.1 million, while losses rose from one million to 9.7 million. A planned public offering stalled amid the Asian financial crisis. Ribar calculated that insolvency was weeks away.

Huang persuaded Diamond Multimedia, STB Systems, and Creative Labs to lend eleven million dollars. Their notes could convert into stock at ninety percent of the eventual public offering price. The arrangement exchanged customers' confidence in Nvidia's recovery for a potential ownership reward. It bought time, but the pressure helped drive Ribar away before even his first stock grant vested.

Huang's larger response was not to slow down. With Hara, he examined why graphics leaders kept losing their advantage. Computer manufacturers refreshed products every six months, while chip makers commonly spent eighteen months developing a single design. A successful chip became stale before its replacement arrived.

Nvidia reorganized into three teams: one developed a new architecture while two prepared faster derivatives in parallel. This supported a new release every six months. Curtis Priem's architecture helped make that possible. A software resource manager could emulate features that were not ready in hardware, accepting some performance cost rather than delaying everything. It could also replace selected hardware functions when computing capacity allowed, saving chip space. Backwards-compatible drivers made each replacement easier for customers to adopt.

By the end of 1999, the system linked technical flexibility to customers' buying calendars. Rivals had to compete not only with the current chip but with a predictable next improvement. Kim's explanation of Nvidia's speed therefore includes architecture, software, manufacturing relationships, and financing. It also retains the contradiction: a company learning to make execution repeatable continued to demand that employees live as though every day were another emergency.

## 6. Just Go Win

In September 1998, 3dfx sued Nvidia over an allegedly infringed rendering patent. Nvidia answered one part of the publicity battle by changing a webpage linked from the lawsuit announcement into a boast about its own graphics leadership. The provocation showed its confidence, without settling the legal allegation.

Nvidia's accelerating release schedule exposed weaknesses its rivals could not solve simply by announcing more products. At 3dfx, engineers kept adding features in pursuit of a perfect chip. Nvidia was more willing to ship what worked and reserve unfinished improvements for the next release. With generations arriving much faster, an imperfect but timely product could outperform an ambitious design that remained unavailable.

Kim traces 3dfx's decline through decisions that diverted attention from this central problem. It broadened its range into combined two-dimensional and three-dimensional graphics chips without building the efficient family of related designs Nvidia used. The additional products required expertise it did not yet possess. Its major future chips, Napalm and Rampage, continued slipping.

In December 1998, 3dfx also bought board manufacturer STB Systems for 141 million dollars. The acquisition promised better quality control, a direct consumer brand, and control over distribution. It would also deprive Nvidia of its leading board partner, which had supplied its first RIVA purchase order and recently helped finance its survival. STB would henceforth use only 3dfx chips.

The apparent strategic victory reversed itself. Other board makers, now competing with their own chip supplier, moved to Nvidia. Managing inventories, board manufacturing, and retail distribution proved far more difficult than 3dfx's executives expected. Leadership attention left the chip business just when faster delivery mattered most. The company ran out of cash. Nvidia purchased its patents and other assets in December 2000 and hired about a hundred employees; 3dfx formally filed for bankruptcy in October 2002.

Former 3dfx executives later regretted abandoning their core expertise. One argued that timely releases could have prevented Nvidia's rise. That remains an alternative history, not an outcome Kim can demonstrate. More revealing is the surprise of engineers who joined the winner expecting a hidden technical shortcut. They found relentless work and enforcement of deadlines. Their recollection emphasizes execution rather than a miraculous process that removed the need for it.

Recruiting was another form of competition. Nvidia repeatedly approached John Montrym, Silicon Graphics' celebrated chief engineer. Huang's initial pitch was blunt: a company selling graphics to millions of personal computers could fund improvements faster than one selling thousands of expensive workstations. Nvidia would eventually compress SGI's capabilities onto a single chip. Montrym declined several approaches, including one from his former colleague Priem.

Words failed where a demonstration succeeded. Nvidia built an immersive military-themed simulation resembling the showcases SGI used for its advanced systems. Montrym could see that work associated with his own painstaking projects was now within the reach of a much faster-moving company. It was also striking that Nvidia could devote so much capability to recruiting one person. He resigned from SGI a week later. Other engineers followed, attracted by the prospect of working with someone they admired.

SGI's subsequent patent lawsuit initially worried employees, but Nvidia's leaders also treated it as recognition that their young company mattered. Kim describes a dispute over whether Nvidia's uncertain internal sales forecasts demonstrated financial harm. A July 1999 settlement produced a partnership: Nvidia would supply SGI's lower-end graphics business and hire fifty of its employees. A hostile encounter became another channel for gaining talent and business.

Aggression toward rivals did not mean every relationship had to be exploitative. TSMC executive Rick Tsai remembered hard negotiations over pricing and urgent calls when wafers arrived late. But Huang also proposed judging fairness over years rather than insisting that both parties win equally in every transaction. Sometimes Nvidia would benefit more; sometimes TSMC would. What mattered was that the relationship eventually balanced. This tolerance for temporary imbalance helped sustain a partnership through repeated difficulties.

The public offering on January 22, 1999, finally provided financial room. Nvidia raised forty-two million dollars, and its shares finished their first trading day up sixty-four percent, valuing the company at 626 million dollars. Employees remembered relief more than triumph. After repeated cash crises, money in the bank meant they could breathe.

At a later management gathering, executives made improbable promises about what they would do if the stock reached a hundred dollars. The price was then about twenty-five. Tattoos, unusual haircuts, green nails, and piercings made the possibility feel like a joke rather than an imminent obligation.

Growing resources also let Nvidia build influence beyond its size. Business-development veteran Oliver Baltuch taught the shy young Keita Kitahama to approach monitor companies bearing free GeForce cards. Manufacturers welcomed access to desirable hardware. Baltuch brought fifty cards to an Intel developer event and offered to replace vendors' existing graphics boards. Compatible drivers made trying Nvidia unusually easy.

Intel eventually chose Nvidia for workstations distributed to software developers. Microsoft received cards around successive releases of DirectX, its software interface for games and multimedia. These were not merely gifts. They made Nvidia familiar to people designing the systems and software its products needed to support. With around 250 employees and fiscal 1999 sales of 158 million dollars, Nvidia could influence much larger partners through useful technology placed in the right hands.

That familiarity helped reopen the console market. Nvidia pursued Microsoft's Xbox project, but Microsoft initially chose the start-up Gigapixel. It invested ten million dollars and committed another fifteen million for development. Gigapixel's thirty-three employees moved into a Microsoft building, and founder George Haber prepared for a major public endorsement from Bill Gates.

Huang and sales executive Chris Diskin kept making their case. They refined presentations late into the night and cultivated Microsoft advocates who considered Nvidia easier and less risky for game development. A week before Gates's scheduled March 2000 announcement, Microsoft reopened negotiations. By Sunday, the companies had agreed that Nvidia would replace Gigapixel.

Nvidia demanded two hundred million dollars upfront for the custom chip's development. Gates personally had to approve the payment. The size and senior-level commitment were intended to make Nvidia harder to displace in the same way. Haber learned of the reversal on Monday. He retained a fifteen-million-dollar development fee but lost the foundation of his hoped-for public offering. His later suggestion that he might otherwise have built the trillion-dollar company expresses lasting bitterness, not a verifiable prediction.

Nvidia's stock passed a hundred dollars during the announcement week, and its executives fulfilled their promises. The celebration, however, was among Priem's last uncomplicated moments at the company.

Priem had unilaterally revised an architecture and replaced shared documentation without telling the software team. A small founding group might once have accommodated this habit. A larger organization could not: programmers were still building against the original specifications. Huang ordered the files restored and required consultation before further changes. The team completed its work and addressed the architectural flaw the following year.

The dispute was also about ownership. Priem regarded the architecture as his creation. Huang insisted that it belonged to the company and that achievement was collective. As specialists arrived from SGI, Priem increasingly doubted whether he could match their expertise. His desire for perfection began obstructing shipments. After repeated conflicts and outside mediation, he moved from product development to intellectual property.

In 2003, a leave to address marital difficulties became a final separation. Huang helped him seek counseling, then offered a full-time return, consulting, or resignation, including the possibility of one last mobile project. Priem chose to leave, exhausted and demoralized, while wishing he could have stayed. Years later, Huang still rejected the idea that his cofounder lacked the ability to learn. Kim preserves both perspectives rather than turning a painful departure into a tidy management lesson.

Even successful Nvidia still faced skeptical investors who expected Intel to crush it or the graphics industry's history of failures to repeat itself. Huang compared the experience with the Monty Python scene in which a living man is treated as already dead. In October 2000, a secondary offering of equity and convertible securities nevertheless raised 387 million dollars. Nvidia had won breathing room and influence through focus, recruitment, and partnership. The chapter's victories remain inseparable from the defeated competitors and strained personal relationships that accompanied them.

## 7. GeForce and the Innovator's Dilemma

A company can serve its best customers brilliantly and still prepare its own defeat. That was the warning Huang took from Clayton Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma. An established business concentrates on demanding, profitable customers while a newcomer enters a cheaper, less attractive market. The newcomer improves until it can challenge the incumbent's core business. Christensen's examples of successive generations of smaller disk drives showed why excellent execution within an existing market was not sufficient protection.

Huang worried less about a rival immediately surpassing Nvidia's most advanced chips than about one establishing itself beneath them. Building a premium graphics processor required enormous resources. A cheap alternative could begin with fewer capabilities, secure customers Nvidia neglected, and gradually move upward.

Intel suggested a defense. Its processors could share a basic design but sell at different speeds. Some manufactured chips failed tests at the highest speed yet functioned correctly at lower settings. Nvidia could similarly stop treating every part unsuitable for a premium product as worthless.

The additional cost of packaging, testing, and assembling one of these parts was only a dollar and thirty-two cents. That was not the full cost of making a chip, but it changed the economics of material that otherwise generated no revenue. Nvidia could recover value through cheaper products without financing an entirely separate architecture. Huang called the approach ship the whole cow: find a use for more than just the most valuable portion of production.

This created several price and performance tiers from a common design. High-end products could support aggressive pricing below them, even if a particular low-cost offering sacrificed profit. Cheaper rivals would have difficulty matching those prices while also recovering the cost of their own development. At the top, Nvidia could discover how much enthusiasts would pay for additional performance. The strategy helped improve overall selling prices while defending the low end. Kim describes it as a major factor in weakening S3 and a practice the graphics industry subsequently adopted.

Technical and economic advantages still needed a story customers understood. The 1999 GeForce 256 could process four pixels at once and move important geometry calculations from the central processor onto dedicated graphics hardware. That allowed more complex scenes, but explaining pipelines, transformation, and lighting was a poor way to establish why an expensive new chip deserved a premium.

Sanford Russell and Michael Hara developed a simpler comparison: call it a graphics processing unit, or GPU. The name placed graphics beside the CPU rather than treating it as an optional peripheral of lesser importance. Dan Vivoli supported the idea. CPUs routinely commanded hundreds of dollars, while Nvidia's comparably complex chips sold wholesale for less than a hundred. Naming a category could change perceptions of value as well as describe a product.

Nvidia's engineers objected that the GeForce 256 did not yet deserve that description. Its operations were largely fixed by its designers. Developers could not program the rendering pipeline as they would program a general processor, and other features the engineers associated with a true GPU were missing. Marketing proceeded anyway, arguing that the chip represented a large practical advance and the next generation would add programmability.

Kim therefore treats the announcement of the world's first GPU as a successful stretch, not an uncontested technical milestone. Vivoli deliberately avoided trademarking the term so competitors would help establish the category Nvidia claimed to have created. The company also advertised its confidence more crudely, placing an unauthorized banner on an overpass near 3dfx's headquarters. Police removed it. The attempt to unsettle a rival remained part of a launch otherwise centered on expanding customers' sense of what graphics hardware was worth.

David Kirk wanted to make the ambitious label technically convincing. In a traditional graphics pipeline, a scene's geometry is positioned, converted into screen locations, colored and textured, then assembled into an image. When these stages offer only fixed operations, artists and developers must select from the same limited menu. Kirk proposed programmable shaders: small developer-written programs that determine how parts of a scene are rendered.

This would transfer creative control from chip designers to game makers. More distinctive visuals could attract players who would then buy compatible Nvidia hardware. But adding this flexibility required a costly redesign, and a technical possibility did not automatically establish a market.

Kirk made the investment harder for Huang to reject by connecting it to a threat. Fixed graphics functions would eventually become compact enough for Intel to incorporate into a CPU or motherboard component. A separate card needed to offer something more adaptable and valuable. Programmability might also allow applications outside games. Huang approved the project.

Released in February 2001, GeForce 3 became the first true GPU in Kim's account. By the third fiscal quarter, Nvidia's quarterly revenue had reached 370 million dollars, up eighty-seven percent from a year earlier. That represented an annualized billion-dollar sales pace, rather than a completed year of billion-dollar revenue. Its valuation by year-end was about twenty times its value at the public offering.

The chip also helped Nvidia expand at Apple. Vivoli had recognized that an impressive demonstration should matter emotionally to its audience, not require an engineer to decode it. He funded Mark Daly's recreation of Pixar's animated short Luxo Jr. The original film had required hours to render individual frames. A real-time version would make the progress in computing immediately visible.

Pixar declined permission for a public demonstration. Nvidia nevertheless showed the completed work privately to Steve Jobs, who ran both Apple and Pixar. At first, the familiar lamp animation merely looked good. Then Vivoli changed the camera angle while it ran. Jobs could see that the computer was generating an interactive scene rather than replaying a finished film. He approved GeForce 3 as a premium option for the Power Mac G4 and asked about permission to use the demonstration publicly.

As the meeting ended, Jobs criticized Nvidia's weak laptop position. Diskin challenged him. He argued that Nvidia's high-performance desktop designs could run at lower clock speeds to fit laptop power limits and still offer competitive performance. Apple did not have to judge the designs only by their full-power behavior. Within half an hour, Phil Schiller called to request a detailed review with Nvidia's laptop team. In the following years, Nvidia's chips reached nearly eighty-five percent of Apple's overall computer lineup.

The successes concealed a dangerous distraction. Xbox business, Apple expansion, and the integration of former 3dfx employees demanded attention while Nvidia's next major graphics design went wrong. ATI, meanwhile, bought ArtX, gaining engineers with console experience who developed the R300 chip for the Radeon 9700 PRO.

A contractual dispute with Microsoft left Nvidia without crucial information. Microsoft required acceptance of revised information-sharing and intellectual-property terms before providing specifications for Direct3D 9. Nvidia objected and attempted to design NV30 while guessing what the interface would require. ATI signed and built around the specifications.

The external disagreement combined with internal fragmentation. One Nvidia software engineer discovered that hardware colleagues had removed a fog-rendering function still important to games. Another employee learned that a popular anti-aliasing technique, used to smooth jagged edges, had been dismissed even though ATI already offered it. Teams were making decisions without understanding one another's needs or the market's priorities.

The resulting chip was poorly matched to the new software interface and to important games. Its choices about numerical precision did not align with the specifications described in the chapter. ATI's card arrived in August 2002, ready for the crucial autumn buying season. Nvidia delayed its own launch by roughly five months.

Software workarounds tried to translate instructions into forms NV30 could execute, increasing the computational burden. Higher clock speeds helped compensate but created more heat. A large cooling assembly then produced an intrusive, high-pitched noise. Nvidia attempted to adjust the fan's behavior, but the result remained unpleasant for players.

Marketing responded with a self-mocking video, presenting the card as suitable for leaf blowing and cooking. The joke acknowledged what customers already knew and made rival criticism less potent. It did not repair the product. Compared with ATI's offering, NV30-based cards were hotter, noisier, slower, and more expensive to produce. Holiday-quarter sales fell thirty percent from the previous year, and Nvidia's shares lost eighty percent from their peak ten months earlier.

Huang attacked the failure publicly, asking why nobody had interrupted development to identify the obvious problems. He also brought in a Best Buy executive to describe customer complaints. Yet Kim does not explain survival as the inevitable reward for such confrontation. ATI retained a price of 399 dollars rather than using its cost advantage to cut aggressively. Diercks later argued that a more ruthless rival could have bankrupted Nvidia. That is a retrospective judgment, but it emphasizes how much room Nvidia had lost to control its own fate.

Huang eventually acknowledged his responsibility for collaboration. He saw the difficult integration of 3dfx employees as one contributor, not a substitute for leadership accountability. Nvidia resolved the Microsoft disagreement, strengthened contact with game developers, and required upcoming chips to perform well in the games customers actually used. Teams were expected to expose weak assumptions before they became expensive facts.

Kim's concluding contrast is between vigilance outward and vigilance inward. Nvidia had guarded against cheap competitors, commoditization, and dependence on one market, yet failed to notice its own growing complacency and separation into silos. The company closing Part II had survived a decade of crises. To survive another, its leader needed to make internal coordination as deliberate as competitive strategy.

## 8. The Era of the GPU

Part III, Nvidia Rising, covers 2002 to 2013. Its opening chapter follows Nvidia beyond better graphics into the slower, more uncertain work of making a new computing market.

One beginning was a thesis about clouds. At the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Mark Harris studied simulations of fluids and atmospheric behavior. In 2002, he noticed researchers making graphics processors perform calculations unrelated to pictures. Programmable shaders could be persuaded to work on tables of numbers instead of colors. Scientists translated their problems into graphics operations because the resulting computations could run much faster than on a CPU alone.

These were ingenious workarounds, not an accessible platform. Researchers needed to understand both their scientific problem and specialized graphics programming. Yet early applications in protein folding, financial calculations, and medical imaging suggested that the hardware's usefulness extended far beyond games. Harris named the practice general-purpose computing on GPUs, shortened to GPGPU, and built a website where practitioners exchanged techniques. After completing his doctorate, he joined Nvidia and discovered that employees were already using his term.

Huang saw medical imaging as an especially persuasive sign of a larger opportunity. Requiring every scientist to disguise calculations as graphics would restrict adoption. Nvidia needed a way to let ordinary technical programmers use the GPU directly.

A forward-looking chip project, NV50, provided the hardware foundation. It would have a compute mode, accessible memory, and support for many concurrent threads of work. Extensions to the widely used C language would spare programmers from relying on a graphics-specific language. Nvidia called the combined approach CUDA, the Compute Unified Device Architecture.

Kim emphasizes that CUDA was not simply software placed on an unchanged chip. John Nickolls, who joined in 2003, helped shape hardware features and mathematical capabilities around the requirements of general computing. Ian Buck, whose Stanford research produced the BrookGPU programming environment, joined in 2004 to lead software work. A small group built driver interfaces, mathematical libraries, and the layers that translate programmers' instructions into executable operations. Hardware and software had to evolve together.

Nickolls remained central until his death from cancer in 2011. Huang later described him as indispensable to bringing CUDA into existence. Including his contribution prevents the platform's origin from becoming a story about one executive's foresight alone.

The next decision concerned distribution. Launching CUDA only on costly professional Quadro products would contain the initial commitment, but could also define it permanently as a specialist tool. Developers would hesitate to write applications without users, while users would hesitate to buy hardware without applications.

Huang chose to put CUDA throughout Nvidia's lineup, including affordable GeForce gaming products. A wide installed base could attract experiments and establish a standard before demand was obvious. Nvidia was effectively paying to create possibilities that most current customers did not need.

The first chip, renamed G80, arrived in November 2006. It combined 128 CUDA computing cores with hardware support for running thousands of threads. Its development took four years and cost 475 million dollars, roughly a third of Nvidia's research and development spending over that period. This was a much longer commitment than an ordinary graphics generation.

The commercial burden persisted after launch. CUDA capability added cost to chips bought primarily for gaming, while the computing market remained small. Nvidia's gross margin fell from 45.6 percent in fiscal 2008 to 35.4 percent in fiscal 2010. The financial crisis also reduced demand for electronics and workstations. Shares fell more than eighty percent between October 2007 and November 2008. Kim presents overlapping pressures, not a clean experiment showing CUDA alone caused the decline.

At an analyst gathering about a year after the launch, Nvidia described a GPU-computing market it expected to grow from almost nothing to more than six billion dollars. Many listeners remained focused on near-term margins. Daniel Ernst asked a different question over lunch: could a GPU prevent his computer from slowing down while he edited photographs of his daughter?

Huang enthusiastically described work with Adobe to accelerate Photoshop. The exchange made the platform's purpose concrete. It did not prove the forecast, but it showed why looking only at the next quarter's cost could miss applications that were not yet commercially established.

Belief was still insufficient. Nvidia had to help people learn what to build. Earlier university relationships had combined donations, teaching hardware, and internships. Those programs served education while also placing Nvidia products in students' hands and identifying potential recruits.

CUDA required a much larger teaching effort. Kirk and David Luebke proposed centers where universities would receive hardware in return for teaching GPU programming. Kirk delivered more than a hundred talks in a year without finding a willing adopter. Faculty had neither an established course nor the spare expertise to invent one simply because a company provided equipment.

At Illinois, department head Richard Blahut proposed a practical answer: Kirk should teach it himself alongside the experienced professor Wen-mei Hwu. Kirk initially resisted, then agreed. In 2007, he flew from Colorado to Illinois every other week. Students completed research projects, and the instructors published recordings and teaching materials for others.

Illinois became the first CUDA Center of Excellence the following year, receiving substantial funding and hardware. Kirk and Hwu also wrote the textbook that appeared in 2010. It eventually reached hundreds of schools. Nvidia was no longer asking professors to create a curriculum from nothing; it was supplying one they could reuse.

Industry adoption required similar attention. Life-sciences business developer Mark Berger provided scientists with cards and foundational software libraries. Researchers wanted to investigate chemistry and biology, not rebuild standard mathematical tools before beginning. Nvidia also convened two-day summits where engineers explained upcoming capabilities and users identified missing ones. Berger recalled more than a dozen hardware or CUDA features arising from such feedback.

Huang connected this effort with his own experience of chip development. Testing designs virtually before fabrication had made engineering faster. Molecular simulation might produce a comparable change in scientific work. The ambition was to improve the process of investigation, not merely sell a faster component.

Ross Walker's work on AMBER showed what that could mean. The software simulates proteins and other biological systems, supporting research into potential medicines. Walker had extensive computational-chemistry experience and access to a major supercomputer center. He also saw how scarce computing time constrained the people without such advantages.

Committees spent minutes assessing proposals that researchers had spent months preparing. Approval rates were in the low single digits. In Walker's view, established scientists received resources partly because earlier access had already allowed them to become established. He recalled being overruled after rejecting an emergency request related to the H1N1 outbreak and interpreted the reversal as political influence. Kim presents this as Walker's experience of the system, rather than independently proving every allocation was unfair.

Walker wanted useful computing power that researchers could own. Custom chips remained too expensive once their surrounding systems were included. PlayStation hardware appeared promising, but Sony's restrictions prevented him from repurposing it. Graphics workstations suggested that ordinary GPUs might provide a programmable alternative.

Early experiments with Brook and Radeon cards ran into immature software. CUDA offered a more workable environment, and Nvidia supplied technical assistance. Walker's team redesigned AMBER to move the computational work onto the GPU rather than treating it as an occasional accessory.

The GPU-enabled release in 2009 ran up to fifty times faster than the preceding version. Researchers could combine several gaming cards in a relatively inexpensive computer instead of competing for access to a large shared machine. AMBER's expanding use illustrated how cheaper computation could change who was able to participate in research, not simply how quickly an established laboratory completed a calculation.

Nvidia celebrated the achievement in its 2010 annual report and recognized Walker as a CUDA Fellow. At the time described in the book, AMBER had more than a thousand university and commercial licenses and appeared in over fifteen hundred academic publications annually. Those figures describe its later reach, not its first year's adoption.

The partnership then exposed a conflict between opening access and charging for value. Nvidia recommended professional Tesla cards costing about two thousand dollars instead of GeForce cards costing around five hundred. It argued that Tesla's error-correction capabilities offered reliability needed for scientific calculations.

Walker disputed their necessity for AMBER. Tests, including work with contacts at Los Alamos, found no relevant errors or advantage from the additional protection. His two-week observations supported his case for this workload; they did not establish that error correction was unnecessary for every scientific application.

Further changes deepened the disagreement. Nvidia changed the mathematical precision available in gaming products in ways that mattered little for pictures but could disrupt simulations. AMBER developers worked around the changes. Restrictions on bulk purchases of GeForce cards also made inexpensive clusters harder to assemble. Walker saw a company that had encouraged broad adoption now steering successful users toward more costly hardware.

Later, at GlaxoSmithKline, he built a cluster using thousands of gaming cards. When Nvidia's health-care leader Kimberly Powell urged him to buy enterprise products, he replied that his responsibility was to choose what best served his employer. Kim leaves the disagreement visible. CUDA could democratize research while the business behind it sought a larger share of the resulting value.

Nvidia's sales culture reinforced that commercial purpose. Derik Moore joined from ATI for almost twice his previous salary, then encountered routine weekend work and even a Christmas Eve sales call. Shared effort, including Huang's, made the demands easier to accept without making them disappear.

Moore increased annual HP sales from sixteen million dollars to 250 million. Yet a successful customer meeting still became a lesson in preparation. HP unexpectedly asked Nvidia to accept unlimited legal liability for failures involving its GPUs in servers. Huang rejected exposure far beyond the value of Nvidia's component and instead offered a limit linked to the customer's annual purchases. HP agreed.

Afterward, Huang criticized Moore for not anticipating the request. A salesperson was expected to understand an account's business deeply enough to lead it, not merely react during negotiations. Nvidia backed sellers with technical engineers who helped customers implement and optimize its products. It resisted commodity pricing and exchanged discounts for something valuable, such as visible branding.

The chapter ends by looking beyond the part's main period to CUDA's later scale. Kim reports millions of developers, hundreds of libraries, thousands of accelerated applications, and roughly five hundred million compatible GPUs. Backwards compatibility protects earlier software investments. Accumulated tools, user knowledge, and debugging reduce the effort required for the next project.

Huang prefers to describe this as a network serving customers rather than a competitive moat. Kim argues it is both. Developers gain real advantages from the shared foundation, but those advantages make moving to another platform costly and risky. The platform's later importance in artificial intelligence grew from years of software investment and collaboration before the demand was obvious. Nvidia had created much more than a versatile chip: it had helped create the skills, applications, and dependencies through which that chip became difficult to replace.

## 9. Tortured into Greatness

By 2010, Nvidia had about 5,700 employees across several continents. The urgency and technical ambition of its beginnings remained, but Huang could no longer reinforce expectations through frequent personal contact with everyone. Kim frames the problem as organizational decay: how could a growing company prevent filtered information, territorial behavior, and disconnected teams from undermining its products?

One answer was more public criticism. Huang argued that correcting a mistake in front of a group allowed everyone to learn at once. He preferred pushing people toward a breakthrough to concluding that they could not improve. His description of this practice gives the chapter its title.

Other accounts are less reassuring. A former executive recalled extended reprimands that seemed unnecessarily punitive. At a 2011 all-hands meeting, Huang repeatedly asked the camera operator to show Tegra 3 project leader Mike Rayfield while criticizing the delayed chip. An observer remembered the episode as profoundly humiliating. Rayfield later responded with humor when asked about it, but left voluntarily within a year of the chip's release, which had arrived nearly eight months late.

The contrast matters. Huang presents exposure as efficient teaching and an expression of refusing to give up on someone. Employees can experience the same encounter as public degradation. Kim preserves the tension rather than assuming that a demanding intention cancels the human effect.

The absence of praise followed the same logic. After a GeForce 256 launch, Dan Vivoli enthusiastically described what had gone well. Huang wanted to know what could have been better. A good result did not excuse stopping the search for improvement. Sales executive Anthony Medeiros recalled Huang applying similarly severe language to himself even after an outstanding quarter. Perpetual dissatisfaction was meant to include the leader, not merely his subordinates.

Criticism alone could not align the company. Looking back at Nvidia's failures, Huang saw organizational causes: a product that did not match customer needs, manufacturing unable to support demand, and teams designing without shared information. Small size had not protected the early company from politics. Growth made the need for a deliberate structure more urgent.

He favored a broad, relatively flat organization rather than a narrow pyramid of approvals. Experienced leaders did not primarily need private career coaching from the CEO. They needed strategic context and access to what other parts of the company knew. Large shared meetings could deliver both.

During the 2010s, roughly forty executives reported directly to Huang; by the book's later account, the number exceeded sixty. He resisted suggestions to install a chief operating officer between himself and this group. Few open-ended one-to-one meetings and many shared discussions were intended to distribute information rather than concentrate it in private relationships.

Former executives described a striking lack of hierarchy in daily communication. Andy Keane might speak with his nominal manager only occasionally while talking with Huang several times a week. As a general manager, he attended board meetings and related gatherings. Oliver Baltuch remembered asking colleagues for something once and seeing it done without repeated requests. Kim uses these recollections to explain how access and responsiveness could counter departmental isolation.

The company also separated the ownership of a mission from the ownership of a permanent department. Conventional divisions can encourage managers to defend head counts, budgets, and status even when a different allocation would benefit customers. Nvidia instead pooled people by function, including engineering, sales, and operations, then assembled the skills needed for particular projects.

Each mission received a named Pilot in Command, directly accountable to Huang and supported by his authority. A vague assurance that a team was handling something was insufficient; responsibility needed an identifiable person. In exchange, the project leader could draw on resources across the company rather than negotiating through competing divisional empires.

Managers were expected to let employees move to new work. Jay Puri described redeploying people instead of repeatedly firing one group and hiring another. That is an account of a staffing principle, not a promise that nobody could lose a job. Its purpose was to reduce the incentive to hoard talent and allow expertise to follow opportunity.

Nor did rejecting a fixed five-year plan mean rejecting long-term ambition. Huang wanted planning to continue as information changed. Decisions and resource commitments, rather than a periodically polished document, would reveal the actual strategy. A project should answer to its purpose and the customer, not simply to the career interests of the manager above it.

This flatter arrangement remained intensely centralized around Huang. He used shared meetings to explain how he compared ideas, considered evidence, and reached decisions. Employees could learn the reasoning rather than merely receive instructions. They were also exposed to the reprimands that accompanied it. Huang regarded embarrassment as a tolerable cost of collective learning, a judgment not all observers shared.

He needed information that large meetings could not supply. Formal status reports often arrived after uncomfortable details had been removed. To hear less-polished observations, he asked employees throughout the company to email their five most important activities and signals from their markets. These Top 5 messages could include customer difficulties, competitors' moves, technological changes, or an approaching delay.

Topic labels made messages searchable across areas such as health care or large cloud customers. Huang read around a hundred each day and more on Sundays, sometimes with a glass of scotch. He wanted clues that were not yet obvious enough to become an executive consensus. Several messages about machine learning, for example, persuaded him that Nvidia should assign more software engineers to its RAPIDS library.

The channel shortened the distance between a junior employee's observation and a resource decision. It also extended the CEO's demands into personal time. Huang answered quickly, expected substantive replies within a day, and challenged assertions unsupported by data. Employees learned that a Friday email could trigger work that consumed the weekend. Some delayed sending their updates until late Sunday.

Mark Berger learned the evidentiary standard through an uncomfortable exchange. His forecast for GPU use in life sciences lacked sufficient direct research. When Huang asked whether he had consulted Ross Walker, Berger admitted that he had not, assuming the academic would lack the relevant practical knowledge. Huang sharply challenged that assumption.

Berger then worked with Walker on a survey. A chance to win a gaming GPU encouraged participation in a questionnaire taking about thirty minutes. They gathered detailed responses from 350 scientists about software, project sizes, desired features, and research backgrounds. This was useful customer information, not a controlled study of every scientist. It nevertheless replaced a confident estimate with evidence from actual users. Berger concluded that admitting an unknown and investigating it was far safer than bluffing.

Whiteboards provided another way to expose reasoning before it hardened into policy. Huang disliked slide presentations that made a completed narrative difficult to question. A blank board forced a speaker to reconstruct the problem, identify assumptions, and explain the connections between them. Others could interrupt, challenge a premise, or develop a competing diagram.

Quarterly gatherings with hundreds of leaders used this method to examine businesses. The point was not simply to review the last quarter's score. Those numbers reflected decisions already made. Discussion should improve the choices that would determine future results, especially strategy and resource allocation. Good performance still invited questions about missed possibilities.

At the end, Huang summarized the ideas and responsibilities the group had agreed on. Shared reasoning was useful only if it produced a common understanding of what happened next. The habit became so important that traveling employees arranged large whiteboards at meeting destinations. Broad markers helped people at the back follow the discussion. Nvidia's later headquarters, built in 2017 and 2022, incorporated extensive whiteboard space, carrying the practice beyond this part's main period.

Kim's concluding claim is that Nvidia matured when it learned to resist its own internal disorder. More revenue or more brilliant employees would not automatically have produced that result. Named responsibility, direct information, and visible reasoning helped the organization revise itself while pursuing long commitments.

The account does not make the experience gentle or the structure independent of its leader. Employees gained access and scope to act while remaining subject to unusually immediate scrutiny. Nvidia's model combined distributed information with a powerful central decision-maker. For Kim, that demanding arrangement explains how the company maintained coordination and adapted quickly enough to survive markets that offered little protection for past success.

## 10. The Engineer's Mind

Kim begins with an encounter that left him wondering whether the person running a technology company understood its technology. As a young analyst, he asked AOL Time Warner chief executive Gerald Levin how the merged company would use AOL's capabilities. The answer wandered through enthusiastic claims about instant messaging without explaining a strategy. For Kim, the exchange exposes a dangerous separation: knowing how to occupy the executive office is not the same as knowing what the business can build.

He connects this to investor Carl Icahn's account of corporate succession. A chief executive may prefer agreeable subordinates who pose little threat. Those people develop relationships with the board and eventually inherit the top job, where they repeat the process. In this interpretation, an organization can become less capable with each promotion even while its leaders remain socially skilled and reassuring.

Kim uses several technology companies to make the consequences concrete. Steve Ballmer could run Microsoft's established business but missed mobile computing and pursued costly acquisitions, including Nokia. Apple's John Sculley initially succeeded at selling increasingly expensive computers, yet the company struggled with product direction and an aging operating system. Buying Steve Jobs's NeXT eventually supplied the foundation for a modern replacement. These are Kim's judgments about particular histories, not evidence that every business-trained executive must fail. His concern is whether a leader can recognize a technological transition before the existing business stops protecting the company.

Intel under Bob Swan provides the fullest contrast. The company was returning billions through buybacks and dividends while manufacturing delays weakened its competitive position. Its AI efforts also lacked a clear center. It bought Nervana, hired graphics executive Raja Koduri, and later spent two billion dollars on Habana. Instead of making those investments reinforce one another, Intel abandoned Nervana near the point of having a product and restarted around Habana. Kim argues that weak technical understanding made it difficult to distinguish a convincing presentation from a coherent engineering plan. When engineer Pat Gelsinger replaced Swan in 2021, one early change was to stop share repurchases.

Huang's advantage, in this account, is not simply having studied engineering. He keeps doing the work needed to understand what his engineers propose. One product manager remembers entering a discussion of a Wi-Fi product and finding the chief executive better prepared on the standards than the people presenting it. The product never shipped, but Huang had still mastered the details. He follows internal technical discussions and forwards research that interests him. At the 2016 NeurIPS conference, a former colleague encountered him despite his having no speaking assignment. Huang had come to attend sessions, meet researchers, and learn.

That knowledge matters because a chip takes years to develop. A leader cannot wait until a market is fully visible to decide what hardware it will need. Technical understanding helps assess whether a proposal is plausible, who should lead it, and how much time and money it deserves.

Yet detailed knowledge can produce its own failure: endless analysis. Huang recalls disliking an Oregon State professor's habit of rounding numbers rather than carrying calculations to several decimal places. Eventually he understood the point. Extra digits do not necessarily improve a decision whose underlying assumptions are uncertain. His later use of rough arithmetic lets him estimate an opportunity's scale quickly, leaving more attention for difficult questions about customers and competition. The aim is useful accuracy, not carelessness.

His communication follows a similar preference for compression, with less uniformly helpful results. Extremely short emails force experienced colleagues to exercise judgment instead of waiting for exhaustive instructions. They also sometimes spend hours debating what he meant before asking for clarification. Bryan Catanzaro sees an important principle behind the brevity: other people's attention is scarce, and a message should make its point without burying the recipient. Still, the time saved by the sender can become work for everyone interpreting it.

The chapter's more troubling form of intensity is the expectation that work should dominate life. One operations executive describes a day stretching from 4:30 in the morning until ten at night. Another manager combines long office hours with late calls to Taiwan and weekend expectations of replying within two hours. Reviewing his calendar, he finds that travel or office work has consumed nearly half his weekends. Some employees leave after a few years rather than sustain that pace.

Huang models the obsession himself. During an early family vacation, colleagues called to persuade him to stop emailing from a balcony and join his children on the beach. He preferred the uninterrupted opportunity to work. He says work relaxes him and does not believe his commitment has deprived him of a fuller life. In a 2024 interview, he accepted the description of himself as demanding. Kim presents that conviction alongside the burdens on employees rather than making the costs disappear. A company built so closely around one person's habits also faces an unresolved question about succession.

The chapter then widens from individual leadership to platform power. IBM's original PC relied on Intel processors and Microsoft software without exclusive arrangements. Cheaper compatible machines followed, eventually driving IBM out of the business, while Intel and Microsoft benefited from the common standard. Once companies had built extensive software around Windows and Intel's architecture, switching meant much more than buying different computers. It meant rewriting systems on which their operations depended.

But an entrenched platform does not eliminate the need for judgment. Microsoft under Satya Nadella moved aggressively toward cloud computing. Intel turned down the iPhone processor opportunity because Apple's proposed price fell below its forecast cost, then sold its ARM-based mobile processor unit. Manufacturing missteps followed. Gelsinger also looks back regretfully on Intel's canceled Larrabee graphics project. Each decision could be defended within an existing business; together they left major new markets to others.

Nvidia instead kept funding CUDA and added Mellanox networking despite pressure to improve near-term financial results. Kim sees the combination of GPUs and software libraries as a possible successor to earlier platforms that captured unusually large shares of industry profits. That is a historical analogy made in his reporting through 2024, not a promise about Nvidia's future returns. His own Intel example establishes the limit: software dependence and past leadership cannot prevent a company from missing the next change. Huang's engineering knowledge matters only if it continues to support learning, investment, and sound decisions.

## 11. The Road to AI

Part IV, Into the Future, covers 2013 through the book's reporting in 2024. Kim opens it by returning to the earlier recruitment and research that made Nvidia's AI turn possible. The transformation was not one sudden insight. It depended on scientists identifying a useful match between a difficult problem and hardware originally built for something else.

By the mid-2000s, chief scientist David Kirk needed relief from Nvidia's exhausting pace. Finding a successor was harder than deciding to step back. His eventual choice, Stanford professor Bill Dally, had spent his career studying how computers could perform work concurrently. Research at Bell Labs, Caltech, MIT, and Stanford had given him both theoretical depth and experience building computing systems. Kirk first involved him as a consultant on the Tesla architecture, then recruited him gradually. Dally joined full-time in 2009, exchanging a distinguished academic position for the opportunity to put his ideas into commercial machines.

The distinction between CPUs and GPUs explains why that expertise became decisive. A CPU is versatile and handles complicated sequences of operations well. A GPU devotes more of its resources to performing many simpler operations at once. More parallel workers help only when the task can be divided productively; they do not make a GPU the best answer to every computing problem.

Nvidia illustrated the difference at a 2008 event with help from the television show Mythbusters. One paintball robot drew a simple blue face by firing repeatedly at different points. A second device fired about eleven hundred paintballs together, producing a recognizable, multicolored Mona Lisa almost instantly. The demonstration exaggerated the contrast for the stage, but made throughput understandable. A machine organized around simultaneous work could be extraordinarily effective on the right problem.

One such problem came from Andrew Ng's work with Google Brain. His team fed a neural network ten million images sampled from YouTube videos without telling it which pictures contained cats. Repeated exposure allowed it to discover recurring visual features, including a representation associated with cat faces. The experiment attracted attention because a useful pattern had emerged without humans labeling every example.

The result required more than a clever algorithm. Google supplied access to enormous amounts of data and retained commercialization rights to discoveries made with it. The computing system used more than two thousand CPUs, containing sixteen thousand cores. That made the experiment an impressive demonstration but a poor starting point for most research groups, which could not afford the machines, building space, electricity, and cooling.

Over breakfast with Ng, Dally recognized a possible alternative. The calculations seemed well suited to GPUs. He assigned Bryan Catanzaro to help, but the solution was not simply to purchase a faster graphics card. The model was too large for one GPU, and distributing it across several servers required software that could coordinate both computation and communication. Catanzaro's team used CUDA to overcome those constraints. Kim reports that twelve GPUs could replace the work of the much larger CPU installation.

This case joins three elements that Dally considered necessary for deep learning: workable algorithms, sufficient data, and affordable computing power. Having any two without the third was not enough. Nvidia's contribution was to make experiments feasible beyond the small number of institutions able to assemble enormous CPU systems.

A separate development showed why that capability might matter beyond a memorable cat demonstration. Beginning in 2007, Fei-Fei Li challenged computer vision's emphasis on devising better algorithms by building a better training resource. Her ImageNet project collected millions of images with human-supplied category labels. When the initial research attracted little attention, she helped establish an annual recognition contest with Oxford. The contest gave researchers a shared task and a visible way to compare results.

Early contest scores improved incrementally. In 2012, a University of Toronto team consisting of Hinton, Ilya Sutskever, and Alex Krizhevsky changed the approach. Instead of writing explicit visual rules for recognizing objects, they trained a neural network to learn useful relationships from ImageNet's labeled examples. This was different from Ng's unlabeled experiment: human-provided categories still supplied the training signal.

Their AlexNet system trained for roughly two weeks on two consumer Nvidia GPUs costing a few hundred dollars each. AlexNet decisively outperformed its competitors. Catanzaro emphasizes the systems achievement. Greater practical computing capacity allowed a larger model and more data to produce results that a more elegant algorithm alone had not delivered.

Huang began asking increasingly detailed questions about the opportunity. Some senior colleagues thought deep learning might be a passing enthusiasm. In 2013, he decided it should become a central priority anyway. Catanzaro remembers an accelerating process over months, not an overnight conversion: questions led to conviction, and conviction led to people being reassigned. Nvidia's ability to move staff across organizational boundaries mattered as much as the announcement.

The company already had an expensive foundation in widespread CUDA compatibility. Now it made that foundation easier for AI developers to use. Catanzaro's optimizations became cuDNN, a library that supplied efficient neural-network operations through leading AI frameworks. Researchers could benefit from specialized GPU routines without rebuilding every low-level component themselves.

Hardware changed too. Scientific users often needed highly precise arithmetic, but neural-network training could tolerate less precision in suitable calculations. Supporting sixteen-bit floating-point operations in 2016 reduced unnecessary work. The point was not that accuracy no longer mattered, but that spending resources on more numerical precision than a workload needed could make the whole system slower.

The more difficult decision concerned Volta, a chip architecture already well into development. Dally and the GPU team proposed dedicated Tensor Cores for the matrix calculations central to deep learning. Adding them consumed valuable chip area and required changes only months before the design was due to be finalized for manufacturing. Waiting would have been safer for the current schedule, but could have postponed specialized AI hardware for years. Nvidia accepted the late disruption. Dally reports that the resulting hardware could train a model about three times faster than the equivalent design using standard CUDA cores.

Kim's larger point is that Nvidia built an environment around the chips. Libraries, programming tools, suitable arithmetic, and specialized circuits reinforced one another. Researchers who moved from universities into Google, Baidu, OpenAI, and other commercial organizations brought experience with that environment. Their familiarity helped turn academic adoption into business demand.

The outcome was neither inevitable nor solely Huang's invention. Kirk's recruitment, Dally's recognition of the workload, Catanzaro's software, Li's dataset, and the Toronto experiment all supplied essential evidence. Huang's contribution was to respond to those signals with coordinated resources before the commercial payoff was certain. Nvidia was preparing for a market whose eventual scale none of the participants yet knew.

## 12. The "Most Feared" Hedge Fund

The investment firm in this chapter initially looks like a threat to the patient research that made Nvidia successful. Starboard Value built its reputation by pressing underperforming companies to change strategy, leadership, and spending. Yet Kim traces a more complicated relationship. Its intervention at Nvidia stopped well short of the confrontation executives feared, while its later campaign at Mellanox helped make a crucial acquisition available.

Jeff Smith established Starboard as an independent fund in 2011 after working in banking and investment management. By 2014, it had replaced more than eighty directors at thirty companies. Its best-known campaign targeted Darden Restaurants, whose brands included Olive Garden and Red Lobster. Darden blamed rising seafood costs for problems at Red Lobster and planned to sell the chain. Smith instead blamed management and argued that a sale would destroy shareholder value.

Starboard's roughly three-hundred-slide turnaround proposal became famous partly for its discussion of unlimited breadsticks. What looked like trivial penny-pinching had an operational argument behind it: bringing smaller quantities could create more contact between servers and guests. Kim treats the broader proposal as detailed and serious, not simply hostile financial engineering. Shareholders eventually replaced Darden's entire twelve-person board, and its chief executive departed. The case explains why another management team would be anxious to discover Smith on its shareholder register.

Nvidia faced that prospect in 2013. Its shares had gone roughly nowhere for four years. In the latest reported quarter, revenue had increased 7 percent while earnings had fallen 2 percent. About three billion dollars of net cash sat inside a company valued at roughly eight billion dollars. To Starboard, that combination suggested a business whose assets were worth more than its market price recognized. The fund accumulated 4.4 million shares, worth around sixty-two million dollars.

Some Nvidia executives feared a campaign to replace directors and curtail CUDA investment. One recalls resisting a request for a board seat. Smith's later description is narrower: Starboard wanted substantial share repurchases and less emphasis on projects outside GPUs, including phone processors. Kim preserves the distinction between what employees worried might happen and what Smith says he advocated. The chapter does not establish that dismantling CUDA was an actual demand.

Meetings with Huang and other leaders remained manageable rather than becoming a public battle. Nvidia subsequently committed to a billion dollars in repurchases by fiscal 2015 and authorized another billion dollars. The stock rose around 20 percent over the following months, and Starboard had sold its position by March 2014. Smith remembers being impressed by Huang; Huang remembers the encounters without much detail. What seemed potentially existential inside Nvidia became a brief shareholder episode.

Starboard's next connection to Nvidia was indirect. Mellanox supplied high-speed networking equipment, including technology based on the InfiniBand standard. Its revenue grew from five hundred million dollars in 2012 to eight hundred fifty-eight million in 2016, but heavy research spending left thin margins. After taking an 11 percent stake in 2017, Starboard argued that Mellanox's stock performance and profitability were far below what shareholders should expect.

The pressure produced a compromise in June 2018. Mellanox added three Starboard-approved directors and accepted further contingent rights tied to undisclosed financial targets. That did not entirely remove the threat of a challenge to chief executive Eyal Waldman. Selling the company offered another route.

A nonbinding offer of one hundred two dollars a share arrived that September, compared with a market price below seventy-seven dollars. Mellanox sought additional bidders. Nvidia joined the process in October, after Huang recognized how important the asset could become. It was not an acquisition he had been quietly orchestrating from the beginning.

The final contest involved Nvidia, Intel, and Xilinx. Nvidia offered one hundred twenty-five dollars a share, slightly above its rivals, and won in March 2019 with a cash bid of 6.9 billion dollars. The strategic logic went beyond adding another profitable product. Large AI workloads would require many servers to cooperate, and powerful GPUs could not deliver their full value if data moved too slowly between them. Networking performance would help determine the performance of the entire installation. Huang was treating the data center, rather than the individual chip, as the relevant computing system.

By May 2024, Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of 3.2 billion dollars from the former Mellanox business, more than seven times its final independent quarterly revenue. That implied an annualized revenue rate above twelve billion dollars, although revenue is not profit or a measure of how quickly an acquisition has paid for itself. CoreWeave's technology chief, speaking as an Nvidia customer, also described InfiniBand as important for limiting delays and congestion in large GPU installations.

Kim calls attention to both opportunity and execution. Activist pressure helped create the sale, but Nvidia still had to recognize the asset's value, win the auction, and integrate it into a larger system. The episode complicates a simple contrast between visionary engineers and shortsighted investors. Starboard could be an uncomfortable shareholder and an indirect contributor to Nvidia's strongest strategic position. Smith's later regret was that his fund had sold Nvidia so early, an irony of this particular history rather than a general rule for investors.

## 13. Lighting the Future

Lighting can make a technically detailed computer image look convincing or immediately artificial. Surfaces must reflect, absorb, and scatter light in ways viewers recognize without thinking. Reproducing those interactions takes far more computation than simply placing objects on a screen. Kim uses Nvidia's long effort to improve lighting to explain how a company known for speed learned to protect work that might not become useful for years.

David Luebke encountered Nvidia's speed from the losing side. As a university researcher, he sometimes saw a new GPU feature overtake his team's graphics technique before its academic paper finished peer review. When chief scientist David Kirk invited him to help establish Nvidia Research in 2006, joining offered a chance to shape the advances that had repeatedly made his own work obsolete.

What the new group should do was initially unclear. Nvidia had survived through disciplined execution and short product cycles, not an open-ended search for inventions. Kirk nevertheless believed some important problems would never be solved within the ordinary development schedule. He asked the researchers to choose work that mattered to the company, could make a substantial difference, and would not happen without dedicated long-term effort.

Ray tracing fit that description. It simulates light interacting with objects in a scene, offering more realistic reflections, illumination, and shadows. The established argument, promoted by Intel researchers, was that such complicated calculations belonged on flexible CPUs rather than GPUs. Within six months, Nvidia Research had experimental evidence suggesting GPUs could perform them faster.

Huang listened to the team's first presentation with unusual patience. He saw potential in games, but directed their attention toward professional customers too. Quadro workstation products sold in relatively small numbers yet, according to the chapter, generated nearly 80 percent of Nvidia's profit at the time. Making expensive design and visualization work faster could establish a business before ordinary gamers could use the technology.

Winning support did not mean the researchers could redesign chips on intuition. At a meeting about the Fermi architecture, they proposed changes to how computing threads ran on a core. GPU engineering leader Jonah Alben considered the idea plausible but demanded data. The researchers spent the following year developing algorithms and demonstrations that could justify the cost. The episode established a productive boundary: speculative ideas were welcome, but incorporating them into commercial hardware required evidence.

Acquiring Mental Images and RayScale added specialized expertise. RayScale's team then helped build a demonstration for the 2008 SIGGRAPH conference: a sports car moving through a city, with curved reflections, crisp shadows, and other lighting effects. Intel employees asked whether it really ran on a GPU. The demonstration challenged their assumptions, but it proved GPU feasibility, not that an affordable consumer card could already reproduce complex scenes in real time.

In 2009, Nvidia released OptiX, a programmable ray-tracing engine for Quadro users. Applications extended beyond attractive pictures to industrial design and radiation research. Successful research moved into the main business along with people able to commercialize it. Nvidia Research could nurture an idea without becoming its permanent organizational home.

Gaming remained out of reach. In 2013, Kirk asked Luebke to revisit the problem, and Luebke circulated an ambitious question: what would a hundredfold improvement in ray-tracing efficiency make possible, and how could Nvidia achieve it? This was not an ordinary incremental upgrade. It required new algorithms and dedicated hardware.

Researchers in Helsinki, led by Timo Aila and supported by architect Erik Lindholm, became central to the effort. American engineers joined the collaboration in 2014. By 2016, the research was sufficiently developed to transfer fully to engineering, but it had missed the window for Pascal. Dedicated ray-tracing cores finally arrived with Turing in 2018, a decade after the original demonstration. Luebke describes his own contribution partly as protecting the team and ensuring it kept receiving support.

A second technology emerged from preparations for Turing's public introduction. Researcher Aaron Lefohn proposed showing AI-assisted anti-aliasing, a method of cleaning up distracting edges in images. Huang wanted a more compelling benefit. Instead of making an already good picture slightly better, could AI help a card produce a higher-resolution image without doing all the usual rendering work?

That reframing became deep-learning super sampling, or DLSS. A card could render at a lower resolution, then use a trained model to reconstruct additional detail. The commercial attraction was straightforward: customers might get something closer to high-end visual performance from less rendering work. The researchers supplied the technical possibility; Huang identified a different way to turn it into a product. Promising early results entered his presentation only days before the keynote.

The launch did not reward the excitement. Turing-based GeForce RTX cards offered little improvement in ordinary frame rates over Pascal, and enabling ray tracing reduced frame rates by about a quarter. With early DLSS enabled, performance could be roughly 40 percent faster than Pascal, but the picture looked worse. It also required laborious training for individual games. Nvidia had created features developers and customers would have to adopt before the hardware's full value became apparent.

Rather than abandon the effort, teams revised it. Catanzaro helped develop DLSS 2.0, introduced in March 2020, which no longer needed separate custom training for every game and earned better reviews. DLSS 3.0 generated intermediate frames using relationships between surrounding rendered frames. Catanzaro describes six years of work to build a sufficiently accurate model for frame generation. Continuing improvement justified continuing investment, even when the work ran far beyond a normal academic project or product cycle.

By the time of Ampere, the combination of better hardware and more mature software made ray tracing and DLSS much more persuasive. Kim connects those features to Nvidia's differentiation: conventional price-to-performance comparisons could favor AMD while customers still valued capabilities that worked particularly well on Nvidia products. His reporting through 2024 cites roughly 80 percent of the discrete GPU market and adoption of the features in hundreds of games. Those observations support the importance of differentiation, but do not isolate its effect from every other reason customers chose Nvidia.

The organizational change is as important as the technology. Early Nvidia could barely afford to do anything beyond finishing the next chip. A larger company had the financial room to explore, tolerate embarrassment, and wait for evidence to improve. That did not mean research needed no purpose. The purpose shifted from meeting an immediate profitability deadline to creating a capability people might eventually want.

Kim contrasts this with the frustration of a Google Transformer-paper coauthor who felt bureaucracy blocked access to resources and effective work. Discovery alone does not ensure that a company will turn an invention into a useful offering. Nvidia's research organization, about three hundred people under Bill Dally at the time of reporting, linked exploration to engineering and commercialization. Kim sees this as an answer to the innovator's dilemma: protect the existing business without letting it veto the next one. The decade-long experiments and disappointing first products explain the achievement more fully than any claim that Nvidia simply invented successful features on command.

## 14. The Big Bang

On May 24, 2023, professional trader Connors Manguino waited for Nvidia's earnings release with his attention fixed on a terminal. His livelihood depended on reacting to news before the opportunity disappeared. He knew ChatGPT had created extraordinary interest in AI, but he did not know how much of that enthusiasm would become Nvidia revenue.

There was reason to distinguish excitement from established financial performance. Data centers accounted for about 55 percent of Nvidia's sales in the fiscal year ending January 2023, but part of that larger share came from a sharp decline in gaming revenue. Nvidia's years of AI preparation had not yet produced the explosion observers would soon assume had been inevitable.

Twenty-one minutes after the market closed, the company forecast eleven billion dollars in revenue for the next quarter, compared with expectations of about 7.18 billion. Manguino hesitated at the size of the difference. Automated traders did not. By the time he recovered, Nvidia shares had already surged, and he bought AMD shares instead, hoping a competitor might benefit from the same wave.

Analyst Stacy Rasgon described the announcement as a big bang. Other investors compared it with landmark reports from Google and Facebook and found the scale exceptional. The following day, Nvidia's shares rose 24 percent, adding 184 billion dollars in market value. This was the market's reaction to a forecast, not revenue already collected, but the forecast revealed demand far beyond the industry's previous assumptions.

At Computex the following week, Huang used that attention to promote a larger computing system. The DGX GH200 linked 256 GPUs, thirty-two times the number in the previous model. His sales argument paired expanding capability with falling cost per unit of useful computation. Buying a much larger system could still be economical if it delivered disproportionately more work.

The broader pitch was more urgent: companies that failed to adopt generative AI risked falling behind. Huang viewed neural networks as tools that could learn useful relationships across many kinds of structured problems, from language to recommendation systems. That was his expansive interpretation of the technology's potential, not evidence that every proposed application would work or justify its price.

Demand nevertheless translated into an extraordinary expansion. Kim cites a subsequent quarter with 22.6 billion dollars in data-center revenue, up 427 percent from a year earlier. Unlike a software business serving another copy at little incremental cost, Nvidia had to manufacture and deliver complicated physical systems. Some contained as many as thirty-five thousand parts.

The response extended outside Nvidia's own offices. Teams worked with manufacturers including Foxconn, Wistron, and TSMC to add equipment, expand space, automate testing, and secure advanced packaging. They examined production delays rather than accepting normal lead times as fixed. Factories placed buffers between stages to absorb upstream problems, but those buffers also left machinery and materials waiting. Earlier quality checks could reduce the chance of defects reaching later stages and make some of that waiting unnecessary.

These improvements required spending, including spending on partners' capabilities. They also exported Nvidia's relentless pace to its suppliers. The company wanted to move AI chips from a two-year release cycle toward an annual one. Kim's explanation is therefore less magical than the market reaction: recognizing demand mattered, but meeting it required expensive, detailed work throughout a manufacturing network.

Nvidia had also prepared for a particular kind of demand. Google's 2017 Transformer research introduced an attention mechanism that helped models weigh relationships in context and supported training at scale. Huang discussed the architecture publicly within months and pushed for optimized support. Nvidia developed the Transformer Engine around its Tensor Core hardware, incorporating it into Hopper, which arrived in 2022 near ChatGPT's launch.

The timing mattered because software support and chip design could not be improvised after orders surged. Nvidia's reported training-speed improvements came from its own testing, but the strategic point is independent of any single benchmark. It offered a coordinated combination of chips, libraries, servers, and networking. Rivals developing AI processors had to compete with that whole environment, not just match the specifications of one component.

Another source of advantage was pricing. Michael Hara, who joined from S3, initially expected graphics chips to behave like commodities. With S3 chips selling for around five dollars, he suggested charging at most ten for RIVA 128. Huang chose fifteen, and the product sold out. Later chips commanded thirty-two dollars and then sixty-five. Hara came to understand the strategy as charging for an improvement customers could recognize rather than accepting the usual downward pressure on component prices.

Graphics provided a durable version of that opportunity because more realistic images kept demanding more computing power. AI created another: more capable models and more ambitious tasks increased the appetite for hardware even as each generation became more efficient. Lower cost per useful calculation could coexist with a higher price for an entire product.

Those prices also reveal the distance between technical possibility and practical access. Kim describes consumer cards above two thousand dollars and AI servers costing hundreds of thousands. Researcher Ross Walker remembers the shock of hearing a price of one hundred forty-nine thousand dollars for the DGX-1. By the book's 2024 reporting, a seventy-two-GPU Blackwell GB200 rack could cost two to three million dollars. What once resembled the cost of a used car could now resemble the cost of a house or more.

Kim does not claim that Huang foresaw the precise date or size of the AI boom. Nvidia initially committed limited resources, then expanded after researchers supplied persuasive signals. His comparison is with Netflix's Reed Hastings: believing that streaming would eventually be important did not mean knowing exactly when the transition would become commercially possible. The skill was preparing for a direction while remaining alert to changes in timing.

Huang's proposed next destination was digital biology. He and health-care executive Kimberly Powell compared computational drug design with the software tools that enabled increasingly complex chip design. Better algorithms, enough computing power, and domain knowledge might let researchers design molecules more systematically instead of relying so heavily on trial and error.

Generate:Biomedicines makes that prospect concrete. Cofounder Gevorg Grigoryan studied statistical regularities in proteins as an academic, but the computing resources needed for a larger machine-learning effort exceeded what he could obtain there. His company used information from millions of proteins to help design new molecular structures and protein-based drugs.

Nvidia was already familiar infrastructure for that work. Molecular scientists had long repurposed gaming GPUs, and Grigoryan later adopted PyTorch, whose strong CUDA integration made Nvidia hardware the straightforward default. The case illustrates how research tools can shape purchasing decisions years later without a fresh contest between chip brands.

The medical ambition still needs careful separation from the accomplishment. Designing molecules and modeling their behavior inside cells are not the same as simulating an entire human body. Grigoryan's eventual hope was software that could take a disease and propose a curative molecule. Kim presents that as a scientist's aspiration, not a demonstrated cure or an assurance that clinical development has become predictable.

A nearer business opportunity lay in information companies already possessed. Emails, documents, presentations, and internal records often remained difficult to search. Huang envisioned converting their contents into numerical representations linked to language models, so employees could ask questions conversationally rather than guess filenames and directory paths.

Kim recalls spending hours or days locating old consulting documents and sees applications such as ChatRTX as a way to compress that work. The appeal includes synthesizing information across records, not merely retrieving a file. His enthusiasm explains the use case, but does not establish that every generated answer will be accurate or that every installation will achieve the same productivity gains.

The spending forecasts in the chapter belong to its reporting period, not to the present. Goldman Sachs projected more than three trillion dollars in possible AI-related cost reductions over a decade. Nvidia management envisioned a large installed base of CPU-powered data-center infrastructure moving toward GPUs. A mid-2024 J.P. Morgan survey of 166 chief information officers, overseeing 123 billion dollars in annual technology spending, found plans for AI hardware budgets to rise more than 40 percent annually over three years. Respondents expected AI's share of IT budgets to increase from about 5 percent to more than 14 percent by 2027.

Those intentions had tradeoffs. About one-third of the surveyed executives planned to take money from other projects, including infrastructure, legacy-system upgrades, and internal application development. Anticipated AI benefits were competing with existing organizational needs, not arriving without opportunity costs. Spending plans also were not completed purchases or proof of the projected savings. Huang cast AI more broadly as a means of improving opportunities for people previously left behind. That social ambition went beyond the corporate spending evidence presented here.

The chapter closes with a vulnerability behind the boom. Larger models, more data, and more computation might eventually produce diminishing improvements. If customers needed less additional training hardware, demand could stumble. Nvidia said in early 2024 that roughly 60 percent of its data-center GPUs were sold for training and 40 percent for inference, the work of producing answers from trained models. That split made the pace of training investment particularly important.

Kim did not know when diminishing returns might matter, and his confidence that Nvidia could adapt rests on its history rather than certainty about future demand. The extraordinary market reaction is the chapter's opening, not its final explanation. The deeper story is how accumulated research, software adoption, supplier capacity, and commercial judgment made Nvidia ready for a boom it could neither schedule nor guarantee would continue.

## Conclusion: The Nvidia Way

Kim returns to the company as an organization rather than a collection of successful chips. Huang works through shared meeting rooms instead of a private office, keeping himself in the flow of discussion. Employees have considerable freedom over execution, but not freedom to invent incompatible objectives. The CEO repeatedly explains the strategy so that independence does not become fragmentation.

The first ingredient is talent, acquired with unusual speed and persistence. When Creative Labs closed a graphics-engineering operation in Huntsville, Intel tried recruiting its people but wanted them to relocate. Nvidia offered to keep them together in Alabama and opened an office there. It removed a practical obstacle rather than assuming a prestigious offer would overcome people's family commitments.

In Pune, Nvidia executives arrived to investigate an acquisition and found the target company announcing its dissolution. They changed the plan overnight, securing approval to hire the employees directly and printing offer packages in a hotel business center. Fifty-one of fifty-four accepted. The team became the foundation of a much larger engineering office. Kim uses the episode to show how clear authority and quick decisions can preserve an opportunity that a normal acquisition process would lose.

Recruiting also required complementary abilities. David Kirk assembled architects who were exceptional at different parts of increasingly complex systems. John Montrym's overall architectural understanding and Walt Donovan's expertise in textures were useful together, not redundant trophies. Dwight Diercks's recruitment illustrates another side of commitment: when his former employer threatened legal action, Huang did not withdraw Nvidia's offer. Diercks saw a leader prepared to stand behind the people he wanted.

The second ingredient is retention. Stock awards give employees a financial interest in the platform's long-term success. Recurring grants reduce the incentive to leave when an initial package finishes vesting, while special awards recognize unusually important contributions. Huang pays close attention to these decisions and can reward someone without waiting for the annual cycle.

Kim reports turnover below three percent in fiscal 2024, compared with an industry figure of thirteen percent. That contrast is descriptive, not a controlled demonstration that a particular management practice caused retention. A rapidly appreciating stock price is itself a powerful reason to stay. Employees also describe compassionate support during family illness or personal emergencies, suggesting that demanding work did not always mean inflexibility about circumstances.

A third ingredient is the chance to do consequential work alongside strong colleagues without constantly maneuvering for status. Kim contrasts that aspiration with the organizations Nvidia veterans found elsewhere. At Nvidia, an employee who is falling behind is expected to ask for help early. Sales leaders can mobilize engineers, peers, or Huang himself. Concealing trouble until it is too late is worse than needing assistance. Shared responsibility makes the demand for results different from simply leaving individuals to sink or swim.

None of this removes the cost of the system. Kim describes sixty-hour weeks as a baseline and eighty-hour stretches during critical development periods. Employees must remain visible, answer difficult questions, and maintain communication beyond the formal reporting chain. Huang may remember a comparatively junior employee's background in impressive detail; the same attention also means there is little room to disappear.

Continuous learning matters as much as exertion. Executives returning from a trade show or business trip can expect Huang to ask what they learned. His insistence on listening to a question, understanding it, and actually answering it resists rehearsed language that obscures a decision. Kim presents directness as a way to save time and reduce misunderstanding, while acknowledging how alarming the questioning can feel.

The conclusion does not resolve the founder problem. The organization's speed depends on a CEO who understands technology, strategy, people, and day-to-day operations deeply enough to connect them. Those strengths also make him a concentration of risk. Kim cannot identify a proven mechanism that guarantees the same coherence after Huang steps back.

Chance remains in the story too. Different career choices by the cofounders, a failed rescue, or a different outcome for an early chip could have changed the industry. Priem even argues that NV1's failure forced the learning that made RIVA 128 possible. That is a revealing interpretation, not a counterfactual anyone can prove. Kim's final account balances these contingencies with decades of deliberate work: Nvidia's success was neither inevitable nor merely lucky, and its culture is inseparable from the difficult choices and human effort that sustained it.

## Appendix: Jensen-isms

The appendix collects Huang's recurring expressions. Their importance lies in the behavior they are meant to trigger, not in their appeal as motivational quotations. Taken together, they make a compact operating vocabulary for the practices described throughout the book.

One group concerns scarce attention. Meetings should include the people whose knowledge is needed, without consuming everyone else's time. Resources should match the task rather than an arbitrary appearance of austerity. Strategy requires consciously declining opportunities, even attractive ones, so that the most important work can receive enough energy.

Another group concerns how people think together. Direct criticism and vigorous debate are meant to improve an argument. Intellectual honesty requires admitting failure instead of protecting an earlier decision. Returning to first principles means testing the actual constraints, not inheriting assumptions from the previous project. Measurement only helps if the chosen measure corresponds to the outcome that matters. When explanations become evasive, the instruction is to listen, understand, and answer.

Several expressions concern execution. The customer-serving mission should outrank organizational status. A designated project leader needs practical help across departments, while people in trouble must request assistance before a problem becomes unrecoverable. A large ambition becomes manageable by identifying and completing the next concrete step. Confidence is useful when it leads to committed action, not when it substitutes for evidence.

The technical sayings carry more specific meanings than their imagery suggests. Recovering value from an imperfect chip means designing redundancy and selling functional parts at a lower performance level rather than discarding every chip with a small defect. Seeking the physical limit of performance means asking what is theoretically possible before celebrating a modest improvement over last year's result.

Finally, the appendix emphasizes hiring and comparing against outstanding external standards, looking for early evidence that a new project is gaining traction, and concentrating on the quality of work rather than fluctuations in the stock price. Market creation expresses the preference for enabling a new capability rather than merely dividing an existing business.

These are contextual rules of thumb, not independently established laws. The book's preceding cases show why they developed and where they can conflict: speed must coexist with reliability, conviction with willingness to change, and bluntness with the support needed to make demanding work sustainable.

# 🇳🇴 Norway: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~4 min** · read once, revisit often

Norwegian **ordtak** (proverbs) come from fjords, mountain farms, fishing
villages, and a long, dark winter. Some are ancient and shared with Swedish and
Danish; others, such as the hiking slogan below, are modern but just as widely
used. The *Hávamál*, an Old Norse poem of practical advice attributed to the god
Odin, shows that this habit of terse wisdom is very old.

Each entry gives Norwegian Bokmål, a literal translation, and the usual meaning.
Notes describe common readings, not a fixed national character.

## The outdoors and friluftsliv

1. **Ut på tur, aldri sur.**

   *Literally:* "Out on a trip, never grumpy." A rhyming slogan for the
   Norwegian love of hiking. On weekends, cities empty toward forests,
   mountains, and cabins (*hytter*).

2. **Det finnes ikke dårlig vær, bare dårlige klær.**

   *Literally:* "There's no bad weather, only bad clothes." Shared across
   Scandinavia; in Norway it is practically a parenting rule.

3. **Nordmenn er født med ski på beina.**

   *Literally:* "Norwegians are born with skis on their feet." A proud joke
   about a nation that has long dominated Winter Olympic medal tables.

4. **Bak skyene er himmelen alltid blå.**

   *Literally:* "Behind the clouds, the sky is always blue." Hope through hard
   times, useful in a land where winter sun is scarce.

## Modesty and Janteloven

5. **Du skal ikke tro at du er noe.**

   *Literally:* "You shall not think you are anything." The first rule of
   **Janteloven**, the "Law of Jante," from Aksel Sandemose's 1933 novel *En
   flyktning krysser sitt spor* (*A Fugitive Crosses His Tracks*). Sandemose
   satirized a small town that crushed anyone who stood out. Scandinavians
   now use the term to describe, and often criticize, social pressure against
   bragging.

6. **Man skal ikke skue hunden på hårene.**

   *Literally:* "Don't judge the dog by its hair." Don't judge by appearances.
   Danish shares it.

7. **Alt er ikke gull som glimrer.**

   *Literally:* "Not everything that glitters is gold."

## Community and shared work

8. **Mange hender gjør arbeidet lett.**

   *Literally:* "Many hands make the work light." This captures **dugnad**, the
   tradition of unpaid community work: neighbors cleaning a shared yard,
   parents painting a sports club, or a whole country pulling together. During
   the COVID-19 pandemic, the prime minister called on Norwegians to join a
   "national dugnad."

9. **Mange bekker små gjør en stor å.**

   *Literally:* "Many small brooks make a big river." Small contributions add
   up.

10. **Kjært barn har mange navn.**

    *Literally:* "A dear child has many names." Something important or loved
    gets many names or nicknames.

## Patience, caution, and habit

11. **Bedre føre var enn etter snar.**

    *Literally:* "Better cautious before than quick after." Better safe than
    sorry.

12. **Gammel vane er vond å vende.**

    *Literally:* "An old habit is hard to turn." Old habits die hard.

13. **Liten tue kan velte stort lass.**

    *Literally:* "A small tussock can overturn a big load." Small problems can
    cause big trouble; Swedish has the same image.

14. **Sakte men sikkert.**

    *Literally:* "Slowly but surely."

15. **Å gå som katten rundt grøten**

    *Literally:* "To go like the cat around the porridge." To avoid the real
    issue.

16. **Den som venter på noe godt, venter ikke forgjeves.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever waits for something good does not wait in vain."

## Courage, time, and work

17. **Den som intet våger, intet vinner.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever dares nothing wins nothing." Norway's history of
    seafaring, from Viking ships to polar explorers such as Fridtjof Nansen and
    Roald Amundsen, gives this saying extra weight.

18. **Morgenstund har gull i munn.**

    *Literally:* "The morning hour has gold in its mouth." Shared with German.

19. **Først til mølla får først malt.**

    *Literally:* "First to the mill gets ground first." First come, first served.

20. **Alle gode ting er tre.**

    *Literally:* "All good things are three." Things come in threes, or "third
    time's the charm."

## Home

21. **Borte bra, men hjemme best.**

    *Literally:* "Away is good, but home is best."

22. **Det ordner seg.**

    *Literally:* "It sorts itself out." A calm everyday reassurance: don't
    panic, things will work out.

## What the collection suggests

Norwegian sayings center on **nature as a shared good** (get outside, dress for
it), **equality and modesty** (Janteloven, don't judge the dog by its hair), and
**collective effort** (dugnad). The tone is steady and unhurried: it sorts
itself out, slowly but surely.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Friluftsliv, Hávamál, and Jante caveats (optional)</summary>

**Friluftsliv** ("open-air life") was popularized by Henrik Ibsen in his poem
*På Vidderne*, written in 1859, (*On the Heights*). It means time in nature for its own sake,
not competitive sport.

The *Hávamál* includes advice such as being a moderate guest, keeping your wits
when drinking, and valuing reputation, which "never dies." Its verses predate
modern Norway but are often quoted as part of the shared Nordic heritage.

Janteloven is a literary satire, not an actual law or folk proverb. Calling
Scandinavians "Jante people" can be an overgeneralization; many Norwegians use
the term specifically to criticize the attitude.

</details>

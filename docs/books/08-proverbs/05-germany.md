# 🇩🇪 Germany: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~5 min** · read once, revisit often

German **Sprichwörter** (proverbs) and **Redewendungen** (idioms) are often
rhymed and blunt. Many were collected and spread by Martin Luther, whose Bible
translation and sermons drew on everyday speech, and by the Brothers Grimm, who
also documented the language in their great dictionary.

Each entry gives the German, a literal translation, and the usual meaning.
Notes describe common readings, not a fixed national character.

## Order, planning, and reliability

1. **Ordnung ist das halbe Leben.**

   *Literally:* "Order is half of life." Tidiness and organization are not
   fussiness but the basis of a good life. Germans often say it with a wink.

2. **Wer A sagt, muss auch B sagen.**

   *Literally:* "Whoever says A must also say B." Finish what you start and
   accept its consequences. Shared with Dutch.

3. **Wer nicht kommt zur rechten Zeit, der muss sehen, was übrig bleibt.**

   *Literally:* "Whoever doesn't come at the right time must see what's left
   over." Latecomers get the leftovers. A rhyming nudge toward punctuality.

4. **Pünktlichkeit ist die Höflichkeit der Könige.**

   *Literally:* "Punctuality is the politeness of kings." Usually credited to
   France's King Louis XVIII, but Germans adopted it as their own. Being on time
   shows respect.

5. **Wer zuerst kommt, mahlt zuerst.**

   *Literally:* "Whoever comes first grinds first." From medieval mills, where
   farmers' grain was milled in order of arrival. First come, first served.

6. **Vertrauen ist gut, Kontrolle ist besser.**

   *Literally:* "Trust is good; control is better." Verify, don't assume. Often
   attributed to Lenin, though that attribution is not documented. Germans may
   use it seriously in business or ironically about bureaucracy.

## Work, effort, and rest

7. **Erst die Arbeit, dann das Vergnügen.**

   *Literally:* "First the work, then the pleasure." Duty before fun. The
   flip side is a strong respect for **Feierabend**, the end of the working day,
   after which work calls and emails are often unwelcome.

8. **Ohne Fleiß kein Preis.**

   *Literally:* "Without diligence, no prize." No pain, no gain.

9. **Übung macht den Meister.**

   *Literally:* "Practice makes the master." *Meister* is also the title of a
   certified master craftsperson in Germany's apprenticeship system, so the
   proverb sounds like a career path.

10. **Wer rastet, der rostet.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever rests, rusts." Keep active in body and mind.

11. **Morgenstund hat Gold im Mund.**

    *Literally:* "The morning hour has gold in its mouth." The early bird
    catches the worm. Scandinavian languages share it.

12. **Aller Anfang ist schwer.**

    *Literally:* "Every beginning is hard."

13. **Gut Ding will Weile haben.**

    *Literally:* "A good thing wants time." Quality can't be rushed.

## Thrift and small things

14. **Wer den Pfennig nicht ehrt, ist des Talers nicht wert.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever doesn't honor the penny isn't worth the thaler." The
    Dutch say nearly the same. Germans are known for saving rather than
    borrowing, and cash remained popular long after cards spread elsewhere.

15. **Spare in der Zeit, so hast du in der Not.**

    *Literally:* "Save in time, and you'll have in need."

16. **Kleinvieh macht auch Mist.**

    *Literally:* "Small livestock make manure too." Small amounts add up.

## Honesty, words, and consequences

17. **Lügen haben kurze Beine.**

    *Literally:* "Lies have short legs." They don't get far before being caught.

18. **Reden ist Silber, Schweigen ist Gold.**

    *Literally:* "Speaking is silver, silence is gold."

19. **Wer anderen eine Grube gräbt, fällt selbst hinein.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever digs a pit for others falls into it themselves."
    Biblical roots in Proverbs 26:27; Scandinavians use it too.

20. **Hunde, die bellen, beißen nicht.**

    *Literally:* "Dogs that bark don't bite." Loud threats rarely become action.

21. **Viele Köche verderben den Brei.**

    *Literally:* "Many cooks spoil the porridge." Too many people in charge ruin
    a project.

## Patience and perspective

22. **Es wird nichts so heiß gegessen, wie es gekocht wird.**

    *Literally:* "Nothing is eaten as hot as it's cooked." Things usually turn
    out less severe than first feared.

23. **Man soll den Tag nicht vor dem Abend loben.**

    *Literally:* "Don't praise the day before the evening." Don't celebrate too
    early.

24. **Ende gut, alles gut.**

    *Literally:* "End good, all good." All's well that ends well.

25. **Ein Unglück kommt selten allein.**

    *Literally:* "A misfortune rarely comes alone."

26. **Not macht erfinderisch.**

    *Literally:* "Need makes inventive." Necessity is the mother of invention.

## Family, upbringing, and tolerance

27. **Was Hänschen nicht lernt, lernt Hans nimmermehr.**

    *Literally:* "What little Hans doesn't learn, Hans will never learn." Learn
    young. Like Korea's "habits at three last until eighty."

28. **Der Apfel fällt nicht weit vom Stamm.**

    *Literally:* "The apple doesn't fall far from the trunk." Children resemble
    their parents.

29. **Andere Länder, andere Sitten.**

    *Literally:* "Other countries, other customs." Expect differences abroad.

30. **Jedem Tierchen sein Pläsierchen.**

    *Literally:* "To every little animal its little pleasure." Let people enjoy
    their own quirks, a charming counterpoint to the reputation for rules.

## Dry humor

31. **Alles hat ein Ende, nur die Wurst hat zwei.**

    *Literally:* "Everything has an end; only the sausage has two." A cheerful
    way to say "all good things come to an end." It is also the title of a 1986
    pop song.

32. **Da liegt der Hund begraben.**

    *Literally:* "That's where the dog is buried." That's the heart of the
    matter.

## What the collection suggests

German proverbs value **order and reliability** (order is half of life, first
come first grinds), **steady effort** (practice makes the master, whoever rests
rusts), and **thrift**. But "every little animal its pleasure" and the
two-ended sausage show a tolerant, dry humor that the stereotype of rigid rules
leaves out.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Luther, Grimm, and regional variety (optional)</summary>

Luther's translation of the Bible (New Testament 1522, complete 1534) helped
standardize written German, and his advice to "look at the people's mouths"
(*dem Volk aufs Maul schauen*) pushed everyday sayings into print. The Brothers
Grimm began the *Deutsches Wörterbuch* in 1838; it was completed in 1961.

Germany is regionally diverse. Bavarian, Swabian, Saxon, and Low German
speakers have their own sayings, and Austria and Switzerland share most of the
proverbs above. "German culture" in this page means the shared Standard German
tradition.

</details>

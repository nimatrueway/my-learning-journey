# 🇯🇵 Japan: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~6 min** · read once, revisit often

Japanese proverbs are called **kotowaza** (諺). Alongside them are
**yojijukugo** (四字熟語), four-character compounds that often come from Chinese
classics. Children learn both at school, sometimes through *iroha karuta*, a
card game in which each card carries a proverb.

Each entry gives the Japanese, Hepburn romanization, a literal rendering, and
the usual meaning. Notes describe common readings, not a fixed national
character.

## Fitting in and reading the room

1. **出る杭は打たれる** · *deru kui wa utareru*

   *Literally:* "The stake that sticks out gets hammered down." Probably the
   most-quoted Japanese proverb abroad. It describes social pressure toward
   conformity. Many Japanese people use it critically, not as advice.

2. **郷に入っては郷に従え** · *gō ni itte wa gō ni shitagae*

   *Literally:* "When you enter a village, obey the village." Follow local
   customs, like "When in Rome..."

3. **和を以て貴しと為す** · *wa o motte tōtoshi to nasu*

   *Literally:* "Harmony is to be valued." The opening article of the
   Seventeen-Article Constitution traditionally attributed to Prince Shōtoku in
   604. Historians debate the attribution, but **wa** (和, harmony) remains a
   core word for Japanese self-description; it is even used as a word for
   "Japanese," as in *washoku* (Japanese food).

4. **雉も鳴かずば撃たれまい** · *kiji mo nakazuba utaremai*

   *Literally:* "Even the pheasant would not be shot if it didn't cry out."
   Speaking up unnecessarily invites trouble.

5. **口は災いの元** · *kuchi wa wazawai no moto*

   *Literally:* "The mouth is the source of disaster." Careless speech causes
   problems.

6. **言わぬが花** · *iwanu ga hana*

   *Literally:* "Not saying is the flower." Some things are more beautiful, or
   kinder, left unsaid. Japanese communication often relies on implication,
   a skill described as *kūki o yomu* (空気を読む), "reading the air."

7. **物言えば唇寒し秋の風** · *mono ieba kuchibiru samushi aki no kaze*

   *Literally:* "When I speak, my lips feel cold in the autumn wind." A haiku by
   Matsuo Bashō that became a proverb: criticizing others leaves you with a
   chill of regret.

## Humility and hidden skill

8. **能ある鷹は爪を隠す** · *nō aru taka wa tsume o kakusu*

   *Literally:* "The skillful hawk hides its talons." Truly capable people don't
   show off.

9. **武士は食わねど高楊枝** · *bushi wa kuwanedo takayōji*

   *Literally:* "A samurai, though he hasn't eaten, holds his toothpick high."
   Keep your dignity even in poverty; also used teasingly about pride that
   refuses help.

10. **猿も木から落ちる** · *saru mo ki kara ochiru*

    *Literally:* "Even monkeys fall from trees." Experts make mistakes. Shared
    with Korea.

## Perseverance and patience

11. **七転び八起き** · *nana korobi ya oki*

    *Literally:* "Fall down seven times, get up eight." Resilience. The *daruma*
    doll, weighted so it always rights itself, embodies this saying.

12. **石の上にも三年** · *ishi no ue ni mo san-nen*

    *Literally:* "Even on a stone, three years." Sit on a cold stone long enough
    and it warms up. Patience pays. It is often quoted to new employees thinking
    of quitting their first job too soon.

13. **塵も積もれば山となる** · *chiri mo tsumoreba yama to naru*

    *Literally:* "Even dust, when piled up, becomes a mountain." Small things add
    up. Compare Korea's "gather dust and get a great mountain."

14. **急がば回れ** · *isogaba maware*

    *Literally:* "If you are in a hurry, go around." The safe, longer route is
    often faster than a risky shortcut.

15. **失敗は成功のもと** · *shippai wa seikō no moto*

    *Literally:* "Failure is the source of success."

16. **案ずるより産むが易し** · *anzuru yori umu ga yasashi*

    *Literally:* "Giving birth is easier than worrying about it." Doing something
    is usually easier than you feared.

17. **二兎を追う者は一兎をも得ず** · *nito o ou mono wa itto o mo ezu*

    *Literally:* "One who chases two hares catches not even one." Focus.

## Impermanence, seasons, and the moment

18. **一期一会** · *ichigo ichie*

    *Literally:* "One time, one meeting." Treat each encounter as unrepeatable.
    The idea comes from the tea ceremony tradition, where every gathering is
    hosted as if it could never happen again.

19. **雨降って地固まる** · *ame futte ji katamaru*

    *Literally:* "After the rain, the ground hardens." Relationships often grow
    stronger after a conflict. A popular wedding-speech line.

20. **覆水盆に返らず** · *fukusui bon ni kaerazu*

    *Literally:* "Spilled water does not return to the tray." What's done is
    done; it comes from an old Chinese story.

21. **花より団子** · *hana yori dango*

    *Literally:* "Dumplings rather than flowers." Practical pleasures beat
    aesthetics, a joke about people at cherry-blossom parties (*hanami*) who
    care more about the snacks than the blossoms.

## Relationships and obligation

22. **情けは人の為ならず** · *nasake wa hito no tame narazu*

    *Literally:* "Compassion is not for the other person's sake." The traditional
    meaning: kindness comes back to you eventually. Surveys by Japan's Agency for
    Cultural Affairs have found many people misread it as "helping someone
    spoils them."

23. **三人寄れば文殊の知恵** · *sannin yoreba monju no chie*

    *Literally:* "When three people gather, they have the wisdom of Monju."
    Monju is the bodhisattva of wisdom. Group thinking beats solitary genius.

24. **十人十色** · *jūnin toiro*

    *Literally:* "Ten people, ten colors." Everyone has different tastes and
    personalities, a useful counterweight to stereotypes about uniformity.

25. **仏の顔も三度** · *hotoke no kao mo sando*

    *Literally:* "Even the Buddha's face, only three times." Even the most
    patient person will lose patience if wronged repeatedly.

26. **旅の恥はかき捨て** · *tabi no haji wa kakisute*

    *Literally:* "Shame on a journey can be thrown away." People behave more
    freely away from home, where nobody knows them. It reveals how much
    everyday behavior is guided by the gaze of one's community.

## Health and the famous cats

27. **腹八分に医者いらず** · *hara hachibu ni isha irazu*

    *Literally:* "Eat until your belly is eight-tenths full, and you need no
    doctor." Often linked to Okinawan longevity, where *hara hachi bu* is a
    widely reported habit.

28. **猫に小判** · *neko ni koban*

    *Literally:* "Gold coins to a cat." Giving something valuable to someone who
    cannot appreciate it, like "pearls before swine."

29. **猫の手も借りたい** · *neko no te mo karitai*

    *Literally:* "I'd even borrow a cat's paws." So busy that any help, however
    useless, would be welcome.

30. **井の中の蛙大海を知らず** · *i no naka no kawazu taikai o shirazu*

    *Literally:* "A frog in a well does not know the great ocean." Shared with
    China and Korea; the Japanese version names the ocean the frog has never
    seen.

## What the collection suggests

Japanese proverbs prize **restraint** (hide your talons, don't cry out like the
pheasant), **endurance** (three years on a stone, get up eight times), and
**sensitivity to the moment** (one meeting, one chance). Yet "ten people, ten
colors" and the self-mocking "dumplings over flowers" show that the tradition
also laughs at its own ideals.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Honne, tatemae, and why proverbs matter here (optional)</summary>

Japanese social life is often described through **honne** (本音, true
feelings) and **tatemae** (建前, the public stance). Proverbs such as *iwanu ga
hana* and *kiji mo nakazuba* help explain why people may keep honne private in
groups. These are useful lenses, but anthropologists caution against treating
them as uniquely Japanese: every society distinguishes private feelings from
public roles.

Many kotowaza have Chinese ancestors. *Fukusui bon ni kaerazu*, for example,
comes from a Chinese tale of a wife who left her poor husband and later begged
to return; he poured water on the ground and asked her to gather it back.

</details>

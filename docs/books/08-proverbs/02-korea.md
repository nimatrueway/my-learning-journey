# 🇰🇷 Korea: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~7 min** · read once, revisit often

Korean proverbs are called **sokdam** (속담). Many come from village farming
life, so they are full of oxen, rice cakes, tigers, wells, and straw sandals.
Koreans use them freely in conversation, dramas, and headlines, often shortening
a proverb to its first half because everyone knows the ending.

Each entry gives the Hangul, an approximate Revised Romanization that follows
pronunciation, a literal rendering, and the usual meaning. The cultural notes
describe common readings, not a fixed national character.

## Speech, courtesy, and secrets

1. **가는 말이 고와야 오는 말이 곱다** · *ganeun mari gowaya oneun mari gopda*

   *Literally:* "The words going out must be gentle for the words coming back to
   be gentle." Courtesy is reciprocal. Korean grammar encodes politeness levels,
   so the way you speak to someone is never neutral.

2. **말 한마디로 천 냥 빚을 갚는다** · *mal hanmadiro cheon nyang bijeul gamneunda*

   *Literally:* "With a single word, you can repay a debt of a thousand nyang."
   A *nyang* was an old unit of money. The right words at the right moment can
   settle what money cannot.

3. **낮말은 새가 듣고 밤말은 쥐가 듣는다** · *nanmareun saega deutgo bammareun jwiga deunneunda*

   *Literally:* "Birds hear daytime words, and mice hear nighttime words." In a
   close-knit community, nothing said stays private.

4. **호랑이도 제 말 하면 온다** · *horangido je mal hamyeon onda*

   *Literally:* "Even the tiger comes when you talk about it." The Korean
   "speak of the devil." Tigers appear everywhere in Korean folklore, from
   proverbs to folk paintings.

5. **빈 수레가 요란하다** · *bin surega yoranhada*

   *Literally:* "An empty cart is the noisiest." People with little substance
   make the most noise.

## Hierarchy, family, and leading by example

6. **찬물도 위아래가 있다** · *chanmuldo wiaraega itda*

   *Literally:* "Even cold water has an up and a down." Even with something as
   simple as a drink of water, elders go first. Confucian ideas about age and
   seniority still shape Korean workplaces, families, and language.

7. **윗물이 맑아야 아랫물이 맑다** · *winmuri malgaya araenmuri makda*

   *Literally:* "The upper water must be clear for the lower water to be clear."
   Leaders and parents set the standard; corruption flows downstream.

8. **세 살 버릇 여든까지 간다** · *se sal beoreut yeodeunkkaji ganda*

   *Literally:* "Habits from age three last until eighty." Early upbringing
   matters, which helps explain the intensity of Korean parenting and education.

9. **벼는 익을수록 고개를 숙인다** · *byeoneun igeulsurok gogaereul suginda*

   *Literally:* "The riper the rice, the lower it bows its head." The more
   accomplished you are, the humbler you should be, like a bowing person.

## Envy, comparison, and fortune

10. **사촌이 땅을 사면 배가 아프다** · *sachoni ttang-eul samyeon baega apeuda*

    *Literally:* "When my cousin buys land, my stomach aches." Envy hurts most
    when the winner is close to you. Koreans often quote it with a wry smile
    about their own competitive society.

11. **남의 떡이 더 커 보인다** · *namui tteogi deo keo boinda*

    *Literally:* "Someone else's rice cake looks bigger." Korea's version of
    "the grass is always greener."

12. **개천에서 용 난다** · *gaecheoneseo yong nanda*

    *Literally:* "A dragon rises from a small stream." Someone from humble
    origins achieves greatness. The phrase is tied to the dream of rising
    through education, and today people debate whether it is still possible.

13. **뛰는 놈 위에 나는 놈 있다** · *ttwineun nom wie naneun nom itda*

    *Literally:* "Above the one who runs is one who flies." No matter how good
    you are, someone is better. Stay humble.

## Shared work and persistence

14. **백지장도 맞들면 낫다** · *baekjijangdo matdeulmyeon natda*

    *Literally:* "Even a sheet of paper is easier when two people lift it." Any
    task goes better shared. It echoes village traditions of cooperative labor
    such as *dure* (두레) farming groups and *pumasi* (품앗이) labor exchange.

15. **시작이 반이다** · *sijagi banida*

    *Literally:* "Starting is half." Beginning is the hardest part.

16. **티끌 모아 태산** · *tikkeul moa taesan*

    *Literally:* "Gather specks of dust and you get a great mountain." Small
    savings and small efforts add up. Today it is also used ironically about
    tiny savings that never become a mountain.

17. **열 번 찍어 안 넘어가는 나무 없다** · *yeol beon jjigeo an neomeoganeun namu eopda*

    *Literally:* "There is no tree that won't fall after ten strokes of the axe."
    Persistence wins, whether in work or in courtship.

18. **하늘이 무너져도 솟아날 구멍이 있다** · *haneuri muneojyeodo sosanal gumeongi itda*

    *Literally:* "Even if the sky falls, there is a hole to rise out of." There
    is always a way out. A resilient saying from a country that rebuilt itself
    after colonization and war.

## Caution and consequences

19. **소 잃고 외양간 고친다** · *so ilko oeyanggan gochinda*

    *Literally:* "Fixing the barn after losing the ox." Acting too late. Often
    used in news commentary after preventable disasters.

20. **바늘 도둑이 소 도둑 된다** · *baneul dodugi so doduk doenda*

    *Literally:* "A needle thief becomes an ox thief." Small wrongs grow into
    big ones if nobody corrects them.

21. **아니 땐 굴뚝에 연기 날까** · *ani ttaen gulttuge yeongi nalkka*

    *Literally:* "Would smoke rise from a chimney with no fire lit?" Rumors
    usually have some cause. "No smoke without fire."

22. **원숭이도 나무에서 떨어진다** · *wonsungido namueseo tteoreojinda*

    *Literally:* "Even monkeys fall from trees." Experts make mistakes too.
    Japan has the same saying.

23. **등잔 밑이 어둡다** · *deungjan michi eodupda*

    *Literally:* "It's dark under the lamp." What is closest to you is often
    hardest to see.

24. **우물 안 개구리** · *umul an gaeguri*

    *Literally:* "A frog in a well." A narrow-minded person who thinks their small
    world is everything. Shared with Chinese and Japanese.

## Geography and history

25. **고래 싸움에 새우 등 터진다** · *gorae ssaume saeu deung teojinda*

    *Literally:* "When whales fight, the shrimp's back bursts." The weak suffer
    when the powerful clash. Koreans often apply it to their own peninsula,
    surrounded by China, Japan, and Russia, and later divided by the Cold War.

## Food, comfort, and easy wins

26. **금강산도 식후경** · *geumgangsando sikugyeong*

    *Literally:* "Even Mount Geumgang is sightseeing after eating." Geumgang,
    the Diamond Mountains, are famously beautiful, yet eating comes first. Korean
    social life centers on shared meals, and *"Have you eaten?"* (밥 먹었어?
    *bap meogeosseo?*) works as a casual greeting.

27. **누워서 떡 먹기** · *nuwoseo tteok meokgi*

    *Literally:* "Eating rice cake lying down." Something very easy, like "a
    piece of cake."

28. **꿩 먹고 알 먹고** · *kkwong meokgo al meokgo*

    *Literally:* "Eat the pheasant and eat its eggs." Two benefits from one
    action, like "killing two birds with one stone," but more appetizing.

29. **미운 놈 떡 하나 더 준다** · *miun nom tteok hana deo junda*

    *Literally:* "Give the person you dislike one more rice cake." Treat a
    difficult person generously to reduce conflict, or so you cannot be blamed.

30. **서당 개 삼 년이면 풍월을 읊는다** · *seodang gae sam nyeonimyeon pungworeul eumneunda*

    *Literally:* "After three years at a village school, even the dog recites
    poetry." Constant exposure teaches anyone. A *seodang* was a traditional
    village school for the Chinese classics.

## What the collection suggests

Korean proverbs pair **warm practicality** (food first, share the load, a kind
word repays debts) with **sharp awareness of status and comparison** (elders
first, cousins' land, someone always flies above you). Behind several of them is
the idea of **jeong** (정), a deep, sticky affection and loyalty that builds up
through shared time, food, and hardship.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Uri, jeong, and han (optional)</summary>

Koreans often say **uri** (우리, "our") where English would say "my": *uri
eomma* ("our mom"), *uri nara* ("our country"), even *uri jip* ("our house") when
living alone. The habit reflects a self defined through groups.

**Han** (한) describes long-held grief or unresolved resentment and is often
discussed as a theme in Korean literature, music, and film. Scholars debate how
far it is an authentic folk concept versus a twentieth-century national
narrative, so treat it as a cultural theme rather than a universal trait.

Many Korean proverbs have Chinese-character equivalents (사자성어, *sajaseongeo*)
shared with China and Japan, but the native sokdam on this page belong
specifically to Korean village speech.

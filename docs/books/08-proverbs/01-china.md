# 🇨🇳 China: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~8 min** · read once, revisit often

Chinese has two overlapping families of sayings. **Chengyu** (成语) are compact,
usually four-character idioms, many traced to classical texts and old stories.
**Suyu** (俗语) and **yanyu** (谚语) are everyday folk sayings, often longer and
rhythmic. Both are used constantly in speech, school essays, business, and
television, so knowing them is a shortcut into how people frame advice.

Each entry gives the simplified characters, Hanyu Pinyin, a literal rendering,
and the usual meaning. Cultural notes describe common interpretations, not a
fixed national personality: a billion people do not share one temperament.

## Harmony, face, and the risk of standing out

1. **家和万事兴** · *jiā hé wàn shì xīng*

   *Literally:* "When the family is in harmony, ten thousand things prosper."
   Family peace is the foundation of every other success. It is a common New Year
   couplet and reflects the long Confucian habit of treating the household as the
   model for social order.

2. **枪打出头鸟** · *qiāng dǎ chū tóu niǎo*

   *Literally:* "The gun shoots the bird that sticks its head out."
   Visibility attracts trouble. People quote it to advise caution before
   challenging superiors or claiming credit too loudly.

3. **人怕出名猪怕壮** · *rén pà chū míng zhū pà zhuàng*

   *Literally:* "People fear becoming famous, as pigs fear becoming fat."
   A fat pig is the one chosen for slaughter. Fame brings envy, scrutiny, and
   demands, so modesty is also self-protection.

4. **树大招风** · *shù dà zhāo fēng*

   *Literally:* "A big tree catches the wind." The same warning in gentler form:
   success makes you a target.

5. **人要脸，树要皮** · *rén yào liǎn, shù yào pí*

   *Literally:* "People need face, as a tree needs bark." Without bark a tree
   dies; without reputation a person cannot function socially. "Face" (面子
   *miànzi* and 脸 *liǎn*) means public dignity and moral standing, so people
   often avoid embarrassing others in front of a group.

6. **家丑不可外扬** · *jiā chǒu bù kě wài yáng*

   *Literally:* "Family ugliness must not be spread outside." Problems are
   handled inside the group, whether that group is a family, company, or team.

7. **退一步海阔天空** · *tuì yí bù hǎi kuò tiān kōng*

   *Literally:* "Take one step back, and the sea is wide and the sky is empty."
   Yielding in a conflict often opens more room than winning it.

## Relationships and reciprocity

8. **在家靠父母，出门靠朋友** · *zài jiā kào fùmǔ, chūmén kào péngyou*

   *Literally:* "At home rely on parents; away from home rely on friends." Life
   runs on networks of trust, an idea close to **guanxi** (关系), the web of
   relationships and obligations.

9. **多一个朋友多一条路** · *duō yí ge péngyou duō yì tiáo lù*

   *Literally:* "One more friend, one more road." Every relationship is a
   possible future path.

10. **远亲不如近邻** · *yuǎn qīn bùrú jìn lín*

    *Literally:* "Distant relatives are not as good as near neighbors." Practical
    help comes from whoever is close at hand.

11. **礼尚往来** · *lǐ shàng wǎng lái*

    *Literally:* "Courtesy values going and coming." A gift, favor, or visit
    invites a return. The phrase comes from the *Book of Rites* (礼记) and still
    governs gift etiquette.

12. **入乡随俗** · *rù xiāng suí sú*

    *Literally:* "Entering a village, follow its customs." The Chinese
    counterpart of "When in Rome, do as the Romans do."

## Patient effort and learning

13. **只要功夫深，铁杵磨成针** · *zhǐyào gōngfu shēn, tiěchǔ mó chéng zhēn*

    *Literally:* "If the effort is deep enough, an iron rod can be ground into a
    needle." The legend says the young poet Li Bai was ready to quit studying
    until he saw an old woman grinding a rod into a needle. Effort, not talent,
    is the hero.

14. **千里之行，始于足下** · *qiān lǐ zhī xíng, shǐ yú zú xià*

    *Literally:* "A journey of a thousand li begins beneath one's feet." From
    chapter 64 of the *Daodejing*. The English version, "begins with a single
    step," is a loose rendering.

15. **不怕慢，就怕站** · *bù pà màn, jiù pà zhàn*

    *Literally:* "Don't fear going slowly; fear only standing still."

16. **台上一分钟，台下十年功** · *tái shàng yì fēnzhōng, tái xià shí nián gōng*

    *Literally:* "One minute on stage, ten years of work off stage." Originally
    about Chinese opera performers; now said of any polished skill.

17. **吃得苦中苦，方为人上人** · *chī de kǔ zhōng kǔ, fāng wéi rén shàng rén*

    *Literally:* "Only by eating the bitterest of bitterness can one rise above
    others." "Eating bitterness" (吃苦 *chīkǔ*) means enduring hardship. The
    phrase is a cornerstone of exam culture and family sacrifice, and some young
    people now criticize it as glorifying overwork.

18. **活到老，学到老** · *huó dào lǎo, xué dào lǎo*

    *Literally:* "Live until old, learn until old." Learning is lifelong.

19. **三人行，必有我师** · *sān rén xíng, bì yǒu wǒ shī*

    *Literally:* "When three people walk together, one of them can be my
    teacher." Adapted from Confucius in the *Analects* (7.22). Everyone has
    something to teach, including their mistakes.

20. **一寸光阴一寸金，寸金难买寸光阴** · *yí cùn guāngyīn yí cùn jīn, cùn jīn nán mǎi cùn guāngyīn*

    *Literally:* "An inch of time is an inch of gold, but an inch of gold cannot
    buy an inch of time." A favorite line on classroom walls.

## Fortune, balance, and change

21. **塞翁失马，焉知非福** · *sài wēng shī mǎ, yān zhī fēi fú*

    *Literally:* "The old man at the frontier lost his horse; how could one know
    it was not a blessing?" The story, recorded in the *Huainanzi*, keeps
    flipping: the horse returns with a fine companion, the son breaks his leg
    riding it, and the injury keeps him out of a deadly war. Good and bad luck
    are hard to label while events are still unfolding.

22. **物极必反** · *wù jí bì fǎn*

    *Literally:* "When a thing reaches its extreme, it turns back." This idea
    of cycles, close to yin and yang, warns against pushing anything too far.

23. **留得青山在，不怕没柴烧** · *liú de qīng shān zài, bú pà méi chái shāo*

    *Literally:* "As long as the green mountain remains, there's no fear of
    running out of firewood." Preserve the source of future strength; retreat
    today if it lets you recover tomorrow.

24. **船到桥头自然直** · *chuán dào qiáo tóu zìrán zhí*

    *Literally:* "When the boat reaches the bridge, it will straighten itself."
    Don't worry too early; things often work out when the moment arrives.

25. **知足常乐** · *zhī zú cháng lè*

    *Literally:* "Knowing what is enough brings lasting happiness." Rooted in
    Daoist thought.

## Strategy and plain good sense

26. **知己知彼，百战不殆** · *zhī jǐ zhī bǐ, bǎi zhàn bú dài*

    *Literally:* "Know yourself and know the other, and a hundred battles will
    hold no danger." From Sunzi's *Art of War*, now quoted in sports and business.

27. **不入虎穴，焉得虎子** · *bú rù hǔ xué, yān dé hǔ zǐ*

    *Literally:* "If you don't enter the tiger's den, how can you catch its
    cub?" Attributed to the Han general Ban Chao in the *Book of the Later Han*.
    No risk, no reward.

28. **授人以鱼不如授人以渔** · *shòu rén yǐ yú bùrú shòu rén yǐ yú*

    *Literally:* "Giving someone a fish is not as good as teaching them to
    fish." Popularly credited to Laozi, but it does not appear in the
    *Daodejing*; its exact origin is uncertain.

29. **一个和尚挑水吃，两个和尚抬水吃，三个和尚没水吃** · *yí ge héshang tiāo shuǐ chī, liǎng ge héshang tái shuǐ chī, sān ge héshang méi shuǐ chī*

    *Literally:* "One monk carries water on a pole; two monks carry it together;
    three monks have no water." The more people share a duty, the easier it is
    for everyone to avoid it.

30. **画蛇添足** · *huà shé tiān zú*

    *Literally:* "Drawing a snake and adding feet." In the story, a man finishes
    his snake first, then loses the wine prize because he keeps adding legs.
    Overdoing ruins good work.

31. **井底之蛙** · *jǐng dǐ zhī wā*

    *Literally:* "A frog at the bottom of a well." From the *Zhuangzi*: the frog
    thinks the sky is the size of the well's mouth. Korea and Japan share this
    image.

32. **对牛弹琴** · *duì niú tán qín*

    *Literally:* "Playing the zither to a cow." Speaking to an audience that
    cannot appreciate the message.

## Food and health

33. **民以食为天** · *mín yǐ shí wéi tiān*

    *Literally:* "To the people, food is heaven." Recorded in the *Records of the
    Grand Historian* (史记). Food is not a side topic: a common greeting has long
    been 吃饭了吗？ (*chī fàn le ma?*, "Have you eaten?").

34. **病从口入，祸从口出** · *bìng cóng kǒu rù, huò cóng kǒu chū*

    *Literally:* "Illness enters through the mouth; disaster comes out of it."
    Watch what you eat and what you say.

35. **饭后百步走，活到九十九** · *fàn hòu bǎi bù zǒu, huó dào jiǔshíjiǔ*

    *Literally:* "Walk a hundred steps after a meal and live to ninety-nine." A
    rhyming health tip familiar to anyone who has seen evening walkers in a
    Chinese park.

## What the collection suggests

Read together, these sayings value **long effort over quick talent**, **the
group over the conspicuous individual**, and **timing and flexibility over
rigid confrontation**. They also argue with each other: "the gun shoots the bird
that sticks its head out" urges caution, while "how can you catch the cub
without entering the den" urges risk. Proverbs are tools for a situation, not a
single rulebook.

<summary>🤓 Chengyu, stories, and caveats (optional)</summary>

Many chengyu compress a whole story into four characters, so native speakers
hear a narrative behind phrases such as 画蛇添足 or 塞翁失马. That is why literal
translations can feel cryptic: the meaning lives in the story.

Attribution is often looser than popular sources claim. "Teach a man to fish"
is the clearest example on this page; quotations from Confucius are also commonly
paraphrased. Entries here name a classical source only where the link is well
established.

Regional languages such as Cantonese, Hokkien, and Shanghainese have their own
rich proverb traditions not covered here.

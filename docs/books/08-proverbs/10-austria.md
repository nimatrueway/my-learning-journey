# 🇦🇹 Austria: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~5 min** · read once, revisit often

A technical device has stopped working. Someone sighs, "De Technik is a Hund."
Literally, technology is a dog. In context, it means the thing is being a
troublesome beast. The joke is the complaint, not an explanation of the fault.

This collection mixes **proverbs, idioms, and everyday sayings** in Austrian
regional speech. Most examples below have Bavarian-dialect forms; the
Vorarlberg work saying comes from an Alemannic-speaking region. These are
written approximations, not a single official spelling system.

Austria also shares many proverbs with Germany, Switzerland, and other
German-speaking communities. The [Germany collection](./05-germany.md) covers
familiar shared sayings about order, practice, and thrift. This page adds
regional wording and humor rather than calling every German proverb Austrian.
It is a selection, not a description of every Austrian or every language
community in Austria.

## Understatement and practical perspective

1. **Ois hoib so wüd.**

   *Literally:* "Everything is half as wild." It is not as bad as it looks.
   The phrase can calm a worried friend, although it should not replace
   checking whether there really is a problem.

2. **Besser ois a Sta am Schädl.**

   *Literally:* "Better than a stone on the head." A deliberately low bar for
   saying something is better than nothing. The humor lies in refusing to
   pretend that a modest result is wonderful.

3. **Des is ghupft wia gsprungan.**

   *Literally:* "That is hopped as jumped." Either way makes no real
   difference. Use it when two alternatives lead to much the same outcome.

4. **In da Not frisst da Deife Fliagn.**

   *Literally:* "In need, the devil eats flies." Necessity makes people accept
   an unappealing substitute. This is a dialect version of a wider German saying.

5. **Lei loßn …** · Carinthian expression

   *Literally:* "Just let [it] …" In this use, keep calm or leave things be.
   The short phrase depends heavily on delivery and context; it is an everyday
   expression rather than a complete proverb.

## Work, timing, and getting started

6. **Schaffa, schaffa, Hüsle baua.** · Vorarlberg; also shared across the border

   *Literally:* "Work, work, build a little house." A compact picture of
   working and saving toward a home. Closely related versions are familiar
   in neighboring Alemannic-speaking areas, including Swabia.

7. **Heinga muaß ma, waun d’Sunn scheint.**

   *Literally:* "You must make hay when the sun shines." Use an opportunity
   while the conditions are right. The farming image explains the urgency:
   the weather will not wait for your calendar.

8. **Gemmas au.**

   *Literally:* "Let's tackle it." Let's get started. This is a practical
   invitation to act, not an ancient maxim with a known author.

9. **Ist der Berg auch noch so steil, a bisserl was geht allerweil.**

   *Literally:* "However steep the mountain, a little is always possible."
   Make the progress you can instead of waiting until the entire task feels
   easy. The mountain gives the advice a memorable image.

10. **Ist die Technik noch so weit, Handarbeit bleibt Handarbeit.**

    *Literally:* "However far technology advances, handwork remains handwork."
    Some tasks still depend on practiced human skill. It can defend craft
    without proving that every manual method is better.

## Luck, regret, and relationships

11. **Das Glück is a Vogerl.**

    *Literally:* "Luck is a little bird." Fortune is hard to hold onto.
    This expression is also the title of a Viennese song; that association
    does not establish the earliest origin of the image.

12. **Hätti, tati, wari!**

    *Literally:* "Had I, would I, were I!" A rhythmic dismissal of endless
    "if only" speculation. Learn from the missed chance, but do not keep
    rewriting yesterday instead of doing something today.

13. **Aufgwamt is nua a Gulasch guat.**

    *Literally:* "Only goulash is good reheated." Reviving an old romance or
    exhausted plan may be less appetizing than reviving the stew.
    It is humorous advice, not a rule that reconciliations cannot work.

14. **Da Gscheidare gibt noch, da Esel fliagt in Boch.**

    *Literally:* "The wiser one gives way; the donkey flies into the stream."
    Stubbornness can leave its owner worse off. Compare the Swiss version
    in which [the donkey stays standing](./09-switzerland.md#compromise-questions-and-perspective).

## Everyday frustration and social tone

15. **De Technik is a Hund.**

    *Literally:* "Technology is a dog." Equipment can be frustratingly
    uncooperative. Here *Hund* supplies the exasperation; translating it
    literally without context would miss the point.

16. **Des pock i ned.**

    *Literally:* "I can't manage that." Depending on context, the speaker
    cannot accomplish something or cannot take any more of it.
    *Des dapock i ned* is another documented form.

17. **Da Schmäh rennt.**

    *Literally:* "The banter is running." The jokes and conversation are
    flowing. *Schmäh* can refer to wit or a line of talk, but its meaning
    shifts with context; it does not always mean honest good humor.

18. **Do spüt d’Musi!**

    *Literally:* "That's where the music plays!" Pay attention here:
    this is where the action or the important matter is.

## Mortality and the limits of possessions

19. **Es letzte Hemd hod kane Toschn.**

    *Literally:* "The last shirt has no pockets." You cannot take your wealth
    with you. This is also familiar in Standard German and other languages,
    not a uniquely Austrian attitude toward money.

20. **Es letzte Auto ist imma a Kombi.**

    *Literally:* "The last car is always an estate car." The joke points to
    the hearse. It is dark humor about everyone's final journey, not a literal
    statement about vehicle design.

## More than one voice

"Work, work, build a house" and "the last shirt has no pockets" pull in
different directions. One encourages accumulation; the other questions its
ultimate value. Likewise, "let it be" sits beside "let's tackle it."
The interesting cultural detail is this range of responses, not a claim
that Austrian people are always relaxed, hardworking, or fatalistic.

<summary>🤓 Sources, regional spelling, and limits (optional)</summary>

The wording and regional labels follow the community-edited

Explanations and illustrative situations here are original synthesis.
The collection documents variants, but often does not establish a first
recorded use; no antiquity or exclusive Austrian origin is claimed here.

Dialect spellings vary by writer and region. Expressions heard in Vienna
do not stand for every part of Austria. Vorarlberg's Alemannic traditions
also differ from the Bavarian dialects spoken across much of the country.
Standard German forms may look more familiar to learners than these
conversational spellings.

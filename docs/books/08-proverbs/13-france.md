# 🇫🇷 France: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~5 min** · read once, revisit often

Does appetite arrive before you start eating, or can eating create it?
*L'appétit vient en mangeant* turns a familiar meal into advice about getting
started. But another saying asks you to wait patiently. Which one fits depends
on the situation, not on finding a single French rule for life.

This selection contains **20 French-language proverbs and expressions**.
French is used far beyond France, so these are not exclusively French national
possessions. The page does not yet cover France's regional-language traditions,
such as Breton, Occitan, Basque, Alsatian, or Corsican.

Each entry gives the original wording, a literal rendering, and its usual
meaning. The explanations connect images to situations rather than claiming
that everyone in France shares a personality. Sayings may be familiar without
being frequent in every age group or region.

## Appearances, experience, and premature conclusions

1. **L'habit ne fait pas le moine.**

   *Literally:* "The clothing does not make the monk."
   Dress or outward status does not establish someone's character or ability.
   Like "don't judge a book by its cover," it asks you to look beyond presentation.

2. **Chat échaudé craint l'eau froide.**

   *Literally:* "A scalded cat fears cold water."
   A bad experience can make someone wary even when the new situation is safe.
   The cat explains overcaution without proving that every later fear is mistaken.

3. **Une hirondelle ne fait pas le printemps.**

   *Literally:* "One swallow does not make spring."
   One encouraging sign is not enough to establish a trend.
   The [Swiss German version](./09-switzerland.md#patience-and-incomplete-evidence)
   names summer instead; the reasoning is the same.

4. **Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué.**

   *Literally:* "You must not sell the bear's skin before killing it."
   Do not count a hoped-for result as already secured.
   Compare the [Swedish bear's hide](./06-sweden.md#patience-and-caution).

## Craft, opportunity, and the patience to finish

5. **C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron.**

   *Literally:* "It is by forging that one becomes a blacksmith."
   Skill develops through doing the work, not just reading about it.
   The craft image makes "practice makes perfect" feel like an apprenticeship.

6. **À mauvais ouvrier point de bon outil.**

   *Literally:* "For a bad worker, no good tool."
   Someone without the necessary skill may find fault with every tool.
   Compare India's [crooked courtyard excuse](./11-india.md#hindi-skill-proof-and-responsibility);
   neither saying settles whether particular equipment really is defective.

7. **Il faut battre le fer pendant qu'il est chaud.**

   *Literally:* "You must strike the iron while it is hot."
   Use favorable conditions before they pass.
   The blacksmith's window of opportunity becomes a practical image for timing.

8. **Qui ne risque rien n'a rien.**

   *Literally:* "Who risks nothing has nothing."
   Some worthwhile gains require uncertainty. It encourages action,
   not the assumption that every gamble deserves taking.

9. **Tout vient à point à qui sait attendre.**

   *Literally:* "Everything comes at the right time to one who knows how to wait."
   Patience may be wiser than forcing a result.
   Set it beside the hot iron: waiting is useful only when the situation permits it.

10. **Rome ne s'est pas faite en un jour.**

    *Literally:* "Rome was not made in one day."
    Substantial work takes time. A French saying can use an Italian city:
    these images travel rather than staying within national borders.

## Food, desire, and moderation

11. **Ventre affamé n'a point d'oreilles.**

    *Literally:* "A hungry belly has no ears."
    Someone whose immediate needs are unmet may not be ready for a speech.
    It redirects attention from persuading someone to understanding their situation.

12. **L'appétit vient en mangeant.**

    *Literally:* "Appetite comes while eating."
    Beginning an activity can awaken the desire to continue.
    It can also suggest that getting more makes someone want still more,
    so it is not always praise.

13. **Mettre de l'eau dans son vin.** · Idiom

    *Literally:* "To put water in one's wine."
    Moderate a position or soften demands. The image concerns dilution;
    the everyday meaning is compromise, not advice about serving wine.

## Small resources, gifts, and avoiding trouble

14. **Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid.**

    *Literally:* "Little by little, the bird makes its nest."
    Repeated small contributions build something lasting.
    This shared French saying also appears in the
    [Switzerland collection](./09-switzerland.md#shared-french-and-italian-traditions).

15. **À cheval donné, on ne regarde pas les dents.**

    *Literally:* "With a given horse, one does not inspect the teeth."
    Do not respond to a gift by searching for faults.
    The image comes from assessing a horse; gratitude does not require accepting
    a gift with dangerous conditions attached.

16. **Mieux vaut prévenir que guérir.**

    *Literally:* "Better to prevent than to cure."
    Avoiding a problem can cost less than repairing its consequences.
    It applies beyond health, from maintaining equipment to checking an agreement.

## Conversation, accountability, and perspective

17. **Les absents ont toujours tort.**

    *Literally:* "The absent are always wrong."
    People who are not present cannot defend themselves or influence the account.
    It can be a cynical observation about group dynamics, not a fair rule
    for judging someone who missed a meeting.

18. **À l'œuvre, on connaît l'artisan.**

    *Literally:* "By the work, one knows the craftsperson."
    Judge ability by results rather than titles or promises.
    The wording also appears in La Fontaine's fable *Les Frelons et les Mouches
    à miel*; a literary appearance does not establish exclusive authorship.

19. **Après la pluie, le beau temps.**

    *Literally:* "After rain, fine weather."
    Difficult times can pass. It offers reassurance,
    without guaranteeing when a particular difficulty will end.

20. **À grands maux, grands remèdes.**

    *Literally:* "For great troubles, great remedies."
    A serious problem may call for a substantial response.
    The proverb cannot establish that a drastic proposal is effective or justified;
    the remedy still needs to fit the problem.

## What these images let you notice

The monk's clothes and the craftsperson's work ask you to distinguish appearance
from substance. The bird's nest and the blacksmith's iron distinguish patient
accumulation from a brief opportunity. Wine and appetite turn everyday food
images into questions about compromise and desire.
Those comparisons are useful; treating them as a fixed French character is not.

entry 20.

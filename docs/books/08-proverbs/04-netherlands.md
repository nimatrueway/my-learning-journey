# 🇳🇱 The Netherlands: Proverbs and Sayings

⏱️ **~6 min** · read once, revisit often

The Dutch language is packed with **spreekwoorden** (proverbs) and
**uitdrukkingen** (expressions). Many come from the sea, farming, and trade, the
three things that built the country. Pieter Bruegel the Elder even painted more
than a hundred Low Countries proverbs in a single 1559 picture, *Netherlandish
Proverbs*.

Each entry gives the Dutch, a literal translation, and the usual meaning. Notes
describe common interpretations, not a fixed national character.

## Modesty and "just act normal"

1. **Doe maar gewoon, dan doe je al gek genoeg.**

   *Literally:* "Just act normal; that's crazy enough already." Perhaps the
   most Dutch saying of all. Showing off, dressing to impress, or claiming
   special status is frowned upon. Even wealthy people often cycle to work.

2. **Boven het maaiveld uitsteken**

   *Literally:* "To stick out above the mown field." Standing out, especially
   by acting superior. People who do so risk being "cut down," much like
   Japan's hammered stake or the Australian "tall poppy."

3. **Hoge bomen vangen veel wind.**

   *Literally:* "Tall trees catch a lot of wind." Prominence draws criticism.

4. **Ieder huisje heeft zijn kruisje.**

   *Literally:* "Every little house has its little cross." Every household has
   its troubles, so nobody should feel superior.

## Thrift and careful money

5. **Wie het kleine niet eert, is het grote niet weerd.**

   *Literally:* "Who does not honor the small is not worthy of the big." Respect
   small sums and small things. Germany has a close twin.

6. **Zuinigheid met vlijt bouwt huizen als kastelen.**

   *Literally:* "Thrift with diligence builds houses like castles." Saving plus
   hard work leads to prosperity, a merchant-Calvinist ideal.

7. **Een gat in je hand hebben**

   *Literally:* "To have a hole in your hand." To spend money too easily, an
   insult in a culture that values frugality.

8. **Op de kleintjes letten**

   *Literally:* "To watch the little ones." To be careful with small expenses.
   The Dutch are famous for splitting bills, and the English phrase "going
   Dutch" reflects that reputation.

9. **Voor wat hoort wat.**

   *Literally:* "For something belongs something." A fair exchange is expected;
   nothing is free. The trade mindset in five words.

## Directness and honesty

10. **Het beestje bij de naam noemen**

    *Literally:* "To call the little beast by its name." To say things plainly.
    Dutch directness can seem blunt to visitors, but many Dutch people consider
    it more honest and respectful than vague politeness.

11. **Eerlijk duurt het langst.**

    *Literally:* "Honest lasts the longest." Honesty is the best policy.

12. **Geen woorden maar daden.**

    *Literally:* "Not words but deeds." Show it, don't promise it.

13. **Afspraak is afspraak.**

    *Literally:* "An agreement is an agreement." Once arranged, keep it. Dutch
    calendars are famously planned, sometimes weeks ahead even for coffee with
    friends.

14. **Wie A zegt, moet ook B zeggen.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever says A must also say B." If you start something, follow
    through with its consequences. Shared with German.

15. **Wie zijn billen brandt, moet op de blaren zitten.**

    *Literally:* "Whoever burns their buttocks must sit on the blisters." Accept
    the consequences of your own choices.

## Water, land, and practicality

16. **God schiep de wereld, maar de Nederlanders schiepen Nederland.**

    *Literally:* "God created the world, but the Dutch created the
    Netherlands." A popular saying, often misattributed to various foreign
    writers, celebrating centuries of dikes, windmills, and polders. Roughly a
    quarter of the country lies below sea level.

17. **Je moet roeien met de riemen die je hebt.**

    *Literally:* "You must row with the oars you have." Make do with the
    resources available, a sailor's pragmatism.

18. **Water naar de zee dragen**

    *Literally:* "To carry water to the sea." To do something pointless, like
    "carrying coals to Newcastle."

19. **Polderen**

    *Literally:* "To polder." To reach decisions by long consensus among
    rivals. Neighbors managing a polder had to cooperate or flood together,
    and the "polder model" now names Dutch consensus politics and labor
    negotiation.

## Caution, cats, and common sense

20. **De kat uit de boom kijken**

    *Literally:* "To watch the cat out of the tree." To wait and see how a
    situation develops before acting, the way a cat assesses a tree before
    climbing down.

21. **Als de kat van huis is, dansen de muizen.**

    *Literally:* "When the cat is away from home, the mice dance."

22. **Een ezel stoot zich in het gemeen niet tweemaal aan dezelfde steen.**

    *Literally:* "A donkey generally does not bump into the same stone twice."
    Even a donkey learns, so repeating a mistake is especially foolish.

23. **Wat de boer niet kent, dat vreet hij niet.**

    *Literally:* "What the farmer doesn't know, he doesn't eat." People distrust
    the unfamiliar, sometimes said of the Dutch themselves.

24. **Wie niet waagt, wie niet wint.**

    *Literally:* "Who does not dare does not win." The risk-taking side of a
    trading nation that once sent ships around the world.

25. **Spreken is zilver, zwijgen is goud.**

    *Literally:* "Speaking is silver, silence is gold."

## Home, neighbors, and fellowship

26. **Oost west, thuis best.**

    *Literally:* "East, west, home is best." Often embroidered or painted on
    tiles. It connects to **gezelligheid**, the Dutch idea of cozy, warm
    togetherness.

27. **Beter een goede buur dan een verre vriend.**

    *Literally:* "Better a good neighbor than a distant friend." A near twin of
    China's saying about neighbors and relatives.

28. **Gedeelde smart is halve smart.**

    *Literally:* "Shared sorrow is half sorrow."

29. **Oude koeien uit de sloot halen**

    *Literally:* "To pull old cows out of the ditch." To dredge up old grievances
    that should stay buried.

30. **De kogel is door de kerk.**

    *Literally:* "The bullet is through the church." A decision has finally been
    made after long debate. It is often explained by old disputes over whether to
    use a church building, though that origin is uncertain.

## What the collection suggests

Dutch proverbs share a **merchant's realism** (fair exchange, careful money,
row with the oars you have), a strong **egalitarian modesty** (act normal, don't
rise above the mown field), and **plainspoken honesty** (call the beast by its
name). The water-management history gives the culture its practical habit of
negotiating with everyone, because the dike protects everyone or no one.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Bruegel's painted proverbs and caveats (optional)</summary>

Bruegel's *Netherlandish Proverbs* (1559, Gemäldegalerie, Berlin) illustrates
more than a hundred sayings, many still used, such as "to bang your head
against a brick wall" and "to fill the well after the calf has drowned." It is
a visual reminder that these expressions are centuries old and shared across
the Low Countries, including Flanders in today's Belgium.

The "Calvinist thrift" explanation for Dutch frugality is a popular story with
some historical basis, but trade conditions, scarce land, and urban guild culture
mattered too. Treat it as one factor, not the cause.

</details>

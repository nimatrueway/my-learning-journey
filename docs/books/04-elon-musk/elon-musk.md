# Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future

Ashlee Vance

## Elon's World

Near the end of a long dinner at a Silicon Valley seafood restaurant, Elon Musk looked at biographer Ashlee Vance and asked, "Do you think I'm insane?" It was a strange, almost vulnerable question from a man who had just spent two and a half hours dishing about his fear that Google's Larry Page might accidentally build a fleet of world-destroying artificial intelligence, and about his low-carb diet, all while granting Vance the access he'd refused for eighteen months. Musk agreed to cooperate with the book on one condition he ultimately gave up on, that he get to add corrective footnotes. He simply said okay, because Vance had done the one thing Musk respects above almost everything else: he refused to take no for an answer.

Any real study of Musk has to start at SpaceX's headquarters in Hawthorne, California, a place Vance came to call Musk Land. Two giant posters of Mars hang near Musk's cubicle, one barren and red as it is today, the other green and terraformed, because turning humanity into a spacefaring, multiplanetary species is, in Musk's telling, his life's purpose. "I would like to die thinking that humanity has a bright future," he said. Walking the SpaceX factory floor in 2012, Vance found not a stunt but an honest-to-God rocket factory, built from a former Boeing fuselage plant, next door to the Tesla design studio where the Model S and Model X were taking shape. It was a revelation: Silicon Valley in the 2000s had grown timid, chasing photo-sharing apps and ad clicks while the era's boldest engineers, in Vance's account, quietly wondered if the age of civilization-changing inventions was over. Musk, alone among his peers, had thrown a fortune from Zip2 and PayPal into rockets, electric cars, and solar power instead of coasting.

That fortune, and Musk's relentless focus, came at a staggering personal cost, laid bare in the account of his working week. He would grind through SpaceX in Los Angeles, fly to Silicon Valley for Tesla, sleep on friends' couches because he owned no home up north, and juggle custody of five young sons with his ex-wife Justine, tabulating his own flight hours each year just to gauge how unsustainable his life had become. Asked how he survives it, Musk said simply, "I had a tough childhood, so maybe that was helpful." Even his rare escapes turn theatrical: costume parties, castles rented for his birthday, a blindfolded knife-throwing act with balloons strapped to his body, a bout against a three-hundred-and-fifty-pound sumo champion. His then-wife Talulah Riley, who staged many of these events, said plainly, "We're trying to make up for his miserable childhood now."

What emerges from Vance's opening portrait is a man equally capable of childlike wonder and blistering demands, someone who once told exhausted staff on a Saturday, "We've grown fucking soft," and meant it as gospel. Employees, Vance found, tend to split into those who adore Musk and those who cannot stand him, often at the very same time. By 2012, after SpaceX successfully resupplied the International Space Station and Tesla shipped the acclaimed Model S, the cynics who had written Musk off as a huckster peddling false hope had to reckon with the fact that he was actually delivering, on a scale matched in modern business only by Steve Jobs. The rest of the book is an attempt to explain how a bookish, bullied boy from Pretoria became the man standing beside those two posters of Mars, certain that saving humanity was simply the only rational thing to do with his life.

## Africa

Elon Reeve Musk was born in Pretoria, South Africa, in 1971, and first appeared in public at twelve, when a local magazine published the source code for Blastar, a space shoot-em-up he'd designed and sold for five hundred dollars. Even then he was already fusing science fiction with a strange, literal sense of destiny. "Maybe I read too many comics as a kid," he said. "In the comics, it always seems like they are trying to save the world." At fourteen he went through a genuine existential crisis and found his answer, half-jokingly, in Douglas Adams's The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, concluding that humanity should strive to expand the scope of consciousness so it could figure out the right questions to ask. Musk inherited some of this restlessness honestly. His grandfather Joshua Haldeman was an eccentric Canadian chiropractor and record-setting amateur pilot who uprooted his family to South Africa in 1950 out of pure adventurousness, once flying 22,000 miles round-trip to Norway and Scotland and later to Australia, and taking his children on expeditions into the African bush hunting for a mythical lost city, sleeping near lions and hyenas along the way.

Musk's childhood in apartheid-era Pretoria was intellectually rich and personally brutal. He read for ten hours a day, devoured two full sets of encyclopedias, and by his own account went into visual, trance-like states of concentration so deep that people thought he might be deaf; doctors even removed his adenoids trying to fix it. "He goes into his brain, and then you just see he is in another world," his mother, Maye, said. That same intensity made him a target. After his parents divorced, Musk chose to live with his father, Errol, an engineer whom Musk and his siblings have described only in the vaguest, darkest terms as a source of grinding psychological cruelty. "It would certainly be accurate to say that I did not have a good childhood," Musk said. "It was like misery." At school the abuse was physical: a gang of boys once beat him unconscious and kicked him down a flight of concrete stairs, and for years afterward continued to hunt him down, even coercing his best friend into luring him out of hiding. Musk later had reconstructive surgery on the nose they broke.

Yet the same boy who was bullied at Bryanston High School and later Pretoria Boys High was, by every classmate's account, an unremarkable, quiet student who never once looked destined to become a billionaire, mostly because he refused to work for grades that meant nothing to him. His real education came from a Commodore VIC-20 computer he begged his father to buy him and then mastered in three sleepless days, from paperback science fiction, and from homemade explosives and rockets built with his brother Kimbal and cousins the Rive brothers in a country with no equivalent of Estes rocket kits. At seventeen, using his mother's Canadian ancestry, Musk left South Africa for Canada, telling people he wanted to reach America and avoid mandatory military service under apartheid, though he'd also quietly spent five unremarkable months at the University of Pretoria first. As his brother Kimbal put it, "South Africa was like a prison for someone like Elon."

## Canada

Musk's escape to Canada in June 1988 was almost comically unplanned. He landed with the address of a great-uncle in Montreal, only to learn by collect call to his mother that the man had already moved to Minnesota. Undeterred, Musk bought a hundred-dollar countrywide bus ticket, tracked down distant relatives by cold-calling from bus stations, and spent a year drifting across the country doing brutal odd jobs, shoveling grain, cutting logs, and, most memorably, cleaning the boiler room of a lumber mill in a hazmat suit for eighteen dollars an hour, a job that whittled a crew of thirty workers down to three by week's end. "If you stay in there for more than thirty minutes, you get too hot and die," Musk said. He turned eighteen sharing a cake with strangers in a Saskatchewan farm town.

Once his brother Kimbal joined him, the pair's ambition sharpened. At Queen's University in Kingston, Ontario, they took to cold-calling accomplished strangers from the newspaper just to have lunch with them, a habit that won Musk a summer internship at the Bank of Nova Scotia and a lasting friendship with an executive there. It was also at Queen's that Musk met Justine Wilson, a fellow student with a black belt in tae kwon do who had hoped for a brooding writer and got a relentless engineer instead. When she stood him up for their first date, Musk hunted down her best friend to learn her study spot and favorite ice cream flavor, then simply appeared with two melting cones in hand. "The man does not take no for an answer," Justine said. "I do think of him as the Terminator." Their courtship, on and off through college, would eventually lead to marriage.

In 1992 Musk transferred to the University of Pennsylvania on scholarship, pursuing dual degrees in physics and economics at Wharton, and found, for the first time, classmates who thought the way he did. He also found Adeo Ressi, a lifelong friend who talked him into renting a rundown frat house and running it as an unlicensed nightclub, complete with blacked-out windows and homemade cover charges, while the strait-laced Musk, who barely drank, kept the business running soberly on the side. His academic papers from this period already show the throughline of his later career: a business-school essay called "The Importance of Being Solar" sketching orbital solar power stations, and another rhapsodizing about ultracapacitors as a revolutionary energy-storage technology, each earning him top marks and hinting he already saw a path from physics to for-profit enterprise. By his own account he had settled, even then, on the Internet, renewable energy, and space as the three fields most likely to remake the world, and quietly resolved to build a company in each. "I told all my ex-girlfriends and my ex-wife about these ideas," Musk said. "It probably sounded like super-crazy talk."

## Elon's First Start-Up

In the summer of 1994, Musk and Kimbal drove a beat-up BMW across America, dreaming up Internet businesses in the desert heat, and Musk spent his college summers interning by day at Pinnacle Research, exploring ultracapacitors, and by night at Rocket Science Games in Palo Alto, writing driver code alongside future architects of the iPod and iPhone. Bruce Leak, the ex-Apple engineer who hired him, marveled that "he had boundless energy... a PC hacker background and was not afraid to just go figure things out." After graduating from Penn, Musk enrolled in a physics doctorate at Stanford and quit after two days, unable to resist the pull of the commercializing Internet. In 1995 he and Kimbal founded Global Link Information Network, soon renamed Zip2, essentially an online Yellow Pages married to driving directions, a service Musk liked to explain through pizza: everyone deserves to know where the nearest slice is and how to get there.

Zip2 was built on nothing. The brothers lived in their tiny Palo Alto office, showered at the YMCA, and ate at Jack in the Box, while Musk slept on a beanbag by his desk and asked new hires to kick him awake when they arrived. He wrote nearly all the original code himself and, according to one investor, pitched himself to venture capitalists with lines like, "My mentality is that of a samurai. I would rather commit seppuku than fail." After Mohr Davidow Ventures invested three million dollars, Zip2 pivoted from door-to-door sales to licensing its software to newspapers like The New York Times, but the board soon installed a professional CEO over Musk's objections, demoting him to chief technology officer, a decision he never fully accepted. A disastrous attempted merger with CitySearch collapsed amid boardroom infighting, and Musk was stripped of his chairmanship as well. Then, in February 1999, Compaq bought Zip2 outright for three hundred and seven million dollars, netting Musk twenty-two million dollars at twenty-seven years old.

Looking back, Musk later admitted his greatest failure at Zip2 was managerial, not technical. "I had never really run a team of any sort before," he said, describing how he'd assumed employees shared his own instant grasp of problems and grew frustrated waiting for slower engineers, sometimes rewriting their code overnight just to prove a point, a habit that alienated even talented colleagues. It was an early, expensive lesson in the difference between being the smartest person in the room and being able to lead one, a lesson that would keep resurfacing, in sharper and costlier forms, for the rest of his career.

## PayPal Mafia Boss

Flush with Zip2 money and newfound confidence, Musk turned his sights on banking, recalling an internship discovery that Wall Street traders were mispricing developing-world debt backed by U.S. Treasury guarantees. "This was like the biggest opportunity ever, and nobody seemed to realize it," he said, though his bosses at the time had ignored the tip. In March 1999 he founded X.com, an audacious attempt to build a full-service online bank from scratch, complete with checking accounts, brokerages, and insurance, at a moment when most people were barely comfortable buying books on the Internet. Musk poured twelve million dollars of his own money into it, bought a million-dollar McLaren F1, and let CNN film its delivery, boasting to camera that he could turn X.com into "a multibillion-dollar bonanza." Five months in, cofounder Harris Fricker staged a coup and walked out with most of the engineering team, leaving Musk to rebuild the company almost from scratch with investor Mike Moritz's backing.

X.com's fiercest rival turned out to share an office building: Confinity, a scrappy outfit built around Max Levchin and Peter Thiel's PayPal service. The two companies fought a brutal, money-burning war for users before merging in March 2000, with Musk as the largest shareholder of the combined entity. But cultural clashes, especially over Musk's preference for Microsoft's infrastructure over Levchin's beloved Linux, split the company, and technical crises around fraud and scaling mounted. In September 2000, while Musk and his new wife Justine were literally boarding a flight to their honeymoon, a faction of executives delivered a letter of no confidence to the board and installed Thiel as CEO in his absence. Remarkably, Musk did not retaliate; he stayed on as an advisor and PayPal's largest shareholder, backing Thiel's push to reject early buyout offers. That patience paid off spectacularly: in July 2002, eBay bought PayPal for one and a half billion dollars, netting Musk about a hundred and eighty million dollars after tax.

The years that followed were unkind to Musk's reputation, with an insider book and the gossip blog Valleywag painting him as an egomaniacal pretender who had ridden Thiel and Levchin's coattails, prompting Musk to fire off a scorching, thirty-hundred-word email defending his cofounder status point by point. Most colleagues Vance interviewed sided with Musk's version of events, while still agreeing he had mismanaged the branding and technical staffing that nearly sank the company. PayPal's true legacy, though, was the talent it scattered across Silicon Valley, an alumni network nicknamed the PayPal Mafia that seeded YouTube, Yelp, Palantir, and a generation of top investors, with Musk its most famous and successful member. Amid all this, Musk's personal life briefly steadied: he married Justine, and in one of the book's darkest passages, nearly died after contracting a virulent strain of malaria in South Africa, misdiagnosed by doctors until a passing specialist recognized the symptoms just in time.

## Mice in Space

Turning thirty in June 2001, just as X.com was formally rebranded PayPal without him at the helm, Musk told his wife Justine, half joking, "I'm no longer a child prodigy." Restless and pushed out of the company he'd built, he and Justine decamped for Los Angeles, drawn partly by its glamour but mostly by its deep roots in the aerospace industry. Musk had begun revisiting his childhood fascination with space, and his first stop was the Mars Society, a nonprofit of space enthusiasts whose founder, Robert Zubrin, was stunned to receive an unsolicited five-thousand-dollar check from a total stranger. Musk soon floated an audacious idea of his own: send a capsule of mice to Mars, to reproduce along the way, as a publicity stunt meant to reignite public passion for exploration. When investor George Zachary asked whether the mice would make it back, Musk realized the stunt needed refining, and the group around him gradually converged on a scaled-down plan called Mars Oasis, sending a robotic greenhouse to grow the first plant on Martian soil.

Chasing a rocket cheap enough to make the stunt affordable, Musk traveled to Moscow three times in late 2001 and early 2002 with aerospace fixer Jim Cantrell and his old college friend Adeo Ressi, who suspected Musk had lost his mind. Russian rocket makers, unimpressed by the young American, quoted eight million dollars per refurbished ICBM and refused to come down, with one official reportedly spitting on Cantrell and Musk out of contempt. Storming out of a final vodka-soaked meeting, Musk sat in stony silence on the flight home, then suddenly spun around with a spreadsheet. "Hey, guys," he said, "I think we can build this rocket ourselves." He had spent months secretly devouring textbooks like Rocket Propulsion Elements, and his estimates convinced him SpaceX could undercut the entire industry by manufacturing almost everything in-house rather than depending on legacy contractors. Space Exploration Technologies was founded in June 2002, in a converted Los Angeles warehouse, with a mission statement borrowed straight from Silicon Valley: become the Southwest Airlines of space.

Musk's first great hire was Tom Mueller, an amateur rocket engineer from Idaho who had built the world's largest liquid-fuel engine constructed by a hobbyist in his garage; together, Mueller and a scrappy crew that included Chris Thompson, Gwynne Shotwell, and assistant Mary Beth Brown, set an outrageous timeline for the Falcon 1 rocket, aiming to fly within about fifteen months. Engineers shuttled between the El Segundo factory and a scorched Texas test site nicknamed the Texas Cattle Haul, blowing up engines by day and drinking cheap cognac by night, before eventually relocating launch operations to Kwajalein Atoll, a remote Pacific missile range, after the Air Force stalled them at Vandenberg. Life on "Kwaj" became a strange, intense fraternity of palm trees, geckos, and improvised barracks, with engineers hand-rolling the rocket down wooden planks because Musk refused to pave a footpath. Meanwhile tragedy struck Musk's personal life: in 2002, ten-week-old son Nevada died suddenly in his sleep. Justine grieved openly; Musk, by his own account, retreated instead, saying only, "It made me extremely sad to talk about it... I'm not sure why I'd want to talk about extremely sad events."

The Falcon 1's first three launch attempts, in 2005, 2006, and 2007, all failed. The March 2006 flight tumbled out of control twenty-five seconds after liftoff when a corroded aluminum fitting cracked, and SpaceX initially, and wrongly, blamed a young technician named Jeremy Hollman for the failure before later admitting salt corrosion, not human error, was the true culprit. A year later, the second attempt reached space beautifully for over five minutes before fuel sloshing in the tank caused the rocket to wobble apart, an agonizingly close near-miss. By this point SpaceX had burned through years and hundreds of millions of dollars of Musk's PayPal fortune, and insiders knew the company could likely survive only one more attempt. Yet even amid the wreckage, Musk kept recruiting relentlessly, personally interviewing candidates with his signature riddle about walking one mile south, west, and north and ending up back where you started, and building a company culture, as recruiter Dolly Singh put it, pitched frankly as "special forces": if you want it as hard as it gets, come; if not, don't.

The chapter closes on the eve of SpaceX's fourth and most consequential launch attempt, with Musk's fortune and both of his companies teetering, having just decided that only one venture, Tesla or SpaceX, could realistically survive if he had to choose. Employees who lived through this era describe an almost sadomasochistic devotion to Musk despite the brutal hours and blunt tirades. "He almost hypnotizes you," Singh said. "He gives you the crazy eye, and it's like, yes, we can get to Mars." Whatever the personal cost, the culture Musk built in these desperate early years, obsessive in-house manufacturing, punishing deadlines, and an unshakeable insistence that failure was simply data to learn from, would soon carry SpaceX from a joke of the aerospace industry to one of its most formidable operators.

## All Electric

Tesla's real origin story starts not with Elon Musk but with J. B. Straubel, a Stanford tinkerer who once turned a \$1,600 junk Porsche into a record-setting electric dragster and became obsessed with a simple insight: the lithium ion batteries powering ordinary laptops had quietly become good enough to power a genuinely fast car. Unable to get any investor interested in his homemade "torpedo on wheels" concept, Straubel got lucky at a 2003 lunch meant to pitch Musk on an electric airplane; when that idea fell flat, Straubel mentioned his battery car instead, and Musk, who had been noodling on electric vehicles using ultracapacitors, was hooked instantly. "Everyone else had told me I was nuts, but Elon loved the idea," Straubel said. Musk wrote a \$10,000 check on the spot and later fell for AC Propulsion's blisteringly fast tzero prototype, though the company's founders had no interest in commercializing it.

Unbeknownst to Straubel, two other engineers, Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning, fresh off selling their e-book company NuvoMedia for \$187 million, had independently modeled the exact same opportunity on a spreadsheet and incorporated Tesla Motors in July 2003, naming it after the inventor Nikola Tesla. Their plan paired a licensed AC Propulsion powertrain with a Lotus Elise chassis, sold directly to consumers rather than through dealers. Musk became their lead investor in early 2004 after grilling Tarpenning with an intense weekend of financial questions, putting in \$6.5 million to become Tesla's largest shareholder and chairman. "You need angel investors to have some belief, and it wasn't a purely financial transaction for him," Tarpenning said. Musk then personally connected the founders with Straubel, who joined immediately, uniting the two parallel efforts that would become the Tesla Roadster.

Working out of a converted plumbing-supply building in San Carlos, Tesla's engineers, most of them fresh out of Stanford, treated the car almost like an ambitious hardware hack, gluing together prototype battery "bricks" from hundreds of individual cells no one had ever combined at that scale. On the Fourth of July, 2005, they tested what happened when a cluster of the batteries caught fire at Eberhard's house, and the resulting fireball, engineer David Lyons said, "went up like a cluster of bottle rockets," a sobering preview of what thousands of cells packed into a real car might do. A dedicated task force spent months deliberately exploding batteries behind a fire department substation until they'd engineered a pack design that could contain, rather than propagate, a single cell failure, a breakthrough that became one of Tesla's lasting competitive advantages. Musk pushed hard for the finished car to feel less like a converted kit car and more like an object of desire, insisting on a carbon-fiber body and door handles that could be touched open with the flick of a finger.

Tesla unveiled its prototype Roadsters publicly in Santa Clara in July 2006, declaring, in Musk's words, that "until today, all electric cars have sucked." The reception was electric in every sense: California governor Arnold Schwarzenegger and Google's Larry Page and Sergey Brin turned up, thirty buyers committed on the spot, and the following month at the Pebble Beach Concours d'Elegance, strangers wrote hundred-thousand-dollar checks to reserve a car they'd never even driven. But behind the glamour, Tesla's manufacturing plan was unraveling. A supposedly simple two-speed transmission, meant to help the Roadster rocket from zero to sixty in under four seconds, kept failing after a few dozen or a few hundred miles no matter which contractor built it. A battery factory outsourced to Thailand turned out to be little more than a concrete slab with a tin roof. And when Valor Equity's operations expert Tim Watkins finally audited the true cost of building each car, he delivered devastating news: a Roadster meant to sell for \$85,000 was actually costing Tesla close to \$200,000 to build. "Of course, it didn't much matter," Musk said, "because about a third of the cars didn't flat-out fucking work."

Convinced that CEO Martin Eberhard had mismanaged the company and hidden the severity of its problems from the board, Musk had him demoted in August 2007 and pushed out entirely by December, igniting a bitter, lasting feud that would eventually spill into a lawsuit. "Martin was so bitter and disruptive," Straubel recalled of the transition. A parade of interim leaders followed, including Michael Marks, a manufacturing veteran who quietly began preparing Tesla to be sold off to a larger automaker rather than fight through its crisis, an idea Musk flatly rejected. "The product was late and over budget and everything was wrong, but Elon didn't want anything to do with those plans," Straubel said. "So Elon decided to double down." By the time Ze'ev Drori took over as CEO at the end of 2007, Tesla had scrapped its original transmission entirely, absorbed its battery operations back in-house, and set out to rebuild the Roadster almost from scratch, a costly, humbling reboot that would soon collide head-on with a global financial crisis and Musk's own unraveling personal finances.

## Pain, Suffering, and Survival

By 2007, Musk's rising celebrity had reached Hollywood: preparing to play Tony Stark in Iron Man, Robert Downey Jr. toured SpaceX's factory and came away so struck by Musk's "accessible eccentricities" that he insisted on placing a Tesla Roadster in Stark's on-screen workshop, cementing a comparison to the fictional playboy inventor that the press eagerly repeated. Musk leaned into the image, buying a Bel Air mansion beside Quincy Jones and Girls Gone Wild founder Joe Francis, partying with Paris Hilton and Leonardo DiCaprio, and calculating his life at the time as "10 percent playboy and 90 percent engineer." His wife Justine chronicled it all on a popular blog, sharing intimate, often unflattering details, and in one post she recounted feeling dismissed as "an arm ornament who couldn't possibly have anything interesting to say" rather than as a novelist and equal partner.

Beneath the glamour, both of Musk's companies were quietly disintegrating. SpaceX's second Falcon 1 launch had failed, Tesla's Roadster costs had spiraled wildly out of control, and Musk had already burned through more than half of the roughly \$200 million fortune he'd earned from PayPal. Silicon Valley's gossip blog Valleywag, led by writer Owen Thomas, seized on the unraveling, dismissing Musk as a lucky opportunist riding his cofounders' talent and naming the Tesla Roadster its top corporate failure of 2007. Musk's marriage crumbled in parallel; after the difficult birth of triplets compounded existing strain, he filed for divorce in June 2008, informing Justine largely by cutting off her credit card. Their split played out in humiliating public detail through her blog and later a Marie Claire essay titled "I Was a Starter Wife," with Justine seeking, and eventually settling for, the house, alimony, and a share of Musk's stock.

Weeks into his funk, Musk's friend Bill Lee dragged him to London to recover, where at a Mayfair nightclub Musk met twenty-two-year-old actress Talulah Riley, freshly known for playing Mary Bennet in Pride and Prejudice. Within days Musk had asked her to marry him in a Beverly Hills hotel room, telling her plainly, "Being with me was choosing the hard path." Riley soon found herself living the truth of that warning, watching Musk come home night after night to fresh catastrophes as SpaceX and Tesla both teetered on the edge of bankruptcy. "He would come home every day, and there would be some calamity," she said. "He was under immense pressure from all quarters. It was horrendous."

SpaceX's third Falcon 1 launch, in August 2008, failed catastrophically during stage separation, devastating a workforce that had already spent years suffering through the isolation of Kwajalein Atoll. "It was like the worst fucking day ever," recruiter Dolly Singh said. "You don't usually see grown-ups weeping, but there they were." Musk knew the company could not afford a fifth attempt if the next one failed too. "Flight four was it," he said. Compounding the tension, a rushed decision to fly the rocket body to the launch site by cargo plane nearly destroyed it when the fuselage buckled under air pressure mid-flight, forcing engineers to cut it open with pocketknives to equalize pressure before it could be repaired. On September 28, 2008, after six weeks of nonstop work, the Falcon 1 finally reached orbit, becoming the first privately built rocket ever to do so. "This is definitely one of the greatest days in my life," Musk told the jubilant SpaceX staff.

The relief was brief. Both companies still faced imminent collapse, and by December 2008 Musk calculated that he likely had enough money left to save only one of them. Tesla needed roughly \$40 million to survive past Christmas, and when investor VantagePoint Capital balked at signing the final funding documents, Musk restructured the deal as debt rather than equity to sidestep their veto and bluffed that he alone would fund the entire round if necessary, a gambit that closed with hours to spare on Christmas Eve. Two days earlier, in an even more dramatic reversal, NASA had awarded SpaceX a \$1.6 billion contract to resupply the International Space Station, a deal that instantly transformed the company's fortunes. Musk broke down in tears at the news while staying with his brother Kimbal for the holidays. His friend and investor Antonio Gracias later called the entire ordeal the truest test of Musk's character he had ever witnessed: "What he went through in 2008 would have broken anyone else," Gracias said. "He didn't just survive. He kept working and stayed focused. The harder it gets, the better he gets."

## Liftoff

By the time Ashlee Vance visited its Hawthorne headquarters, SpaceX's Falcon 9 had become the aerospace industry's most unlikely workhorse, a 224-foot, nine-engine machine that has turned launching rockets from a rare national spectacle into something SpaceX now does about once a month, ferrying satellites and cargo to the International Space Station at roughly sixty million dollars a launch, a fraction of what Boeing, Lockheed, or the Russians charge. Musk's ultimate goal is not incremental savings but reusability: rockets that land themselves upright on a floating platform or back at the pad, ready to refuel and fly again, the way an airline reuses the same plane rather than scrapping it after every flight. "Zip2, PayPal, Tesla, SolarCity, they are all expressions of Musk," Vance writes. "SpaceX is Musk."

That obsession shows up most vividly in how SpaceX hires and manages people. Recruiters like Dolly Singh scoured academic papers and robotics competitions for engineers who had been "building things since they were little," and nearly every hire, from janitor to engineer, was personally interviewed by Musk, who reliably poses his signature riddle: you walk one mile south, one mile west, and one mile north, and end up exactly where you started, where are you? He cares less about the answer, Singh said, than about how a candidate reasons through the problem. "The recruiting pitch was SpaceX is special forces," she said. "If you want it as hard as it gets, then great. If not, then you shouldn't come here." Many new hires quit within months, unable to sustain ninety-hour weeks or Musk's blunt, unfiltered feedback, yet those who stay often describe an almost cult-like devotion. "He almost hypnotizes you," Singh said. "He gives you the crazy eye, and it's like, yes, we can get to Mars."

Inside the all-white, 550,000-square-foot Hawthorne factory, hung with a scarred Dragon capsule returned from orbit and a glowing Iron Man statue near the elevators, SpaceX builds roughly 80 to 90 percent of its rockets, engines, and electronics itself, a strategy competitors like United Launch Alliance, which depends on some 1,200 outside suppliers, consider reckless. By designing its own radios, motherboards, and flight computers, SpaceX has cut costs by orders of magnitude, and it pioneered large-scale friction stir welding, a technique Musk pushed engineers to master so the Falcon's aluminum body panels could be joined into stronger, lighter seams than traditional welding allows, a manufacturing edge SpaceX later shared with Tesla. Musk's demand for radical in-house control extends to his notorious impatience with jargon: a 2010 all-company email titled "Acronyms Seriously Suck" banned any abbreviation not personally approved by him, on the grounds that a "test stand" needed no acronym at all. Employees nicknamed it the ASS Rule.

Musk's other defining trait, an almost pathological optimism about deadlines, has become legend among his engineers. "He can be a downright liar about when things need to get done," said early engineer Kevin Brogan. "He will pick the most aggressive time schedule imaginable assuming everything goes right, and then accelerate it by assuming that everyone can work harder." Musk himself, recalling SpaceX's original 2003 target to fly the Falcon 1, laughed at his own naivete: "Are you serious? We said that? I think I just didn't know what the hell I was talking about... This isn't like software. It doesn't work that way with rockets." Rather than simply dictate due dates, Musk has learned to make engineers own their own impossible deadlines. "He doesn't say, 'You have to do this by Friday,'" Brogan explained. "He says, 'I need the impossible done by Friday. Can you do it?' Then you are not working hard because he told you to. You're working hard for yourself." Engineer Steve Davis once spent nine months building an actuator Musk had budgeted at five thousand dollars instead of a supplier's \$120,000 quote; when he finally sent a three-hour, meticulously reasoned email justifying the design, Musk's entire reply was, "Ok."

Steering this engineering army day to day falls to Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX's president, whose path to the company was almost accidental. A former Chrysler management trainee and systems engineer at the nonprofit Aerospace Corporation, Shotwell joined SpaceX in 2002 as employee No. 7 after telling Musk bluntly that his fledgling company needed a real business development person; she gave three weeks' notice at her previous job and remodeled her bathroom, she said, "because I knew I would not have a life after taking the job." Shotwell went on to sell the company's earliest launch contracts before it had ever reached orbit and negotiated the NASA deals that kept SpaceX alive through its leanest years. She is famously blunt with recruits about the company's mission against entrenched rivals. "Our competitors are scared shitless of us," she told a room of interns. "It is our job to have them die."

Nowhere was the payoff of this culture clearer than on May 25, 2012, when SpaceX's Dragon capsule, after a tense malfunction with its docking sensors that Shotwell described as "two and a half hours of struggle," became the first vehicle from a private company ever to dock with the International Space Station, prompting astronaut Don Pettit's now-famous radio call, "Houston, Station, it looks like we've got us a dragon by the tail." Two years later, Musk unveiled the Dragon V2 in a Hollywood-style evening event, popping open its hatch with a Fonz-like fist bump to reveal a spacious, seven-seat cabin built around 3-D-printed SuperDraco thrusters capable of landing the capsule gently anywhere on Earth. "That is how a twenty-first-century spaceship should land," Musk said. "So long as we continue to throw away rockets and spacecraft, we will never have true access to space."

None of this progress has come without friction with entrenched competitors and regulators. In a pointed 2014 congressional hearing, Musk laid out figures showing SpaceX could launch national security payloads for ninety million dollars against United Launch Alliance's \$380 million, prompting ULA's CEO to lose his job within months. Musk has little patience for bureaucratic caution generally. "There is a fundamental problem with regulators," he said. "If a regulator agrees to change a rule and something bad happens, they could easily lose their career... It's very asymmetric." Whatever the political battles, the underlying machine Musk built, one that treats even veteran astronauts and government officials as just another obstacle to route around, has turned SpaceX from Kwajalein's punchline into the aerospace industry's most feared new competitor.

## The Revenge of the Electric Car

In mid-2012, Tesla began shipping the Model S, an all-electric luxury sedan that could travel more than 300 miles on a charge, hit 60 miles per hour in 4.2 seconds, and seat seven, complete with door handles that popped out only as the driver approached and a seventeen-inch touchscreen controlling nearly every function. Scientist Craig Venter, an early owner, called it "a computer on wheels." The car spread first through Silicon Valley's tech elite before winning Motor Trend's Car of the Year in a unanimous vote, the first ever for a non-combustion vehicle, and then Consumer Reports' highest rating in the magazine's history, ninety-nine out of a hundred. Within a year Tesla had posted a profit and become as valuable as Mazda, an astonishing turnaround for a company that had nearly collapsed only years earlier.

That earlier turmoil ran deep. The Roadster years had been marred by a public price hike, two safety recalls, and a bitter 2009 lawsuit from ousted cofounder Martin Eberhard accusing Musk of stealing credit for founding the company, a feud the two men settled but never truly resolved. Undeterred, Musk pushed Tesla toward an entirely new car built from scratch, code-named WhiteStar, with its 1,300-pound battery pack mounted low in the chassis for better handling. To design it, Musk hired Danish designer Henrik Fisker, whose stodgy early sketches, described by one Tesla executive as looking "like a giant egg," so disappointed Musk that when Fisker later launched his own rival electric car company, Fisker Automotive, Musk sued him for allegedly stealing Tesla's ideas, a suit Musk ultimately lost. In August 2008, at nearly the same moment Tesla teetered on bankruptcy, designer Franz von Holzhausen arrived from Mazda and, working out of a tent inside the SpaceX factory, redrew ninety-five percent of what became the finished Model S in about three months. "It was a blob," von Holzhausen said of Fisker's abandoned design. "It was clear to me that the people who had been working on this were novices."

Every major design choice with the Model S met resistance that Musk simply refused to accept. Engineers said aluminum body panels, a seventeen-inch touchscreen, and pop-out door handles were all impossible by automotive standards; Musk's answer was always some version of, as he put it about the screen, "I know. That's because it's never been put in a fucking car before." When Tesla unveiled a rough prototype at SpaceX in March 2009, the body panels were literally held on with magnets, and observers recalled being able to slide the hood clean off, in what one attendee called "one of those Wizard of Oz, man behind the curtain moments." Financing the actual factory to build the car proved just as improbable. A Department of Energy loan of \$465 million in January 2010 helped, but the real windfall came a few months later when Tesla, in partnership with Toyota, took over the shuttered NUMMI plant in Fremont, California, paying just \$42 million for a factory once worth a billion, while Toyota simultaneously invested \$50 million in Tesla for a stake in the company.

Tesla went public that June, raising \$226 million despite skeptics like the car blog Jalopnik dismissing the company as "a money pit." The Model S launch event in June 2012, held at the newly acquired Fremont factory, brought out former NUMMI autoworkers who had lost their jobs when the plant closed, some of them moved to tears as they watched the first cars roll off a line they were now helping run again. But the underlying business nearly failed a second time. By February 2013, Tesla had thousands of unconverted reservations, an inadequate service network, and, according to Musk, terrible word of mouth; the company was forced to shut down its factory for what it publicly called maintenance. Musk pulled employees from every department to become car salesmen overnight, warning them, "If we don't deliver these cars, we are fucked." In parallel, he opened talks with his friend Larry Page at Google about an outright acquisition of Tesla, worth an estimated six billion dollars, insisting on retaining control for eight years to guarantee the company would still build a mass-market electric car.

The rescue effort worked almost too well. In the space of about two weeks, Tesla's newly deputized sales force converted enough reservations that the company posted its first-ever quarterly profit on May 8, 2013, sending shares from roughly thirty dollars to a hundred and thirty within months and ending the talks with Google entirely. What followed, in Vance's telling, was the "Summer of Musk": a rapid string of announcements including a national Supercharger network offering free, fast charging, and a June 2013 event where Musk revealed that Model S batteries could be swapped out in ninety seconds, joking, "The only decision that you have to make when you come to one of our Tesla stations is do you prefer faster or free." When The New York Times published a critical review of the Supercharger network and a handful of Model S cars caught fire after collisions, Musk personally rebutted the review using data pulled from the car itself and added a titanium underbody shield to protect the battery pack, brushing off any dent to Tesla's momentum.

By October 2014, Tesla had unveiled a dual-motor "Ludicrous" version of the Model S that could hit sixty in 3.2 seconds, along with early autopilot software, and the company's market value had climbed to roughly half that of General Motors or Ford, a staggering reversal for an industry that had spent years dismissing Musk entirely. Even Ford's CEO, Alan Mulally, got dragged into the rivalry when he blocked Tesla from trademarking the name Model E, prompting Musk to register Model Y as a joke instead, so that Tesla's lineup, S, 3, X, and Y, would spell out a word Musk found much funnier than any press release. Musk summarized his own account of the era simply: "I may have been optimistic with respect to the timing on some of these things, but I didn't over-promise on the outcome. I have done everything I said I was going to do." By turning the Model S into a lifestyle rather than merely a car, complete with over-the-air software updates and a direct-sales model that infuriated dealership lobbies nationwide, Musk had accomplished something no American entrepreneur had managed since the 1920s: building a car company from nothing, that not only survived, but forced Detroit itself to take electric vehicles seriously.

## The Unified Field Theory of Elon Musk

Musk's third company traces back to a Burning Man road trip in 2004, when he suggested his cousins Lyndon, Peter, and Russ Rive, then running a modest IT-services outfit called Everdream, look into solar energy. After two years studying the industry, the Rive brothers noticed that every major solar installer at an industry conference gave the same excuse for high costs: "We're waiting for the cost of the panels to drop." None of them, Lyndon said, were taking ownership of the problem. So in 2006 the brothers founded SolarCity with a simpler model: rather than making customers buy panels outright, SolarCity would install them for no money down and lease them at a fixed monthly rate below the customer's existing utility bill. Musk helped design the financing structure and became chairman and largest shareholder. Within six years SolarCity was the largest solar installer in the country, and by 2014 it was valued near \$7 billion.

SolarCity's success was an anomaly in an industry littered with expensive failures like Solyndra, Fisker, and Better Place. "We had a blanket rule against investing in clean-tech companies for about a decade," said Peter Thiel, the PayPal cofounder. "On the macro level, we were right because clean tech as a sector was quite bad. But on the micro level, it looks like Elon has the two most successful clean-tech companies in the U.S. We would rather explain his success as being a fluke... but I think it's a reflection on the insanity of the world and not on the supposed insanity of Elon." For Musk, SolarCity was never just a business opportunity but an extension of a worldview: enough sunlight strikes the Earth in an hour to power the planet for a year, and he intends solar to become humanity's dominant energy source as fast as possible. By 2014 the company had begun selling Tesla-built battery storage packs alongside its panels and acquired a solar-cell manufacturer, moves that revealed what Vance calls Musk's "unified field theory": Tesla makes the batteries SolarCity sells, SolarCity's panels power Tesla's charging stations, and Model S owners routinely adopt what employees call the "Musk Lifestyle" by outfitting their homes with solar.

Tesla's own long-term plans hinge on the Gigafactory, a lithium-ion battery plant announced in 2014 that Nevada won with \$1.4 billion in incentives after a bidding war among five states. Musk expects it to slash battery costs enough to hit a \$35,000 price point for the upcoming Model 3 and to enable electric cars with 500-plus miles of range. He described his instinct to raise far more capital than Tesla immediately needed as a kind of inherited caution: "I feel a bit like my grandmother. She lived through the Great Depression... Even later in life when my grandmother knew there was really no possibility of her going hungry, she always had this thing about food. With Tesla, I decided to raise a huge amount of money just in case something terrible happens." Musk is characteristically blunt about the stakes of any delay: "A six-month offset would be like Gallipoli. You have to make sure you charge right after the bombardment."

Underneath all three companies sits what Musk describes as his true motivation: making humanity a multiplanetary species. He has laid out rough Mars economics in first-principles terms, arguing that a self-sustaining colony requires bringing the cost per person down to around \$500,000, "It's not about tourism," he said. "It's like people coming to America back in the New World days. You move, get a job there, and make things work." In August 2013 he unveiled the Hyperloop, a proposed 800-mph pressurized tube system between cities like Los Angeles and San Francisco, born partly out of his contempt for California's planned high-speed rail: "They're going for records in all the wrong ways." He initially insisted he had no time to build it himself, but the overwhelming public response, including a White House meeting where investor Shervin Pishevar found that "the president fell in love with the idea," pushed Musk toward funding a prototype after all.

Musk's demanding, often unsentimental management style has its own mythology. Longtime SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell and Tesla's J. B. Straubel are held up as the loyal lieutenants who absorb pressure without seeking credit, but the most notorious example of Musk's coldness was his abrupt 2014 dismissal of Mary Beth Brown, his executive assistant of more than a decade, after she asked to be compensated closer to the level of SpaceX's top executives. One former employee described the pattern more broadly: "Elon's worst trait by far, in my opinion, is a complete lack of loyalty or human connection... people who worked for him were like ammunition: used for a specific purpose until exhausted and discarded." Vance pushes back on the armchair diagnosis, common in Silicon Valley, that Musk's detachment reflects Asperger's syndrome, arguing instead that Musk behaves like someone "profoundly gifted," driven by an almost existential urgency about humanity's survival that leaves little patience for small talk or mistakes that, in his mind, cost mankind time.

Opinions on whether Musk represents genuine technological progress or expensive spectacle split sharply. Economist Tyler Cowen dismissed the Hyperloop as likely publicity and questioned whether Mars was worth pursuing at all, while manufacturing historian Vaclav Smil called Tesla "nothing but an utterly derivative overhyped toy for showoffs." Google's Larry Page, by contrast, counts Musk among his closest friends and most admired peers, marveling that Musk approaches enormous problems by starting "with the first principles of a problem... Elon is unusual in that he knows that, and he also knows business and organization and leadership and governmental issues." Page has mused, perhaps only half-jokingly, about leaving Musk his fortune, reasoning that a multiplanetary backup plan for humanity is worth the wager. Looking a decade ahead, Vance imagines a Musk who could be the richest and most influential entrepreneur alive, majority owner of three transformative public companies, but cautions that the outcome remains precarious: any of SpaceX's rockets could explode with astronauts aboard, any of Tesla's cars could suffer an unforeseen recall, and the entire unified field theory depends on Musk's ability to keep outrunning failure at a scale few humans ever attempt.

## Epilogue

Ashlee Vance closes his biography by noting that Elon Musk remains, above all, a body in motion, someone whose ambitions kept expanding even as the book went to print. Just before publication, Musk unveiled a plan to surround the Earth with thousands of small satellites to build a space-based internet, a project he framed explicitly as infrastructure for Mars. "It will be important for Mars to have a global communications network," he said. "I think this needs to be done, and I don't see anyone else doing it." SpaceX secured a billion dollars from Google and Fidelity to fund the effort, though Musk, uncharacteristically cautious about timelines, warned that "people should not expect this to be active sooner than five years." Around the same time, SolarCity bought a research facility near the Tesla factory that had once belonged to Solyndra, the failed government-backed solar company, a quiet symbol of Musk thriving where so many green-technology ventures had collapsed, while Tesla pressed ahead on its Nevada Gigafactory and floated the idea of eventually rivaling Apple as a trillion-dollar company.

Not everything was triumphant. Early 2015 brought a resurgence of skepticism from Wall Street over soft Tesla sales in China, and Musk, for once, sounded rattled defending the company publicly. On a more personal level, Musk told Vance he was divorcing Talulah Riley for a second time, explaining that she wanted a smaller, quieter life in England far from Los Angeles. "Tried to talk her out of it, but she insisted," he said.

Reflecting after his reporting was finished, Vance concludes that Musk is, and has always been, a man consumed by a singular quest, one so much larger than ordinary ambition that it leaves him seemingly unable to stop announcing ever bigger undertakings, from the Hyperloop to the space internet. Vance argues that Musk is not cold so much as differently calibrated: a deeply emotional person whose empathy extends to humanity as a whole more readily than to the individuals immediately around him, an imbalance that makes him appear aloof even as it may be precisely the quality required to make something as audacious as a space-based internet, or a Mars colony, real.

# Does Your Startup Need To Be In San Francisco?

A city cannot build your product. It can change who you meet, what ambitions feel normal, and how quickly a chance conversation becomes a hire or introduction. The decision is not whether San Francisco is objectively best. It is whether its density improves this team's work enough to pay its costs.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 15-minute source video is optional

## Location Is a Bundle of Effects

The episode deliberately presents a disagreement. Dalton Caldwell prefers a quieter, warmer place with room for his family and fewer distractions. Michael Seibel lives on Market Street and enjoys city life, visible ambition, restaurants, and proximity to activity. Both work in the same startup ecosystem. Their preferences show why "move to San Francisco" is too blunt to be useful.

Separate the bundle. A location affects runway, founder energy, customer access, recruiting, peer learning, and serendipitous encounters. Those variables can point in different directions. A robotics company may need engineers and suppliers around the Bay Area. A logistics company may learn faster beside the port it serves. A founder who becomes exhausted by housing pressure may lose more execution speed than density provides.

The city itself is also not the whole Bay Area. The episode notes that Apple is in Cupertino and other major technology employers sit south of San Francisco. Earlier companies proved that a downtown address is not required. More recent companies such as Uber and Airbnb did benefit from San Francisco, while DoorDash began as Palo Alto Delivery and moved later.

## The Real Advantage Is a Network Effect

The strongest argument in the episode is not prestige. It is the network effect created by concentrated people. A founder in the Bay Area can encounter future cofounders, employees, investors, and peers through ordinary social paths. The value includes people the founder did not know to search for.

That is different from scheduling meetings remotely. Planned calls reach the existing graph. Density can extend the graph. The episode compares this with an actor moving to Los Angeles or a band moving near a music scene: proximity does not guarantee success, but it increases exposure to specialized collaborators and information.

Network effects only matter when consumed. A founder who moves, stays home, and talks to the same online circle has purchased expensive scenery. Likewise, a founder elsewhere with strong local customers, active peers, and frequent targeted visits may already receive most of the useful benefit.

## Episode Case: Two Partners, Two Energy Systems

Caldwell describes preferring a boring environment where he can focus, with sunlight, trees, and a yard for his children. Seibel describes growing up near New York City, seeing the city as where the action was, and liking a place that made greater success visible. The same density that energizes one person can distract another.

This is more than lifestyle trivia. Founder performance is part of company strategy. A location decision that ignores sleep, family stability, concentration, or motivation treats the founder like interchangeable hardware. The episode's disagreement produces the right rule: measure the ecosystem gain and the human cost together.

## Original Example: The Six-Week Hardware Test

A medical-device team in Minneapolis considers moving west for fundraising. Instead, one founder spends six weeks in the Bay Area with twelve meetings booked. During the stay, she attends two hardware events, meets a regulatory engineer through another founder, and recruits an advisor. No customer discovery improves because hospitals remain in Minnesota.

The evidence supports a hybrid plan: keep product work near customers, fund regular Bay Area visits, and recruit selectively there. The test identifies which benefit is real without converting a slogan into a lease.

## Try It Yourself: Design a Location Trial

Choose the biggest claimed benefit of moving: hires, customers, capital, peers, or energy. Plan a two-week trial with a numeric target and a comparison baseline. Pre-book only half the schedule, leaving room for introductions and events. Track useful new relationships, work output, daily cost, and founder energy. Thirty days later, count which relationships produced a second interaction. Decide among moving, visiting regularly, sending one founder, or staying put.

<details>
<summary>🧭 A visit can overstate the upside (optional)</summary>

A concentrated trip is novel and socially intense. Permanent life includes commuting, rent, routines, and family obligations. Do not extrapolate from one exciting dinner. Conversely, a poorly planned visit does not disprove the network effect. Repeat the test or speak with founders who share your company type and life constraints.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- San Francisco's startup advantage comes from concentrated people and information, not an address by itself.
- Founder energy, family stability, runway, customer location, and recruiting all belong in the same decision.
- Test a claimed ecosystem benefit with a temporary visit before making an expensive, difficult-to-reverse move.

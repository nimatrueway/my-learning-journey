# Silicon Valley's Cargo Culting Problem

Successful companies leave visible artifacts: open offices, referral buttons, unusual titles, aggressive spending, and memorable founder personas. Those artifacts are easy to copy. The conditions that made them useful are harder to see.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 17-minute source video is optional

## Recover the Missing Mechanism

The episode defines cargo culting as superficial imitation without understanding why the original worked. It introduces the term through the story of people recreating the appearance of an airfield in hopes that supply planes would return. A runway shape did not recreate the logistics, pilots, or purpose that caused flights.

Startup imitation fails the same way. Wearing a black turtleneck does not reproduce Apple's product judgment. Copying an interview manner does not reproduce Uber's marketplace. The visible practice may be good, but its value depends on a hidden system.

Before borrowing a tactic, ask four questions: what problem did it solve, what conditions were present, what causal step linked it to the result, and which of those conditions exist here? If the answers are vague, copy a small test rather than the whole ritual.

## Three Generations of Startup Costume

In the 2000s, founders copied Google's colorful offices, free snacks, flat organization, broad engineering hiring, cute names, and shiny logos. Some practices helped Google recruit scarce technical talent. None independently created a superior search engine.

Facebook produced a different costume. Products acquired share buttons and plans to "go viral" even when users had little reason to invite others. Virality worked when using the product naturally exposed or improved it for another person. A button could not create that loop.

Uber inspired aggressive spending, market launches, and a combative founder style. Uber's subsidies operated inside a local two-sided marketplace where rider demand and driver supply could reinforce each other. Spending heavily on a product without those economics simply purchased activity that stopped with the subsidy.

Modern cargo cults move faster. Founders add AI, crypto, or another fashionable label because funding appears attached to it. This version can be worse because the copied thing may not even be proven. The founder imitates an imitation before anyone has established the mechanism.

## Episode Case: Facebook's Share Button

The hosts recall the era when every product was asked how it would go viral and where its share button was. They explain why copying Facebook missed the customer behavior underneath. A product used daily by a connected group has many natural opportunities to spread. A business tool opened once a month does not gain the same loop by placing a share icon on every screen.

The useful lesson is not "ignore growth loops." It is to identify the transmission event. What valuable user action exposes the product to a qualified non-user? If no such event exists, referral design must begin with value and context, not button placement.

## Original Example: The Unlimited Vacation Policy

A twelve-person support company copies unlimited vacation from a famous software firm. Employees take less time off because staffing is tight and managers never model absence. The policy's visible rule arrived without the capacity planning, trust, and performance norms that made it workable elsewhere.

The company replaces the ritual with a mechanism: minimum annual leave, published coverage rotations, and a manager measured on whether people disconnect. The wording becomes less fashionable and the intended rest becomes more likely.

## Try It Yourself: Autopsy a Borrowed Practice

Select one practice your team adopted from another company. Draw five boxes: original constraint, practice, expected behavior change, business result, required conditions. Put one piece of evidence under every arrow. Circle the weakest arrow and design a seven-day test. If you cannot name the original constraint, suspend the practice until someone can. End by writing a cheaper tactic that targets the same mechanism in your context.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Imitation is often efficient (optional)</summary>

Copying standard payroll, security controls, interface conventions, or proven sales operations can save enormous time. The warning applies when context determines the result or when the tactic consumes meaningful money and attention. Do not reinvent a commodity process merely to demonstrate originality.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Cargo culting copies a visible practice while losing the mechanism and conditions that produced its result.
- Growth loops, spending, culture, and technology labels work only when connected to specific user or market behavior.
- Borrow quickly when the mechanism is understood; otherwise test the weakest causal link before importing the full playbook.

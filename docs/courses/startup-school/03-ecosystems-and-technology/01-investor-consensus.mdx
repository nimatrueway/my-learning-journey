# The Secret That Silicon Valley's Top Investors All Share

An investor can criticize an accelerator and still invest heavily in its graduates. For a founder, that is not hypocrisy to gossip about. It is a lesson in reading incentives: public positioning is cheap, while repeated checks reveal what a firm actually values.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

## Read Portfolios as Behavior

The episode begins with a mismatch. Founders sometimes hear investors dismiss YC or suggest that going directly to a fund is better. Yet major firms hold many YC companies. At the time discussed, Andreessen Horowitz had made 234 investments in YC companies, Sequoia 139, and Founders Fund 104. The totals will age, but the revealed preference is durable: these firms repeatedly chose to invest.

That does not mean all investors agree about every company. It means they share an economic objective. Venture firms need access to rare, exceptional outcomes. When a promising company appears, branding differences matter less than the possibility of owning part of it.

Founders should separate three kinds of evidence:

- **Positioning:** what a firm says makes its approach distinctive.
- **Selection:** the stages, markets, and founders receiving actual checks.
- **Partnership:** how a particular partner behaves after investing.

The first helps a fund compete for attention. The latter two are more useful when choosing whom to invite onto your cap table.

## Why Rivals Choose the Same Companies

A typical venture partner makes relatively few investments each year. Very early companies provide little signal, so evaluating every raw opportunity is expensive. The episode explains YC as a filter: roughly 20,000 applications become a batch of a few hundred companies. The program then gives those teams time, financing, advice, and a public fundraising moment.

This creates value for downstream investors even when they dislike the price or competition around Demo Day. They can watch a smaller group, compare companies at the same moment, and meet founders who survived an earlier selection process. The filter is imperfect, but it reduces search cost.

Competition and consensus can therefore coexist. Firms agree that a company is interesting while competing over allocation, timing, ownership, and price. A founder who interprets one firm's pass as a universal verdict misses this structure. The pass may mean the company is too early, the partner lacks room, the round is too competitive, or the evidence is below that firm's threshold.

## Episode Case: The Rejection Gift

The hosts describe founders who report an odd interaction: an investor passes on the company, then advises them not to do YC. The investor is not offering an alternative check. The advice arrives as what the hosts jokingly frame as a parting gift.

The case matters because the investor's incentives and the founder's incentives have separated. The investor benefits if an attractive company remains easy to access later and avoids a competitive process. The founder benefits from money, progress, advice, and more financing options now. Neither side must be malicious for the recommendations to diverge.

The practical response is not to ignore investors. Ask what concrete fact caused the pass, then compare the advice with behavior. If the same firm routinely invests in accelerator graduates after Demo Day, its portfolio is stronger evidence than a broad warning delivered during a rejection.

## Original Example: Two Conflicting Signals

A climate-software founder hears that accelerators are unnecessary from a fund that declines her pre-seed round. She checks the firm's recent investments and finds six accelerator-backed climate companies, all funded after pilots produced usage data. The pattern suggests a stage threshold, not hostility to accelerators.

Her next move changes. She stops debating the firm's slogan and asks what proof would justify another conversation. She then chooses the financing path that best helps her reach that proof. The portfolio did not tell her which investor to trust; it helped her form a better question.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Revealed-Preference Map

Choose five investors you might approach and inspect their last ten relevant deals. For each firm, record the earliest stage at which it invested, the evidence those companies had, the named partner's market experience, portfolio overlap with supposed rivals, and one founder reference you would request.

Finish by replacing one reputation claim with a behavioral statement, such as "This partner leads infrastructure seed rounds after a design partner converts." That sentence can shape a fundraising sequence. "Top-tier investor" cannot.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Consensus is not diligence (optional)</summary>

Portfolio overlap can show where investors place bets, but it cannot establish that a partner will be helpful, fair, or available when the company struggles. Popular deals can also reflect social proof rather than independent judgment. Check references with founders whose companies failed or stalled, not only visible winners, and evaluate terms as carefully as reputation.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Repeated investments reveal an investor's preferences more reliably than public positioning does.
- Accelerators can reduce search cost by filtering and developing very early companies for later investors.
- A pass is one firm's stage-specific decision, so inspect the cause and portfolio before treating it as a market verdict.

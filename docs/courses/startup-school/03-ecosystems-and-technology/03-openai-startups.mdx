# Will OpenAI Kill All Startups?

When a platform gains a powerful feature, every dependent product can look temporary. The same capability also makes previously impossible products cheap enough to build. The founder's job is to distinguish a renamed feature from a newly solvable customer problem.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 13-minute source video is optional

## Use the Historical Frame

The episode sets aside predictions about artificial general intelligence because that debate cannot guide a product decision today. It asks a narrower question: what usually happens when a major technology changes quickly?

Modern farming, electricity, the internet, open-source software, cloud computing, and mobile devices did not reduce the world to one provider. They enabled more businesses and often advantaged startups over incumbents. A rapid shift makes existing systems, staffing, and assumptions liabilities. A small team can begin with the new capability as a default instead of grafting it onto an old architecture.

This does not make every AI company durable. It means "the platform will do everything" is an inadequate reason to stop. Platforms tend to build broad capabilities. Startups can own narrow workflows, customer relationships, integrations, proprietary operational data, and the thousand edge cases between a model response and a completed job.

## Feature, Product, or Costume?

The episode distinguishes genuine product improvement from cargo-cult AI. Adding AI to a pitch because investors expect the word changes nothing for a customer. Adding a capability that sharply improves retention, output quality, or willingness to pay is real, even when another company supplies the model.

Evaluate the product in layers:

1. **Inference:** what the model produces and how replaceable the provider is.
2. **Workflow:** the inputs, approvals, exceptions, and actions around the output.
3. **Learning:** what repeated customer use teaches the company.
4. **Distribution:** why the user encounters and trusts this product.

A thin summarizer may disappear into a platform. A clinical documentation system that captures visits, applies hospital policies, routes uncertain codes, records corrections, and integrates with billing owns a larger job. The model is essential, but it is not the whole company.

## Episode Case: Cloud Computing Was Not a Homepage Badge

The hosts revisit the arrival of cloud computing. Some people dismissed it, while others treated "using the cloud" as an innovation by itself. The important companies did neither. They used cheap, flexible infrastructure to build products and operating models that had been uneconomical before.

The episode makes the same point with mobile: producing a high-quality mobile application could materially increase retention. The value came from changed user behavior and product quality, not from announcing a mobile strategy. AI earns its place under the same standard. A capability is meaningful when a customer outcome moves.

## Original Example: Permits, Not Prompts

A startup drafts construction-permit applications with a language model. If it only turns a description into prose, the permitting portal can absorb it. The team instead connects parcel data, local code, drawings, submission rules, fee payment, and reviewer comments. Every rejected application becomes a structured lesson about one jurisdiction.

The defensible asset is not a clever prompt. It is a completed permit workflow, accumulated exception knowledge, and distribution through architects who submit every week.

## Try It Yourself: Run a Provider Shock

Write the release note your model provider might publish six months from now that would most threaten your product. Then remove that feature from your architecture diagram. List what customers would still need before and after the model call, including data collection, verification, permissions, exception handling, and completed actions. Interview three active users about the remaining work. Choose one layer to deepen this month and one provider dependency to make replaceable.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Platform dependence can still be fatal (optional)</summary>

Workflow depth is not magic. A provider can move up the stack, prices can change, access can disappear, and regulation can constrain data sharing. Track concentration risk and test alternate models. Also avoid manufacturing complexity merely to look defensible. Customers pay for a better result, not for your architecture diagram.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Fast technology shifts often create startup openings because incumbents must unwind old systems and assumptions.
- AI is product substance only when it improves a customer outcome such as retention, quality, speed, or price.
- Durability can live in workflow ownership, customer learning, integration, and distribution rather than the model call alone.

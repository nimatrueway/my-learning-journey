# The Secrets To Setting Smarter Goals

A goal can focus a startup or teach it to counterfeit progress. The number is
not useful because it is measurable. It is useful when pursuing it causes better
decisions about the actual business.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 29-minute source video is optional

## A Goal Should Change How You Work

Caldwell and Seibel describe a new YC company that wants $30,000 in monthly
recurring revenue by Demo Day because an alumnus said that was the amount needed
to raise. The target is detached from product state, sales cycle, and baseline.
Its real purpose is investor approval, so missing it is nearly guaranteed to feel
like failure even if the company learns quickly.

At the other extreme, an app team plans to build privately for three months,
launch, and immediately reach 100,000 daily active users. The launch is treated
as a switch that creates distribution. The hosts’ corrective is practical:
launch earlier so there is time to learn how growth works after release.

A demanding goal is useful when it prompts invention and prioritization. An
impossible one produces fatalism, excuses, or frantic activity unrelated to the
constraint. Calibrate ambition to a known baseline, a plausible mechanism, and
the time available for iteration.

## Choose the Metric the Business Cannot Fake

The episode’s paid-product example charges $10 per month but sets a goal for
registered users. Registration can rise while revenue and active use remain flat.
If the company sells subscriptions, collected recurring revenue and retention
are closer to value than accounts created. If it is pre-revenue, repeated use of
the core workflow may be the better leading signal.

Every metric invites a behavior. “Increase calls” rewards short, unqualified
calls. “Close revenue” can reward discounts that create churn. Pair an outcome
with a quality guardrail and controllable weekly inputs:

- outcome: retained paid teams at the end of the month;
- inputs: qualified demos and onboarding sessions completed;
- guardrail: no discount beyond the approved range and no rise in early churn.

The outcome prevents busywork from winning. Inputs tell the team what to do this
week. The guardrail stops a local victory from damaging the business.

## Sandbagging Corrupts the Feedback Loop

The hosts also discuss deliberately easy goals that teams can exceed. Sandbagging
makes reports look healthy while hiding capacity and uncertainty. It changes the
game from learning what the team can achieve to managing other people’s
impressions.

Missing a sound goal is not automatically bad. The miss can reveal a false sales
assumption or a product bottleneck. Hitting a weak goal is not automatically
good. Review the mechanism: which actions moved the number, what happened to the
guardrail, and what was learned that changes the next target?

## Original Example: The Activation Trap

A payroll startup has 400 signups and wants 1,000 by quarter end. Only 60
companies have completed a payroll run. The team could buy more leads and hit the
signup target while preserving the real failure.

They replace it with: increase companies completing a first payroll from 60 to
120 in six weeks, while keeping support response under one business day. Weekly
inputs are five onboarding observations and two fixes to the largest activation
drop-off. The revised goal points every function at the same constraint.

## Try It Yourself: Red-Team One Goal

Take the goal mentioned most often this week. Write its baseline, deadline, and
owner. Then list three ways a clever teammate could hit the number while harming
customers or the company. Add one guardrail that blocks the easiest exploit.
Finally, name two actions the team controls this week and the causal reason each
should move the outcome. Review both result and mechanism at the deadline.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Early-stage numbers are noisy (optional)</summary>

Small cohorts and long sales cycles make precise forecasts fragile. Use ranges
or milestone goals when a single number implies knowledge you do not possess.
Do not respond by abandoning measurement; state the uncertainty and shorten the
learning interval.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A goal needs a baseline and mechanism; an impressive number borrowed from another company is not a plan.
- Pair the business outcome with controllable inputs and a guardrail against the easiest harmful shortcut.
- Review what moved the metric, because a revealing miss can teach more than a sandbagged win.

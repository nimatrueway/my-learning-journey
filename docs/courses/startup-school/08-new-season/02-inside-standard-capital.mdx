# Design a Series A Like a Product

A founder can lose months to a Series A without receiving a clear yes, a clean
no, or even the final terms. The company keeps paying salaries while the CEO
runs a second business called “talking to investors.”

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 35-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell uses the launch of Standard Capital's \$425 million fund to
explain a broader idea: financing itself is a product. Its application,
decision speed, terms, governance, and founder community all shape the cost of
capital. The useful question is not whether a fund sounds prestigious. It is
whether the financing mechanism helps this company do better work.

## Start With Founder-Fund Fit

Dalton says he approached the new firm like advice about a startup pivot. Ask
where the founding team has unusual advantages: accumulated judgment, network,
reputation, and a problem it understands from repeated exposure. His team had
spent years reading applications, interviewing founders, and advising early
companies. The proposed move was to apply parts of that operating model at the
Series A stage.

This is a heuristic, not proof that the fund will perform. It does offer a
useful test for any new institution or startup: can the founders name a
capability they possess that directly changes the customer experience? “We know
important people” is vague. “We can evaluate a structured application quickly
because we have repeatedly practiced that workflow” is testable.

## Source Case: Seven Months Away From the Company

Dalton contrasts the proposed model with his own fundraising roughly 20 years
earlier. He recalls needing a lead investor and priced term sheet, selling 25%
of the company, giving up a board seat, and spending seven months on a seed
round. Later fundraising involved warm introductions, CRM tracking, coffee and
dinner meetings, and investors who could sound enthusiastic for weeks before
passing or disappearing.

The damage was not only emotional. Every fundraising meeting displaced work on
the startup. That creates a useful accounting identity:

**cost of capital = financial terms + governance rights + founder time + lost operating momentum**

A lower valuation with a fast, legible close may be cheaper than a higher one
that consumes a quarter. Conversely, speed does not rescue bad terms. Compare
the complete cost rather than optimizing one visible number.

## Make the Interface Legible

Dalton describes several design choices for Standard Capital: anyone can apply
through a website; the application should take roughly an hour or two; the
standard Series A terms are visible before applying; founders choose the price
at which they want to raise; and the fund does not plan to take board seats.
The stated goal is to replace a relationship-heavy, opaque sequence with a
documented path.

Whether this model works is an open empirical question. Still, the product
logic is transferable. A good financing process lets a founder answer four
questions early:

1. What information must we provide?
2. When should we expect a decision?
3. What ownership and control are exchanged?
4. What help is available after the wire arrives?

If an investor cannot answer those questions, uncertainty is part of the price.

## Try It Yourself: Price the Process

Take one real or hypothetical raise and set a **30-minute timer**. Create two
columns for plausible financing paths. Score each from 1 to 5 on decision
speed, term clarity, dilution, governance burden, and useful post-close support.
Then estimate founder-weeks consumed before a yes or no.

Circle the two assumptions with the weakest evidence. Your next action must be
one bounded question to an investor or founder reference, not another hour of
spreadsheet polishing.

## Transparency Does Not Remove Judgment

An open application can widen access, but it does not make selection objective.
Published terms reduce surprise, but founders must still judge valuation,
dilution, signaling, and whether no board seat is helpful for their situation.
A first-time founder may actively benefit from a strong, accountable board
member. A profitable company may not need outside capital at all.

The fund is also being described by one of its founders during its launch.
Treat the episode as an explanation of design intent, not independent evidence
of returns, selection quality, or founder outcomes.

<details>
<summary>🧾 A financing-interface checklist (optional)</summary>

Before entering a process, request the expected timeline, decision makers,
reference policy, standard documents, ownership target, board expectations,
follow-on policy, and conditions that can reopen agreed terms. Record answers
in writing. Ambiguity may be unavoidable, but it should be visible enough to
price into your decision.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Evaluate capital by terms, control, founder time, and operating momentum, not valuation alone.
- A fund's real differentiation should change a founder's experience in a specific, testable way.
- Transparency reduces process risk, but it cannot decide whether raising is right for your company.

# Contract for the Advice You Need

You spend 28 minutes discussing homepage copy. With two minutes left, you
mention that three of four cofounders are leaving and runway ends next quarter.
Technically, the meeting had an agenda.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel argue that advice quality depends on a
contract between seeker and adviser. The seeker must reveal the real issue and
the kind of help wanted. The adviser must understand the facts, name the limits
of their knowledge, and resist answering a different question merely because
it is easier.

## Choose the Mode Before the Answer

Not every conversation is a request for a decision. A founder may want:

- **Diagnosis:** Help me find the problem I am missing.
- **Options:** Help me generate and compare paths.
- **Challenge:** Try to disprove my current choice.
- **Expertise:** Explain a domain, process, or failure mode you know.
- **Support:** I have decided; help me process or execute it.

Confusing these modes produces avoidable friction. A founder seeking support
feels attacked by a decision audit. An adviser offering encouragement fails a
founder who needs the plan challenged. Asking “What kind of conversation is
this?” is not ceremony. It allocates the scarce minutes correctly.

## Source Case: The Acquisition Question

Michael gives a recurring office-hours example. A founder says the company has
an acquisition offer and asks what to do. Sometimes the decision is already
made and the founder wants the adviser to celebrate. Sometimes the founder does
not want to sell and needs confidence to decline. Presenting either need as a
neutral decision request invites irrelevant analysis.

The same episode describes founders withholding the central problem until the
end: three of four founders are leaving, runway is only three months, or the
founder is considering shutting down. Meanwhile, the agenda focuses on a
smaller product detail. Better advice begins by putting the stomach-ache issue
first, even when it is uncomfortable or poorly formed.

Use this opening:

**The decision is ____. The deadline is ____. The facts I trust are ____. I am
leaning toward ____ because ____. I want you to _____.**

That statement gives the adviser something falsifiable to work with.

## Stop Shopping for Permission

The speakers call out “shopping for advice”: asking people until one agrees
with a decision you already prefer. Multiple perspectives can be valuable, but
collecting endorsements is not triangulation. It hides ownership of the choice
and overweights the most agreeable answer.

Before each conversation, write what evidence would change your mind. After it,
record the adviser's assumptions and relevant experience, not merely the
recommendation. If five people disagree because they assume different runway,
customer behavior, or goals, the disagreement may be resolvable. A vote will
not resolve it.

## Try It Yourself: Write the Five-Line Brief

Set a **20-minute timer** for one decision due within 30 days. Complete the
opening template, then add one line for the adviser's relevant experience and
one line naming what they cannot know better than you, such as what your
customers said yesterday.

Send the brief before a 25-minute conversation. Reserve the final five minutes
to restate the options, assumptions, and next evidence-gathering action. The
exercise ends with a decision owner and deadline, not another advice meeting.

## Expertise Has Borders

Michael notes that an adviser often lacks facts on the ground. They cannot infer
what a founder's customers want or reconstruct an industry's full history on
demand. Professional experts need boundaries too: legal counsel can identify
legal risk, but the founder must specify the business objective and still own
the commercial decision.

There are counterconditions. In emergencies involving safety, law, harassment,
fraud, or acute mental health risk, do not limit the conversation to a tidy
founder-advice format. Escalate to qualified help. And when a team repeatedly
hides existential facts, the problem may be trust or governance rather than
meeting technique.

incentives, and the decisions for which you would not use them. Ask operators
about work they have actually done; ask customers about their own behavior; ask
lawyers about legal exposure. No single adviser gets a universal jurisdiction.

## Three Things to Keep

- State the real decision, deadline, known facts, current leaning, and desired advice mode up front.
- Compare advisers' assumptions and relevant experience instead of collecting votes or permission.
- Advice can improve a decision, but the founder retains ownership and access to ground truth.

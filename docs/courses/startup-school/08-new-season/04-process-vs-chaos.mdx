# Make Process Earn Its Keep

The weekly launch meeting has an agenda, a template, seven reviewers, and no
launches. Everyone is organized. The product is still late.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 13-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel reject an easy binary between process and
chaos. An orderly process can be ineffective; a messy-looking team can produce
excellent results. Their practical test is simpler: does the process create a
good, consistent, measurable outcome?

## Process Is Stored Learning

When a task is repeated and a best method is known, process prevents every
person from rediscovering it. The episode uses an assembly line making bolts:
standard production creates consistent output, while inspection detects a bad
bolt and removes it. The procedure earns its cost because the desired result is
clear and quality can be measured.

This logic fits payroll, incident response, code release, data backups, and
other recurring work where mistakes are expensive and novelty adds little.
Useful process compresses previous learning into a default path. It should
reduce variance, coordination cost, or risk.

Bad process reverses the relationship. The organization starts serving the
procedure. More participants, approvals, and documents become signs of
seriousness even when the output does not improve. In the speakers' exaggerated
factory image, a thousand people manage process while one person makes one bolt
a day.

## Source Case: Big-Company Caution in an EdTech Startup

Michael describes an EdTech startup exploring AI capabilities that had been
inconceivable two years earlier. The team believed children wanted to read
Pokemon books but treated copyright constraints as a reason not to probe the
risky product path. He characterizes this as applying Google-style carefulness
inside a startup whose job was precisely to explore what a large incumbent
would avoid.

The lesson is not to violate copyright. The episode explicitly acknowledges
that constraint. The sharper point is that a startup must search for a lawful
experiment rather than importing an incumbent's blanket caution. It might test
licensed characters, public-domain stories, creator partnerships, or the more
general hypothesis that familiar worlds increase reading. The constraint stays;
the exploration method changes.

This case exposes a common mismatch. Big companies often optimize a known
machine and protect a large existing asset. Startups usually need to discover a
machine. A procedure copied from the incumbent may efficiently prevent the
learning that justifies the startup's existence.

## Give Every Process a Contract

Before adding a recurring procedure, write four lines:

1. **Outcome:** What observable result should improve?
2. **Owner:** Who can change or remove the process?
3. **Cost:** How much team time does each cycle consume?
4. **Review:** When will evidence determine whether it stays?

Without those fields, process tends to become permanent by default. With them,
the team can preserve useful repetition while deleting ceremony that has
outlived its purpose.

## Try It Yourself: Delete or Defend One Ritual

Choose one recurring meeting, report, or approval and set a **20-minute timer**.
Write its intended output and inspect the last four cycles. Count the total
person-hours and note whether the output became faster, safer, more consistent,
or more accurate.

Make one bounded change for the next two cycles: remove an approver, shorten the
meeting, automate a check, or delete the ritual. Define the metric that will
decide whether the change remains. Do not redesign the entire company.

## Chaos Is Not Automatically Courage

Some procedures are imposed by law, safety, security, or hard-earned incident
history. A medical workflow, financial control, or production rollback deserves
more caution than an exploratory prototype. “We are a startup” does not repeal
consequences.

The opposite caveat matters too: a charismatic founder can call avoidable
confusion “speed.” If the same failure repeats, ownership is unclear, or quality
cannot be inspected, the team is not exploring. It is discarding learning.

<details>
<summary>⚙️ A process lifecycle (optional)</summary>

Start with the lightest repeatable path. Instrument the outcome and failure
rate. Add a step only when a recurring failure justifies its cost. Assign an
owner and review date. When the task, risk, or evidence changes, simplify or
retire the process. A living process has a reason to exist and a way to die.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Good process stores learning and improves a named, measurable outcome.
- Match process to the work: exploiting a known path and exploring a risky path need different controls.
- Repeated chaos is not speed; recurring failures should become checks, ownership, or a better default.

# Problems Money Can't Solve

A funding round can solve “we cannot pay for this known input.” It cannot solve
“we do not know why customers leave,” “our team does not care,” or “we are doing
three things because choosing one feels dangerous.” More cash can make those
problems quieter while making them larger.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 21-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel suggest treating money as one tool in a
larger box. Before raising or spending it, identify the bottleneck and explain
the mechanism by which capital changes that bottleneck. If the explanation is
“hire people and do stuff,” the mechanism is still missing.

## Name the Constraint Before the Budget

Money is effective when a working system is limited by a purchasable input. If
retained customers reliably repay acquisition cost in ten months, additional
marketing capital may accelerate a demonstrated loop. If a delivery team has a
known queue and a strong hiring process, another excellent operator may expand
capacity.

Money is weak against discovery and judgment problems:

- It cannot make customers want a product they do not value.
- It cannot decide which product direction deserves focus.
- It cannot turn a weak hiring process into a high-talent team.
- It cannot make employees care whether the company wins.
- It cannot convert an undefined executive role into useful leadership.

This distinction prevents paid activity from impersonating progress. A graph of
new users purchased with ads may rise at exactly the rate cash falls. Without
retention or contribution margin, the company has learned that an ad platform
can sell traffic, not that the startup has demand.

## Source Case: The Wrong Lesson From Workday

Michael describes a founder insisting that Workday's product was terrible. The
founder disliked characteristics that, according to Michael, enterprise buyers
actually valued and prioritized interface qualities those buyers cared less
about. Spending money to build the founder's imagined “better” product would
have accelerated in the wrong direction.

The constraint was customer understanding, not engineering capacity. Until the
founder learned how Workday's buyers evaluated reliability, configurability,
integration, and risk, a larger team would merely produce more unsupported
decisions.

This is a speaker's anecdote and interpretation, not a comparative product
study or endorsement of Workday. Enterprise customers are not uniform, and a
better interface may matter in some segments. The generalizable move is to test
which attributes drive the purchase before funding an expensive response.

## Capital Can Remove Useful Friction

Scarcity forces a small team to choose. After a large round, parallel projects,
premature executives, and headcount plans become possible. They can feel safer
because no opportunity is abandoned, yet limited founder attention and top
talent are now diluted across several learning loops.

The hosts call this early imitation of large-company behavior dangerous. It is
difficult to reverse spending once teams, expectations, and careers depend on
it. Money can also attract candidates motivated mainly by cash and signal to
employees that the company has already “made it,” even when product-market fit
is absent.

That does not make scarcity inherently noble. Underpaying people, skipping
health care, or denying teams necessary tools is not strategic discipline.
Money can improve basic working conditions and remove real capacity limits. The
heuristic is to preserve decision clarity, not manufacture hardship.

## Try It Yourself: Run the Capital Test

Choose one problem you currently describe as underfunded. In two minutes, fill
in four lines: “The bottleneck is...,” “An extra `\$100,000` buys...,” “That
input changes the bottleneck by...,” and “Within thirty days, we would observe....”

If the third line contains “hopefully,” replace the spend with the cheapest test
that would establish the missing causal link. Your output must be one decision:
spend, test first, or stop.

## Spend Behind Proof

The source gives examples of capital used after learning: DoorDash expanding
from a set of markets it understood into more markets, or a company funding
customer acquisition after validating payback and retention. The exact company
histories are simplified illustrations, but the sequence is sound:

1. Demonstrate customer value in a bounded setting.
2. Identify the repeatable input that produces more of it.
3. Verify the economics without convenient asterisks.
4. Raise or spend to scale that input.

Fundraising is then a trailing indicator of a working opportunity, not the
moment the underlying problems disappear. It also adds obligations: investor
expectations, board relationships, and employee assumptions about success.

<details>
<summary>🔧 A mechanism-versus-hope review (optional)</summary>

Label a proposed use of capital:

- **Mechanism:** Prior evidence connects the input to retained customer value.
- **Capacity:** Demand exists, but a specific resource limits delivery.
- **Experiment:** The spend buys bounded information with a stopping rule.
- **Hope:** The spend increases activity without explaining why outcomes improve.

Mechanism and capacity can justify scaling. An experiment needs a budget and
decision date. Hope needs discovery, not a larger round.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Capital works best on a named, purchasable constraint inside a mechanism already supported by evidence.
- Demand, focus, hiring quality, and culture require judgment and attention before they benefit from more money.
- Preserve useful discipline after fundraising by scaling proven loops instead of multiplying unsupported projects.

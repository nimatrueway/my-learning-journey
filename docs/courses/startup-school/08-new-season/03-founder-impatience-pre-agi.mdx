# Be Impatient With Work, Patient With Outcomes

A competitor posts a vertical revenue chart. A new model drops. Suddenly your
three-year plan feels like a handwritten map during a rocket launch, and every
week without explosive growth looks like proof that you chose wrong.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 15-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel argue that the pre-AGI moment does reward
urgency, but not indiscriminate panic. Their central tension is useful: founders
must be impatient enough to learn new tools and push hard today while remaining
patient enough for a consequential body of work to compound.

## Separate Two Clocks

The **learning clock** is fast. New capabilities create temporary advantages
for people who test them early, spend time with strong practitioners, and build
enough to discover what changed. Waiting a year for certainty can turn a live
question into old news.

The **outcome clock** is slower. Customers need trust, products need iteration,
teams need shared context, and markets adopt at uneven rates. Demanding that
these systems resolve at tool-release speed encourages shallow pivots.

The speakers' heuristic is not “relax.” It is to direct impatience toward
controllable work: learning, shipping, talking to users, and increasing the
quality of each attempt. Patience belongs to the lag between good work and a
large result.

## Source Case: The One-Year Rocket Test

Michael describes founders concluding that an idea is bad if it cannot reach
\$100 million in revenue within a year, especially while companies such as
Cursor or Lovable appear to be taking off. That comparison silently changes the
question from “Are users pulling this product forward?” to “Does our curve look
like the most exceptional current curve?”

Dalton counters with Palantir. He notes that the company began around 2002 and
was dismissed for perhaps its first 10 or 15 years as something like a
consulting company. Decades later, younger observers may encounter it as an
obvious success. The anecdote does not prove that slow companies eventually
win. Most do not. It shows that current cultural visibility is a poor clock for
judging how long durable work took.

Ask for evidence at the scale your company can actually produce: Are retained
users increasing? Is the painful workflow becoming easier? Is distribution
learning accumulating? Those signals can justify another cycle without
pretending patience alone creates progress.

## Do Not Let a Forecast Make the Decision

The episode explores a stronger claim: if AGI is imminent, perhaps students
should drop out, founders should abandon ordinary plans, and everyone should
maximize short-term position before the world changes. The speakers treat this
as a forecast with enormous uncertainty, not a calendar fact.

You can act on the part that survives multiple futures. Learning powerful tools,
working with capable people, and shipping useful products remain valuable if
AGI arrives quickly, slowly, or under a different definition. Burning every
bridge only pays in a much narrower branch of the future.

## Try It Yourself: Run Two Clocks

Set a **25-minute timer**. Write one AI-era anxiety at the top of a page. Under
“seven days,” define one tool to learn, one artifact to ship, and three users to
observe. Under “six months,” define the outcome you hope will compound and two
monthly signals that would support continuing.

Finally, write a stop condition. If the signals remain flat after a specified
number of cycles, you will revise the thesis. This keeps patience from becoming
an alibi and urgency from becoming random motion.

## Some Windows Really Are Short

Patience is dangerous when a platform change creates a brief distribution
opening, cash is running out, a regulatory deadline is fixed, or users are
actively leaving. In those cases, speed may dominate elegance. Likewise, a
student with a rare, time-sensitive opportunity may rationally change plans.

The countercondition is evidence. “The era feels historic” is not enough.
Name the window, what closes it, and why your action captures it. If you cannot,
you may be reacting to status pressure rather than an operating constraint.

<details>
<summary>⏳ A reversible urgency test (optional)</summary>

For a major decision, ask: Which benefits survive if the AGI timeline is wrong?
Which costs are reversible? What evidence will arrive within 30 days? Prefer
actions with useful learning across several futures. Reserve irreversible bets
for cases where the window and your advantage are unusually concrete.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Be impatient with learning, shipping, and contact with reality; those are under your control.
- Judge progress with company-specific evidence, not the fastest public growth curve of the moment.
- A dramatic technology forecast should change irreversible plans only when the window is concrete.

# How Great Founders Approach Sales

A customer does not want your burrito. Cutting its price does not create
hunger. It may only make them wonder what is wrong with the burrito.

That joke captures a serious sales error: negotiating before discovering value.

⏱️ **~6 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 21-minute source video is optional

## Sales Is Applied Problem Solving

Founders often picture sales as pressure, charm, or a heroic feature recital.
The hosts offer a different model: listen until you understand the customer's
business, identify a costly problem, and determine whether your product can
actually relieve it. A good conversation feels less like winning an argument
and more like debugging with another operator.

The intermediate logic matters. A feature has no value in isolation. It changes
a workflow; that workflow changes a business result; and that result must matter
enough for somebody to act. If you cannot explain those links, a discount only
makes an unclear proposition cheaper. It does not make it useful.

## Source Case: The CEO Is Part of the Sales System

The hosts use Palantir's very large enterprise deals to challenge the fantasy
that a junior salesperson independently originates a huge Boeing contract.
Their account emphasizes executive sponsorship: founders or CEOs open or scout
important relationships, hand work to the sales team, and return when a deal
needs debugging. The team multiplies founder leverage; it does not make the
founder disappear.

That case explains why hiring a VP of Sales too early often disappoints. Before
there is a repeatable playbook, the new executive is being asked to discover the
customer, positioning, process, and team simultaneously. Skills from operating
an established sales machine may not transfer cleanly to inventing one.

The practical sequence is founder discovery, repeated wins, an explicit
playbook, and then delegation. Even later, strategic accounts may still require
founder-level access and judgment.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Problem Chain

Choose one real prospect. In two minutes, write four lines:

1. Their business is trying to achieve **___**.
2. The current workflow fails because **___**.
3. That failure costs or risks **___**.
4. Our product changes the result by **___**, measured as **___**.

Circle every blank supported only by your assumption. Your next call has one
job: ask enough neutral questions to replace the riskiest assumption with the
customer's evidence. Do not pitch a new feature during this exercise.

## Bespoke Work Has a Boundary

One host recounts an early startup declining a potentially multimillion-dollar
Disney project: build and operate a tightly controlled, white-label social
network for park fans. The objection was not merely that the work looked like
consulting. It would have redirected the company into bespoke software and
operations that might never generalize.

But the hosts caution against rejecting every deep integration as “consulting.”
Early manual work can reveal the problem and later become productized. The
countercondition is opportunity cost: if one customer owns the roadmap and the
result cannot serve a broader market at attractive margins, revenue may be a
detour rather than validation.

<details>
<summary>🧭 A founder-to-team handoff test (optional)</summary>

Before hiring a sales leader, document three recent wins. For each, record who
created access, the problem discovered, the proof that changed the buyer's mind,
the objections, and every founder-only intervention. A playbook is becoming
delegable when those patterns repeat and another person can run most of them
without pretending the founder's relationships do not exist.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Sales creates value by understanding and solving a consequential customer problem.
- A sales team should multiply a founder's learned playbook, not substitute for discovery.
- Bespoke work is useful learning when it can generalize; it is dangerous when one customer captures the company.

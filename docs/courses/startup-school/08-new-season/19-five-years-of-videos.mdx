# 5 Years of Dalton + Michael: What We've Learned Making Videos

Advice can become another form of procrastination: one more framework, one more
episode, one more elegant explanation of why the company remains exactly where
it was.

⏱️ **~7 min** · ~1,050 words at 200 wpm + 2 minutes of practice · the 13-minute source video is optional

After five years of making videos, Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel say the
startup fundamentals still feel calming because they reduce complicated stories
to a few decisions. Their sharper retrospective insight is that advice should
help a founder cross into the next stage, not merely become more comfortable in
the current one.

## Principles Repeat; Bottlenecks Move

Talking to users, building useful things, and choosing a strong cofounder remain
recognizable across stages. Their operational meaning changes.

An idea-stage team may need one painful problem and three credible users. A
company with early pull may need a repeatable way to serve ten times as many.
A later-stage company may need a new market, organization, or distribution
system. Applying a true principle at the wrong resolution creates polished
stagnation.

Use a transition diagnosis:

| Current evidence | Next-stage evidence | Likely bottleneck |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People describe the pain | A few people repeatedly use a solution | Problem and user access |
| A few people use it | Usage or revenue repeats without founder heroics | Product reliability and distribution |
| One channel works | Multiple teams or markets can reproduce growth | Systems, leadership, and expansion |

These are orientation heuristics, not universal stage gates. Consumer,
enterprise, biotech, and regulated companies produce evidence on different
timelines.

## Source Case: An Accidental Five-Year Practice

The hosts say they did not begin with a five-year content plan and did not
expect the videos to become what many people knew them for. Viewers responded
partly because the conversations looked enjoyable and natural. Repetition also
gave the hosts a library that reminded them of their own advice.

That case supports a modest lesson about compounding: a useful practice can
become meaningful before its long-term identity is obvious. The mechanism was
not only consistency. The format fit their relationship, the subject connected
to years of founder advising, and the feedback stayed positive enough to
continue. “Post for five years” without those conditions is not the lesson.

## Optimize for Passage, Not Decoration

Michael's retrospective is that he would spend more advising time helping
founders move between stages and less time optimizing the stage they already
occupy. Translate that into a weekly question:

**What evidence is missing for the next stage, and what action could create it?**

Suppose five design partners like a workflow but every deployment requires the
founder. Improving the dashboard may make the present state nicer. Documenting,
automating, or narrowing onboarding attacks the transition to repeatability.
The second choice may feel less glamorous because it exposes whether the
business can cross at all.

## Try It Yourself: Make a 25-Minute Transition Card

Set a 25-minute timer and fill one index card or half-page:

1. **Current stage:** What can the company reliably do today?
2. **Next stage:** What new capability would materially change its options?
3. **Missing evidence:** What would prove that capability exists?
4. **Seven-day test:** What one action could produce that evidence?
5. **Stop rule:** What result would make you change the approach?

Choose a test that can finish within seven days. Do not redesign the whole
company or create a twelve-month roadmap.

## Simple Is Not the Same as Easy

The source celebrates fundamentals while acknowledging that every stage feels
stressful and essential. “Keep it simple” should remove invented complexity,
not erase real technical, market, organizational, or regulatory constraints.

Advice also suffers from survivorship and selection effects. The hosts draw on
many founder conversations, but a concise video cannot specify every
countercondition. Treat a principle as a question generator, then test it
against your market and evidence.

<details>
<summary>🎥 A useful-practice durability test (optional)</summary>

Before committing to a repeated public or internal practice, check four forms
of fit:

- **Energy:** Do the participants still want to do it after novelty fades?
- **Capability:** Does the practice draw on something they can genuinely teach or produce?
- **Audience:** Is there repeated evidence that someone finds it useful?
- **Learning:** Does repetition improve the work or reveal new questions?

The Dalton + Michael series appears to have had all four. Consistency without
fit can compound waste just as reliably as value.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Durable principles recur across startup stages, but the evidence and bottleneck change.
- Advice is most useful when it helps a company cross a stage rather than decorate its current one.
- A repeated practice compounds when format, capability, audience value, and participant energy align.

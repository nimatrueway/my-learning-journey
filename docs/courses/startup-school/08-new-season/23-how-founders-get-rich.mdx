# How Startup Founders Actually Get Rich (Quick?)

“We will build for eighteen months and sell” sounds like a plan. Usually it is
a wish wearing a spreadsheet. The buyer has not agreed to exist, the product
has not earned attention, and the founder has quietly chosen a business they do
not want to run if the shortcut fails.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel do not deny that fast acquisitions happen.
They challenge whether speed can be selected directly. Their counterintuitive
claim is that a founder becomes more acquisition-worthy by building durable,
interesting value than by optimizing for the appearance of a quick exit.

## The Exit Timeline Is Not Yours Alone

Michael contrasts two companies from his experience. Socialcam spun out of
Justin.tv and sold in roughly six months, while the path that became Twitch took
years to discover and more time to exit. Early in his career, a startup lawyer
told him that exits commonly took around eight to twelve years; his own company
sold about eight years later.

That range is an anecdotal heuristic, not a forecast distribution. Markets,
company types, financing, and survivorship all distort the visible examples.
The useful lesson is narrower: if the company only makes sense under a fast
sale, an event outside your control is carrying the entire strategy.

Longer time horizons also change behavior. A founder expecting to operate the
business must create customer value, preserve trust, and choose work worth
repeating. A founder imagining a quick flip can rationalize shallow demand,
expensive growth, or an idea chosen only because investors currently like its
category.

## Optimize for Being Worth Buying

An acquirer can copy a generic feature more easily than it can copy a respected
builder, a distinctive technical asset, an active community, or hard-won domain
knowledge. This gives a practical test for “strategic value”:

1. Is the work meaningfully different from common templates?
2. Do credible people use it, discuss it, or contribute to it voluntarily?
3. Does the founder understand the problem beyond a fundraising narrative?
4. Would continuing independently still be a coherent choice?

None guarantees an acquisition. They are leading signals that real value may
exist without requiring a buyer to rescue the company.

## Source Case: Bun Earned Builder Attention

The hosts point to Bun, a JavaScript runtime and toolkit, as an example of a
founder becoming notable by doing technically interesting public work. In their
account, the founder first pursued a niche social product, pivoted, shared Bun's
progress repeatedly, and earned respect from other builders before an eventual
acquisition.

The mechanism matters more than the reported payoff. Public artifacts let
skilled observers evaluate the work. Repeated releases built credibility. A
specific technical point of view made the project harder to confuse with a
cookie-cutter startup.

The hosts mention acquisition timing and value conversationally; those details
are not independently verified by the cached source. This is one successful
story with severe selection bias, not proof that open-source attention reliably
produces wealth. The controllable move is to make valuable work legible, not to
assume a particular company will buy it.

## Try It Yourself: Delete the Buyer

Take your current one-year plan and cross out every line that depends on an
acquisition, fundraising round, or viral moment. You have two minutes to replace
one deleted line with a proof you can produce in thirty days: a retained user
cohort, a respected open-source release, a paid workflow, or a hard technical
milestone.

Write who can inspect the proof and what reaction would count as signal. “People
will love it” does not count. “Five domain engineers install it twice in one
month and two contribute fixes” does.

## Enjoyment Is Strategic Runway

The hosts' final advice is not “follow your passion and money appears.” It is
that liking the problem, product, or customers makes sustained effort more
plausible. Twitch improved after pivoting toward work its team cared about.
Enjoyment can support the years of iteration that the fast-exit story omits.

There are counterconditions. A beloved project can have no market, builder
admiration may not become revenue, and personal interest does not excuse weak
customer discovery. Treat enjoyment as one source of endurance, not evidence
of demand.

multi-party obligation.

## Three Things to Keep

- Fast acquisitions happen, but timing and buyer appetite are not a strategy a founder can command.
- Distinctive work and inspectable proof create more strategic value than rehearsing an exit narrative.
- Choose a problem and people you can stay with if the supposedly quick path becomes long.

# Building A Big Company: Non-Obvious Insights

The product works. Revenue is growing. The team improves conversion every month.
Yet the current path still ends at a company much smaller than the one you intend
to build.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 22-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel draw a stage boundary. Before product-market
fit, strategy can become sophisticated procrastination. After product-market
fit, endlessly optimizing the same product can trap a company at a local
maximum. This boundary is their operating heuristic, not a precise threshold.

## Earn the Right to Strategize

Before product-market fit, the main uncertainties are concrete: does anyone want
the product, is it meaningfully better, and will users return or pay? Talking to
users and shipping tests answer those questions. A day spent debating how to
defeat a giant incumbent cannot rescue a product nobody uses.

After product-market fit, the company has a different asset: a working model of
customers, their needs, and why the product helps. That knowledge makes larger
decisions less imaginary. The question changes from “Can we create value?” to
“Can this position compound into the scale and defensibility we need?”

A strategy review is warranted when the current trajectory has evidence but a
visible ceiling: a constrained market, a weak distribution position, an
incumbent bundle, poor switching costs, or a category in which only one or two
players capture most value.

## Source Case: Facebook Left Its Best Niche

The hosts trace Facebook from a sticky network restricted to supported
universities. Exclusivity helped the product: students saw relevant peers, while
parents and younger users stayed out. It also created an obvious ceiling.

Facebook then made moves that were not simple feature improvements. It admitted
employees at selected companies, later high-school students, and eventually
anyone. It bet heavily on mobile while its revenue still came from the web. It
made other strategic bets that did not all work, including a phone effort, and
it acquired Instagram. In the hosts' telling, becoming a huge advertising
business required reach that the original college niche could not supply.

The case supports strategic ambition, but hindsight distorts it. Other funded
social networks also made choices and failed. Facebook's wins do not provide an
algorithm for knowing which departure will work; they show that preserving a
successful niche can itself become a risk.

## Try It Yourself: Run the Strategy Gate

Give yourself 20 minutes. First choose one stage: **pre-PMF**, **uncertain**, or
**post-PMF**, and cite two behavioral facts such as retention, repeated purchase,
or pull from a defined customer segment.

If pre-PMF or uncertain, choose one user conversation or product test to run this
week and stop. If post-PMF, estimate the three-year ceiling of the current path,
name the constraint producing it, and design one reversible two-week test of a
larger market, bundle, channel, or use case. Do not commit the company in this
exercise; purchase information first.

## Source Case: A Burrito Before General Logistics

DoorDash is the source's second scale case. The hosts say Tony Xu thought about
delivery more broadly than restaurant food, eventually placing the company in
competition with Amazon's logistics experience. Michael gives a contemporary
customer observation: local-store fulfillment can make products feel more
trustworthy and arrive faster than goods discovered in a marketplace crowded
with uncertain listings.

But the sequence matters. Before DoorDash could discuss general logistics, it
had to deliver a burrito in Palo Alto. The narrow operation created the customer
and logistics knowledge from which a broader strategy could emerge. Grand
strategy did not substitute for the first working loop.

Large-company competition also deserves calibration. The speakers treat “What
if Google builds it?” as mostly unhelpful for an early startup, yet relevant once
incumbents notice the category. Their suggested lens is the customer's switching
decision: bundles, defaults, procurement, data, and workflow integration may
matter more than feature quality alone. They cite Microsoft Teams versus Slack
as a partly secondhand interpretation and explicitly mark that account as not
first-party, so it should remain a hypothesis rather than settled history.

<details>
<summary>🧭 A post-PMF local-maximum review (optional)</summary>

Ask four questions:

1. If current growth continues, how large can this exact product and segment become?
2. Which customer behavior or market structure creates the ceiling?
3. What advantage from the current business transfers to the next position?
4. What is the cheapest test that could disprove the proposed strategic move?

Do not use a fundraising milestone as the endpoint. The hosts recommend studying
companies that reached durable public scale, while acknowledging that market
structures differ and some categories support several winners.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Before product-market fit, customer contact and shipping beat elaborate competitive strategy.
- After product-market fit, optimizing the current product may preserve a local maximum rather than create a large company.
- Big strategic moves require transferable advantage, a specific ceiling, and tests that respect uncertainty.

# How To Influence Decision Makers

Important choices about you are often made after you leave the room. The final
promotion discussion, investment committee, budget review, or hiring debrief
does not include your closing argument.

Influence is what remains when you are absent.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 13-minute source video is optional

## The Decision Includes More Than Your Proposal

Caldwell and Seibel begin with an uncomfortable premise: a coherent factual case
is only table stakes. Decision makers also ask what accepting the proposal will
commit them to. A promotion creates more meetings with you. An investment may
create a board relationship lasting years. A larger budget increases dependence
on your judgment.

That means people are evaluating two things at once:

1. Is the proposed action sound?
2. Does this person's past behavior make the future commitment attractive?

You cannot repair a weak proposal with charm. You also cannot assume the facts
erase every signal around them. Preparation, temperament, reputation, and the
way colleagues respond to you become evidence in the later conversation.

## Build the Evidence Before You Need the Yes

The strongest form of influence is a record of useful work. The episode offers
a severe but clarifying test for a new employee: about 90 days after the person
starts, imagine they announce that today is their last day. If the manager would
not feel genuine distress, the employee has not yet become essential to an
important outcome.

This is not advice to hoard knowledge or create operational fragility. It is a
test of contribution. Do you own a result, understand a difficult system, earn
customer trust, or reliably unblock other people? A title asks an organization
to predict future value. A visible track record reduces how much prediction is
required.

The same principle applies to founders. If a product disappeared tomorrow,
would customers scramble to replace it? A company that has become important to
customers enters a financing decision with more leverage than one relying on a
beautiful forecast.

## Read the Culture Without Performing Politics

Attempts to influence can backfire when they violate the decision maker's norms.
The hosts describe founders sending elaborate messages to force a brief meeting
about YC admission. The tactic signals that the sender has not understood the
selection process. In another organization, thoughtful internal advocacy might
be expected. The behavior cannot be judged without the culture.

Before lobbying, learn how good decisions normally travel. Do leaders expect a
written memo, a prototype, support from peers, or a direct conversation? Then
make the relevant evidence easy to inspect. Excessive maneuvering is risky
because a single awkward campaign can become the story repeated after you leave.

How you handle a refusal also enters that record. A calm response preserves the
next opportunity. Retaliation, frantic escalation, or contempt teaches the room
what future disagreement with you will cost.

## The Runway Story: Why Desperation Changes a Pitch

The episode connects influence to bargaining position through YC's standard
deal. When companies previously received $125,000, founders could approach Demo
Day with little runway and an urgent need for one investor to say yes. Investors
with many repetitions could recognize that dependence, even when the spoken
pitch sounded confident.

YC later increased its standard investment to $500,000. With substantially more
runway, the same team at the same stage could enter a meeting knowing it would
survive a no. The product facts had not changed, but the founder's attachment to
one outcome had. That calmer posture was easier to associate with effectiveness.

The lesson is not to pretend you do not care. It is to create alternatives
before the meeting. More runway, several qualified candidates, a smaller first
request, or another route to the goal prevents one decision maker from becoming
your only plan.

## Original Example: A Budget Request That Travels

A product lead wants two engineers for onboarding work. Instead of presenting a
large redesign, she ships one low-cost fix, shows that activation rose from 31%
to 36%, and documents the next bottleneck. She asks for one engineer for six
weeks, with a review if activation does not reach 40%.

When finance discusses the request without her, the room has a completed result,
a bounded commitment, and a stopping condition. Her influence comes from making
the decision defensible, not from delivering the loudest presentation.

## Try It Yourself: Write the Conversation After You Leave

Choose one request you will make this month. Write a six-line shadow brief:

1. The exact decision and smallest useful commitment.
2. The result you have already produced that lowers perceived risk.
3. What the decision maker becomes responsible for after saying yes.
4. The strongest reasonable objection another person may raise.
5. A safeguard, review date, or stopping rule that answers that objection.
6. Your credible path forward if the answer is no.

Give the brief to a colleague who will not attend the meeting. Ask them to argue
both sides. If they cannot reproduce your case, the absent-room version needs
work.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Influence without manipulation (optional)</summary>

Reputation and cultural awareness become manipulation when they conceal material
facts, manufacture pressure, or punish dissent. Durable influence lets the other
person make a better-informed choice and defend it later. Also avoid making
yourself "irreplaceable" by withholding access or documentation. The healthy
version creates exceptional value while leaving the organization operable.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Decision makers assess both the proposal and the future relationship created by accepting it.
- A track record of valuable work and credible alternatives influence the room more reliably than last-minute lobbying.
- Make the request easy to repeat: state the evidence, bounded commitment, objection, safeguard, and response to a no.

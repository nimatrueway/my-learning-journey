# What Is ZIRP And How Did It Poison Startups?

Zero interest-rate policy sounds like background economics. For startups, it
changed who invested, how quickly money moved, what companies were priced at,
and which founder habits appeared successful.

The poison was not cash itself. It was mistaking abundant cash for validation.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 18-minute source video is optional

## Follow the Money Before Judging the Behavior

Under ZIRP, central-bank rates sat near zero. Banks and large pools of capital
could earn little from conservative assets, so they searched for higher yield.
Some of that money moved into real estate, private funds, family offices, and
venture capital. Fund managers were also paid to manage and deploy capital,
giving the system another reason to expand.

Caldwell and Seibel compare the effect to subsidized corn. When corn becomes
cheap and abundant, industries discover more products that can contain corn.
During ZIRP, nearly every startup problem attracted the same proposed ingredient:
more money. Weak margins, uncertain product-market fit, and labor-intensive
operations could all be temporarily covered by financing.

This changed the apparent scorecard. A larger round produced a higher valuation,
more hiring, and more press. None of those outcomes necessarily showed that
customers valued the product or that the company could survive the next market.

## New Investors Brought the Wrong Measuring Stick

The episode recalls an investment firm whose background was trading mature
public companies. It tried to adapt a public-market valuation rubric to startups
with roughly $1 million in revenue. The arithmetic looked familiar, but the risk
was not. A public company producing hundreds of millions in revenue has survived
many tests. A small startup can disappear before reaching the next milestone.

Because capital was plentiful, some firms could offer founders tens of millions
of dollars after a short conversation. Founders sought introductions precisely
because the money seemed unusually easy. When rates rose, at least one such firm
left venture investing entirely. Its appetite had been a feature of the funding
environment, not durable conviction about early-stage companies.

This is a crucial distinction for founders: investor demand is evidence about
the market for startup equity. It is not direct evidence about the market for
your product.

## The 350-Times-Revenue Warning

In a board meeting, Seibel heard a founder compare acquisition targets using
revenue-to-valuation multiples. The idea prompted him to inspect newly announced
unicorn rounds. One company with about $3 million in revenue had raised at a
valuation around $1 billion, roughly 350 times revenue.

He compared that stage with Justin.tv and Twitch when they had similar revenue.
At that point, those companies still had enormous uncertainty ahead. The billion-
dollar price did not mean the new company had resolved that uncertainty. It
showed how aggressively capital was pricing the possibility of future success.

The word *unicorn* originally described rarity. During the boom, new unicorns
appeared so frequently that the label became a marketing milestone. A valuation
could rise much faster than the underlying company learned.

## Cheap Capital Can Manufacture Product-Market Fit

Lending startups make the rate dependency especially visible. When a lender can
borrow cheaply and lend at a higher rate, many customers want its product. As
the hosts note, money has unusually reliable demand. That popularity can make
the team feel like it discovered a brilliant customer need.

When interest rates rise, the lender's input cost increases. Passing the full
increase to customers may destroy demand, while absorbing it may destroy the
margin. A whole category can become difficult almost overnight. The customer
did not suddenly stop wanting money. The economic engine supporting the offer
changed.

Every startup should ask whether a celebrated metric is being purchased by a
temporary subsidy. Discounts can buy growth. Hiring can hide inefficient
software. A large balance sheet can postpone pricing. The hidden bill arrives
when the subsidy ends.

## Original Example: Growth With a Rate Switch

A payroll startup advances wages to small businesses and charges a modest fee.
At a 2% cost of capital, each advance contributes enough margin to fund support
and defaults. At 8%, the same fee loses money. The team could raise prices, but
interviews show customers would return to ordinary payroll timing.

Its user count was real, yet the business model depended on financing conditions
outside the product. A sound plan would either secure durable funding economics,
charge for additional value customers retain at higher rates, or reduce exposure
before scaling the balance sheet.

## Try It Yourself: Run a Funding Winter Test

Choose your fastest-growing metric and answer:

1. How much cash is spent to produce one unit of growth?
2. Which part comes from lasting product improvement, and which part comes from
   discounts, headcount, or cheap financing?
3. What happens if the next round takes 18 months and costs twice as much in
   dilution?
4. Which expense could be removed without hurting retained customer value?
5. What milestone would let revenue or learning finance the next step?

Write one change you can make this quarter. "Raise before conditions worsen" is
not enough because it preserves the dependency instead of reducing it.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Capital is a tool, not a diagnosis (optional)</summary>

Some founders used boom-era capital intelligently. The episode describes
companies that preserved enough cash, reached profitability, and no longer
needed another private round before an eventual public offering. Underfunding
can also kill a business that should invest aggressively. The test is whether
capital accelerates a working mechanism or substitutes for discovering one.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- ZIRP pushed capital toward risk and made fundraising, valuation, and hiring easier without guaranteeing business quality.
- Cheap inputs can make demand and growth look durable, especially when discounts, labor, or financing hide weak economics.
- Build for multiple capital climates by knowing burn, runway, retention, margins, and which milestones reduce dependence on the next round.

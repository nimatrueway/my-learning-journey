# When Should You Trust Your Gut?

One founder has built an internal tool for years, watched colleagues depend on
it, and knows exactly what feels wrong in competing products. Another is bright,
technical, and searching for a promising market.

Both have instincts. Only one has earned much weight in this particular domain.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

## Intuition Is Local, Not a Personality Trait

Caldwell and Seibel frame the choice as **commit or validate**. Founders often
treat "trust your gut" as a universal identity: visionary people commit, cautious
people research. The episode makes the advice conditional. Intuition becomes
useful through repeated contact with a problem, decisions, and feedback.

An experienced founder may know more than they can fully explain. They have seen
edge cases, watched users improvise, tried alternatives, and developed taste
about what good work looks like. Their gut compresses those repetitions.

A newcomer can feel equally certain, but the certainty may come from analogy,
fashion, or one personal frustration. That is not disqualifying. It means the
startup must initially be a machine for building expertise.

## Evidence That You Have Earned a Commit

The episode's first founder built something valuable inside a company. Other
employees adopted it, and they continued using it after the founder left. That
external behavior matters. Expertise is not merely thinking you have excellent
taste; other people repeatedly received value from decisions you made.

You can reasonably give your intuition more weight when several conditions hold:

- you have performed the user's work or solved the problem repeatedly;
- people used, bought, or depended on something you built in the domain;
- you can name specific failures in existing alternatives;
- you hold detailed opinions that differ from generic market commentary;
- feedback from past decisions arrived quickly enough to correct your model.

In that situation, excessive validation can dilute the insight. Asking random
people what to build may replace hard-won judgment with average preferences.
The better standard may be to create something that would genuinely impress you,
then put it in front of users who share the problem.

## The Same Mortgage Idea, Two Different Plans

The hosts compare two teams building software for mortgage underwriters. In the
first, a founder spent ten years inside mortgage underwriting and built software
for the work. They know which vendor tools disappoint, why exceptions occur, and
which tasks consume an underwriter's day. If they believe underwriters need a
particular workflow, that belief deserves a substantial head start.

The second team has no industry experience. Perhaps the founders bought a house,
found the process strange, and concluded that real estate is broken. They could
still build an excellent company, but they should not spend two years creating a
comprehensive system from market reports and personal annoyance. Their first
product should be small enough to expose them to real underwriting work.

The failure pattern runs both ways. Experts sometimes abandon what they know to
build whatever investors currently favor. In the episode, an experienced team
might force AI into its pitch even when the important customer problem does not
require AI. Meanwhile, novices may produce hiring plans and large market models
before learning the basic workflow. The expert acts needlessly uncertain; the
novice acts prematurely certain.

## Match Validation to the Knowledge Gap

Do not assign one confidence score to the whole startup. Break the decision into
claims. A compiler expert may have excellent product taste but weak knowledge of
enterprise procurement. A nurse may deeply understand shift handoffs but know
little about hospital security reviews.

For each claim, choose a method:

- **Commit** when expertise is deep, feedback history is strong, and the decision
  is reversible enough to correct after launch.
- **Prototype** when you understand the problem but are uncertain about the
  interaction or technical approach.
- **Interview or observe** when you lack direct contact with the user's work.
- **Pre-sell or pilot** when willingness to pay or organizational adoption is
  the unknown.

Validation should target what you do not know. Repeating interviews about a pain
you have lived for a decade can become avoidance. Building a complete platform
to learn whether anyone has the pain is expensive confidence theater.

## Original Example: The Handoff Checklist

A former emergency-department nurse wants to build a shift-handoff tool. After
thousands of handoffs, she trusts her judgment that interruptions and missing
task ownership cause dangerous confusion. She commits to a fast, role-based
checklist rather than surveying strangers about the basic problem.

She does not assume expertise everywhere. She prototypes the checklist with two
nurses, interviews an IT administrator about integration, and asks a department
manager to approve a paid pilot. Her domain intuition determines the product
thesis. Focused validation covers workflow details, security, and purchasing.

## Try It Yourself: Create an Expertise Ledger

Choose one product decision and list its three most important claims. For each,
write:

1. the number of times you directly performed or observed the relevant work;
2. an external result that confirmed or corrected your judgment;
3. what you know specifically that a smart newcomer probably would not;
4. the cost and reversibility of acting now;
5. whether to commit, prototype, observe, or pre-sell.

Act on the weakest claim within 48 hours. If you lack expertise, do not write a
larger plan. Get closer to the work. If you have strong expertise, stop asking
general audiences for permission and ship the smallest expression of your view.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Expertise can expire or become defensive (optional)</summary>

Deep experience can anchor a founder to an old workflow, especially after a
technology or regulation changes. Intuition is also vulnerable to ego: "I know
this market" can become protection from unwelcome evidence. Keep a disconfirming
signal and review point for consequential bets. The lesson is calibrated trust,
not immunity from users.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Intuition earns weight through relevant repetitions and external feedback, not confidence or founder identity.
- Experts should commit to the insight they have earned while testing unfamiliar channels, buyers, and constraints.
- Newcomers can win by starting small, observing real work, and using each product cycle to build domain expertise.

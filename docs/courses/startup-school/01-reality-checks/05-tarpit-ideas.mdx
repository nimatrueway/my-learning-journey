# Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel

A tarpit idea does not look foolish. It looks like a clean solution to a problem
everyone recognizes. Friends praise it. New founders independently discover it.
Many teams try it. Somehow, the same obstacle keeps winning.

That repeated attraction is part of the definition.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 14-minute source video is optional

## What Makes an Idea a Tarpit

Caldwell and Seibel introduced the term after seeing the same startup concepts
in application after application. YC funded multiple attempts, yet the category
kept failing. Like a pool that appears safe until an animal steps into it, the
idea reveals its trapping condition only after founders commit time and money.

Not every weak idea is a tarpit. A tarpit has three features together:

- **Frequent rediscovery:** many founders arrive at it independently.
- **A persuasive surface:** ordinary people immediately recognize the problem
  and often respond positively.
- **An unchanged blocker:** previous attempts repeatedly failed for a reason the
  new team has not removed.

The label is not a static blacklist. Technology, regulation, cost, or behavior
can change. A former tarpit can become viable when the actual blocker moves.
The founder's job is to identify that blocker, not merely announce that the
current version uses newer tools.

## Praise Is Not Retention

Friend-group coordination is a classic shape from the episode. Nearly everyone
has struggled to choose a restaurant or find a time to meet. An app that shows
who is free sounds obviously useful, and friends are unlikely to discourage a
founder trying to reduce loneliness.

The hidden requirement is collective behavior change. The product becomes useful
only if enough friends install it, keep availability current, and respond. One
inactive person reduces value for everyone else. Better calendar screens do not
necessarily solve that participation loop.

This explains why verbal enthusiasm is weak evidence for tarpit-shaped ideas.
The user can sincerely love the outcome while declining the repeated behavior
required to create it. Test continued use, not recognition of the problem.

## Ask What Changed Since the Failures

The episode treats modern AI as a reason to reopen some old questions. It points
to a YC company producing personalized, AI-generated podcasts. Seibel played one
for his wife; it sounded enough like a conventional program that she assumed he
had given an interview when a synthetic segment used his voice and ideas.

Years earlier, the same product would have required costly writing, recording,
editing, and personalization. Better language and voice generation changed a
capability constraint. That does not prove people will return or pay, but it
creates a defensible reason to test the category again.

Contrast that with adding "copilot" to every profession. A copilot for lawyers,
real-estate agents, or dentists is common, but common does not yet mean tarpit.
There may not be enough historical attempts. The useful question remains
specific: which task is newly possible or dramatically cheaper, and was that
task the old reason products failed?

## Two Common Traps Beneath the Surface

One trap is utopian coordination: the product works if many people adopt a new,
socially beneficial habit together. The friend app fits here. The founder must
show a wedge that creates value before universal participation.

The other is temporary arbitrage. During the pandemic, some founders saw gains
shared in online trading communities and wanted to turn friends' money into an
investment-advisory startup as quickly as possible. The apparent advantage was
not proprietary insight or durable distribution. It was a rising market and a
short window of excitement. When conditions changed, the startup premise changed
with them.

An idea built on "this cannot possibly remain this easy" deserves a different
test from an idea built on a ten-year customer problem.

## Original Example: AI Restaurant Planning

A team proposes an AI assistant that chooses a restaurant for a group chat. The
model can summarize dietary needs and compare menus, which genuinely lowers the
research cost. But earlier coordination apps did not fail because reading menus
was too expensive. They failed because friends ignored prompts, changed plans,
and resisted installing another app.

The team can escape the old trap only with a changed adoption mechanism. For
example, one organizer might send a no-install voting link that produces value
for the group in a single session. The new model solves research; the link tests
whether participation can also be redesigned.

## Try It Yourself: Perform a Tarpit Excavation

Choose an idea that receives immediate praise. Spend 30 minutes on this record:

1. Find three prior products that served the same user and desired outcome.
2. For each, record whether it failed on capability, acquisition, retention,
   trust, payment, regulation, or required behavior change.
3. Circle the failure mode appearing more than once.
4. State the external change you believe removes it.
5. Design a seven-day test of the changed mechanism, not merely the feature.

For a coordination product, the measure might be how many invited groups complete
a second event without founder reminders. A polished demo cannot answer that.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Do not let history become fatalism (optional)</summary>

Previous failure is evidence, not destiny. Incumbents may have executed poorly,
entered too early, or targeted the wrong wedge. Conversely, a new technology can
remove one constraint while leaving the decisive one intact. Use history to
write a sharper experiment. Do not use it either to ban an idea or excuse it
from contact with users.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A tarpit is unusually attractive, repeatedly attempted, and blocked by a condition that has not changed.
- New technology matters only when it attacks the historical failure mode, not when it merely refreshes the pitch.
- Research previous attempts, then test retention, participation, payment, or another mechanism the old products could not sustain.

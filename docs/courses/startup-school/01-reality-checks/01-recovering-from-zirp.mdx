# Recovering From ZIRP

Cheap capital can make activity look like traction. The harder question arrives
later: has the company rebuilt the habits that survive when money costs money?

⏱️ **~8 min** · the 18-minute source video is optional

<Meme quote="A big part of ZIRP was using money to not have to make any hard decisions." author="Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel, Y Combinator" />

## The Idea: Recovery Means Choosing Again

**ZIRP** means *zero interest-rate policy*: a period when central-bank rates sat
near zero and capital became unusually cheap. Caldwell and Seibel use the joking
diagnosis **"Zirpes"** for the habits that cheap money encouraged inside tech
companies. When financing is abundant, a company can postpone uncomfortable
choices. It can fund projects with no customer, preserve fuzzy roles, add perks,
and celebrate activity without asking whether the underlying business works.

The hangover begins when capital becomes expensive. A recovering company is not
simply spending less. It is regaining the ability to choose: which strategy to
keep, which project to kill, who owns the result, and what work customers will
actually pay for.

That distinction matters. Layoffs and cancelled lunches are visible; better
judgment is not. Use the five signals below as clues, then inspect whether they
add up to a coherent operating model.

## Five Signals of a Real Recovery

### 1. Founders return to the decisions

During easy-money periods, layers of executives can insulate founders from the
work. In the episode, Caldwell and Seibel describe companies cycling through
senior leaders who had been hired to execute a strategy that no longer made
sense. Replacing such a leader may be fair to the person and still necessary for
the company.

The healthy signal is not executive churn by itself. It is accountable
leadership: founders or senior operators can explain the strategy, know the
numbers, and make the unpleasant tradeoffs instead of outsourcing judgment.

### 2. Work policy follows the work

The hosts use return-to-office mandates as a deliberately contentious example.
Their larger point is more useful than a universal office rule: policies should
serve the company's actual coordination needs, not preserve a pandemic-era
assumption or imitate another CEO.

A distributed team with fast decisions and strong output may be healthy. An
office mandate that merely signals toughness may not be. Ask what problem the
policy solves and what evidence will show that it worked.

### 3. Vanity projects lose their oxygen

One of the episode's sharpest stories is the internal project built mainly to
earn its sponsor a promotion. The team ships something that customers do not
need; the artifact is discarded, but the executive gets a larger title. Company
resources have been converted into private career capital.

Recovery makes this harder. Every substantial project should have a named user,
an expected outcome, an owner, and a stopping rule. "Important strategic work"
is not a customer.

### 4. Perks stop impersonating a business model

Lavish benefits can be lovely, but they are not proof of product-market fit.
When a company removes benefits while protecting the work that creates customer
value, it may finally be acknowledging constraints. When it cuts random comforts
but keeps executive vanity projects, it has learned theatre, not discipline.

### 5. Expectations become honest

The episode opens with a caricature: an employee recalls working three days a
week, travelling, and enjoying hobbies, then treats one weekend request as an HR
violation. The joke is not that everyone should work every weekend. It exposes a
mismatch between the intensity of a struggling company and expectations formed
during an exceptional funding market.

Healthy recovery replaces both entitlement and permanent panic with an explicit
deal: what the mission requires, how urgent periods are handled, and what a
sustainable normal week looks like.

## A Practical Diagnostic

Imagine two companies that each cut spending by 30%.

- **Company A** cancels meals, travel, and learning budgets. Its three prestige
  projects remain because powerful executives sponsor them. Nobody can name the
  core customer metric.
- **Company B** closes a product line that never retained users, gives one
  operator clear ownership of the core product, and keeps a modest benefit that
  helps the small team work effectively.

Only Company B shows evidence of recovery. The difference is not austerity; it
is resource allocation connected to customer value.

## Try It Yourself: The Recovery Memo

Choose your company or one you know well. In ten minutes, write five lines:

1. The customer outcome that must improve.
2. One project that directly improves it.
3. One activity that survives mainly because of status or history.
4. One hard decision leadership is postponing.
5. The evidence you would expect within 90 days after making that decision.

If you cannot name the customer outcome, start there. A cost-cutting plan without
a theory of value is just a smaller pile of confusion.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Important caveats (optional)</summary>

These are heuristics from Caldwell and Seibel's discussion, not universal laws.
Executive turnover can signal chaos. Office mandates can reduce effectiveness.
Benefit cuts can be shortsighted. Long-horizon research can look like a vanity
project before it pays off. Evaluate the mechanism and the evidence rather than
scoring each visible action as automatically good or bad.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Recovery is the return of hard choices, not cost cutting for its own sake.
- Leadership, work policies, projects, perks, and expectations are clues; customer-linked decisions are the test.
- Ask what value a change protects, what tradeoff it makes, and what evidence should appear next.

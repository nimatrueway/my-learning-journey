# The Problem With Startup "Experts"

Founders rarely seek advice at a calm, obvious moment. They seek it when the
product is uncertain, the team is tired, and someone nearby sounds sure.

Confidence is easy to observe. Relevant experience and aligned incentives take
more work.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

## Bad Advice Can Be Worse Than Ignorance

Caldwell and Seibel describe meeting founders who had absorbed an elaborate but
incorrect model of startup building. They believed the first steps were forming
a board of advisers, developing a patent strategy, and arranging impressive
partnerships. Before the founders could learn what customers wanted, they had to
unlearn a system of ceremonial work.

A beginner who admits "I do not know" can observe reality. A founder protecting
a borrowed doctrine may reinterpret every failure to preserve it. This is why
the quality of startup advice matters more than the quantity consumed.

The first filter is provenance. Ask:

- What has this person built, sold, funded, or operated?
- Was that experience at a stage resembling yours?
- How recently did they face the problem behind this recommendation?
- Which result would make them revise the advice?

Experience does not make someone infallible. It reveals what the advice is based
on and where it may transfer poorly.

## Separate Support From Operating Direction

Part of an adviser conversation is often emotional. A founder needs to say that
the week was terrible and hear from someone who cares about them. Friends,
family, and peers can be excellent at that role without knowing how to price an
enterprise product or run a seed process.

Trouble starts when emotional trust silently becomes domain authority. Label the
conversation. Are you asking for encouragement, a contact, a factual answer, or
a recommendation that will redirect months of work? The more expensive the
decision, the closer the source should be to the relevant work.

Online material can sometimes beat convenient local advice because it gives a
founder access to people who have actually done the thing. It still requires a
filter. A famous operator describing a mature sales organization may be less
useful to a pre-revenue team than an unknown founder who closed a similar first
customer last month.

## The Innovation Lab With the Wrong Customer

The episode's most concrete warning concerns startup programs attached to large
companies. Imagine a car manufacturer creating an innovation lab that promises
to mentor startups and become their first customer. The offer sounds ideal:
brand credibility, domain experts, and a path to revenue.

But incentives can point elsewhere. The parent company is unlikely to help a
startup that seriously threatens its core business. The innovation executive
may instead be rewarded for reporting how many startups entered the program,
how many events occurred, or whether quarterly innovation targets were met. In
that system, the startup is not necessarily a future supplier. It may be an item
in someone else's performance report.

This does not mean every corporate program is empty. It means the founder should
test the promise as a sales claim. Who owns a purchasing budget? What was the
last pilot converted into a paid deployment? How long did procurement take? A
mentor's enthusiasm is not the same as a customer's commitment.

## Geography Is a Proxy, Not Proof

The hosts argue that dense startup ecosystems improve the odds of meeting people
who have built, invested in, or advised successful companies. They contrast that
density with local institutions whose mission may be job creation or keeping
founders in the region. Such an institution has understandable reasons not to
recommend moving, even when moving would help a particular company.

Proximity to the Bay Area is not a certificate of wisdom. The transferable point
is density of relevant repetitions. Seek people who have seen both surprising
successes and failures. Someone whose rejected companies never became visible
can maintain perfect confidence in their selection skill.

## Original Example: The Partnership Detour

A first-time founder building inventory software asks a retired retail executive
for go-to-market advice. The executive recommends recruiting a prestigious
advisory board and negotiating a national-chain partnership. Those tactics fit
the executive's world, where a known company already has lawyers, procurement
staff, and market credibility.

The founder instead calls six independent shop owners. Four will pay for a
simple stock-alert feature, and one agrees to start next week. The executive's
advice was not absurd. It was imported from a stage the startup had not reached.

## Try It Yourself: Add an Advice Nutrition Label

Choose one recommendation that could consume at least a week. Write:

1. **Claim:** the action you are being told to take.
2. **Source:** the adviser's direct, relevant experience.
3. **Stage match:** what was similar and different when it worked for them.
4. **Incentive:** what they or their institution gain if you follow it.
5. **Role:** support, introduction, factual information, or operating direction.
6. **Reality check:** one conversation or test you can run within 48 hours.

Do the reality check before adopting the recommendation. The goal is not to win
an argument with the adviser. It is to keep borrowed certainty from outranking
customer evidence.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Useful outsiders and imperfect experts (optional)</summary>

An outsider can notice assumptions that insiders no longer see, and experienced
operators can be wrong. Do not turn provenance into credential worship. Use it
to calibrate confidence. Also remember that local ecosystems can provide loyal
teammates, early customers, and lower operating costs. The caution applies when
an institution's mission is mistaken for neutral advice about your company.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Judge advice by relevant repetitions, stage match, and incentives rather than confidence or local status.
- Keep emotional support separate from operating direction so trust does not become accidental expertise.
- Test costly recommendations against customers and concrete commitments before building your plan around them.

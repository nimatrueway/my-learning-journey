# How To NOT Get Screwed As A Software Engineer

The person turning an idea into a functioning product may carry founder-level
risk while holding employee-level ownership. Titles do not settle that mismatch.
Contribution, commitment, control, and written terms do.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 18-minute source video is optional

## Read the Relationship Through the Cap Table

The episode describes a recurring YC application: a nontechnical founder owns
90 percent, while the technical founder who builds the core product owns 10
percent or less. The explanation may be that the first person had the idea or
negotiated more aggressively.

That history rarely justifies the future. Most of the company's work remains
ahead, and a startup needs co-founders who behave like owners through years of
failures and emergencies. Equity is not only a reward for past contribution. It
shapes motivation, control, and whether the partnership can survive.

Equal equity is not mandatory, but a large difference needs a concrete reason:
different time commitment, meaningful existing assets, cash invested, or a
substantially different role. "My idea" is weak because ideas change and
execution compounds.

## Founder Work With an Employee Label

The hosts also describe companies with two or three business founders and one
lead engineer holding around one percent. If that engineer owns architecture,
ships the entire product, responds to failures, and makes technical strategy,
the title may disguise a technical co-founder role.

Their Sunday-night outage story explains why ownership matters. When a site goes
down, the nontechnical founder cannot repair it. In one world, they must cajole
an under-incentivized builder. In another, a genuine partner sees the same
company-level emergency and acts from shared ownership. Equity cannot purchase
character, but a glaringly unequal arrangement can tell the builder that the
company does not regard them as a partner.

The college student whom MBA candidates ask to build prototypes and the highly
productive Googler whose manager takes credit face the same structural question:
who captures the value of their work, and who controls the decisions it enables?

## Ask Before the Product Creates Leverage

Clarify founder status, percentage ownership, vesting, cliff, dilution,
decision rights, intellectual property, salary expectations, and departure
terms before months of code exist. Understand whether the percentage is fully
diluted and what securities or option pools already exist. Use qualified legal
and tax advice for actual agreements.

Also inspect behavior. Can you challenge the customer strategy when analytics
show a launch failed, or are you told business is someone else's department?
A partner needs enough information and authority to protect the product they are
responsible for building.

## Original Example: The "Founding Engineer" Offer

A designer and salesperson recruit an engineer to build an insurance platform.
They call her founding engineer, offer 0.75 percent, no salary for six months,
and full responsibility for architecture and hiring. Each existing founder owns
45 percent because they developed the concept for a year.

She writes the next 24 months of responsibilities beside the cap table. Her role
is plainly founder-level. She proposes co-founder status, meaningful ownership,
standard vesting for everyone, and shared product decisions. If they refuse, the
disagreement is useful evidence before she contributes unpaid code.

## Try It Yourself: Run the Founder-Work Audit

Write four columns: person, weekly responsibility, downside risk, and ownership.
Then:

1. List product, sales, fundraising, hiring, operations, and emergency ownership.
2. Record salary forgone, cash invested, and time commitment.
3. Compare voting and information rights with responsibility.
4. Explain every material equity difference without using "idea" or "title."
5. Put agreed vesting, IP, departure, and salary terms into proper documents.

Do not use the worksheet as a legal agreement. Use it to expose assumptions that
lawyers can then document correctly.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Fair does not always mean numerically equal (optional)</summary>

A founder joining years later, working part-time, or receiving market salary may
rationally own less. The warning is against unexplained asymmetry and ambiguous
status, not a universal split. Evaluate total terms and seek independent advice
where interests differ.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Compare ownership with future responsibility and risk; an early idea rarely justifies permanent 90/10 control.
- Founder-level technical work needs matching information and decision rights, not an impressive employee title.
- Resolve equity, vesting, IP, salary, dilution, and departure terms before code creates one-sided leverage.

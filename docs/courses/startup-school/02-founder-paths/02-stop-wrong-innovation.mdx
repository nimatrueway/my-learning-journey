# Stop Innovating (On The Wrong Things)

Novelty is expensive. A startup already needs one improbable breakthrough:
making something people want. Adding experimental governance, compensation,
location rules, and management systems turns one hard bet into five.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 12-minute source video is optional

## Concentrate the Uncertainty

Caldwell and Seibel describe innovation as a limited budget. The product may
need a new technical method, behavior, or business model. That is where an
unusual choice can create customer value. Incorporation, vesting, payroll, and
basic team operations usually do not distinguish what customers buy.

Every custom practice adds a hypothesis the company must prove. A novel product
inside a familiar legal and operating shell asks the team to solve its customer
problem. A novel product inside novel governance asks the team to solve the
customer, law, incentives, recruiting, and financing simultaneously.

Defaults are valuable because other companies have already paid to discover
their failure modes. Using one is not intellectual surrender. It is choosing
where your scarce attention will have the highest return.

## The Wyoming LLC Warning

The episode points to YC applications that proudly announce an unusual entity,
such as a Wyoming LLC, instead of the conventional structure investors and
startup lawyers expect. The choice acts as a voluntary warning because the
founder often lacks corporate-law expertise yet has decided the standard form
must be wrong.

The risk is not aesthetic. An unconventional entity can complicate investment,
equity grants, taxes, and conversion precisely when the company needs speed.
The customer receives no benefit from that complexity.

OpenAI supplies the larger cautionary story. Its nonprofit control of a
for-profit operation attracted attention during a governance crisis. Observers
could mistake the structure for an instruction manual because it belonged to a
famous company. The hosts make the opposite point: even people involved had
publicly questioned whether the arrangement was wise. A structure surviving at
an exceptional company does not make it a sensible default for a new one.

## Distinctive Is Not the Same as Useful

Founders sometimes attach identity to side constraints: the startup must use an
unusual decision system, reject common advice, or operate only from a preferred
place. These constraints may feel principled, but the customer did not request
them. They can become a private hobby financed by the company's runway.

Use a simple test for internal novelty: what customer outcome improves, by what
mechanism, and how will you know? If a four-day week produces better retention
and sustained output, it may be worth testing. If a custom title hierarchy only
makes the founders feel unconventional, adopt ordinary titles and return to the
product.

## Original Example: The Rotating Salary Council

A six-person security startup creates a monthly council where employees score
one another and allocate raises from a shared pool. The founders expect radical
transparency. Instead, engineers prepare cases for the council, avoid risky
projects before review week, and debate scoring fairness. Two enterprise pilots
still need audit exports.

The team switches to standard salary bands and twice-yearly reviews. Nothing
about compensation becomes visionary, but the engineer who spent six hours
preparing peer scores ships the export. The default did not solve the startup.
It stopped an internal experiment from competing with the customer problem.

## Try It Yourself: Spend the Novelty Budget

List every nonstandard practice your team currently uses or plans to use. For
each one, write:

1. the customer outcome it is supposed to improve;
2. the evidence that the standard approach blocks that outcome;
3. the legal, recruiting, coordination, or financing dependencies it adds;
4. the boring default available now;
5. whether to keep, time-box, or replace the experiment.

Replace one unsupported novelty this week. Redirect the recovered hours to a
named customer problem and record what ships.

<details>
<summary>🧭 When the default is genuinely wrong (optional)</summary>

Defaults encode common conditions, not every condition. A cooperative, public-
benefit organization, regulated entity, or globally distributed team may need a
different structure. Break the default with relevant counsel, a named
constraint, and an explicit cost. "Famous company X did it" is not a mechanism.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A startup can afford only a few uncertain bets, so concentrate them where customers receive value.
- Standard legal and operating forms carry learned reliability; unusual forms add dependencies customers do not reward.
- Break a default only for a named constraint, with expert input and evidence that the standard blocks the mission.

# Should Your Startup Bootstrap or Raise Venture Capital?

Bootstrapping and venture capital are not rival religions. They are financing
systems with different return requirements. Choose the system your company can
actually satisfy, not the identity that looks best online.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 14-minute source video is optional

## Venture Money Has a Job

Most businesses should not raise venture capital, and venture firms do not want
to fund most businesses. A healthy restaurant, agency, niche SaaS product, or
regional service can create substantial wealth without becoming enormous.

A venture fund needs rare winners to repay many losses. The episode describes
the required shape as an investment capable of returning roughly one hundred
times, perhaps far more. That makes venture money more like jet fuel than a
general badge of quality. Put it into a company with no plausible path to huge
scale and the investor pressures the founder to chase growth the underlying
business cannot support.

The first question is therefore not "Can I raise?" It is "Does acceleration
create a much larger outcome?" Capital is useful when speed, network effects,
research cost, regulation, inventory, or infrastructure create a genuine race.

## Shark Tank Distorts the Map

The hosts use *Shark Tank* to explain a common misconception. Many products on
the show are attractive small businesses, but their television funding does not
resemble venture capital. Watching every entrepreneur ask investors for money
can make outside funding seem like the normal next step for any product.

It is not. Venture-backed companies are an unusual subset of new businesses.
Even software does not automatically qualify. A profitable app for a narrow
professional niche may be better when customer revenue funds measured growth.
The founder keeps control and avoids a return promise the market cannot support.

The opposite mistake is claiming a company can reach planetary scale without
ever needing capital. The episode notes that trillion-dollar software companies
did take venture funding. Enormous outcomes often require hiring, distribution,
infrastructure, or acquisitions before revenue can finance them.

## The Choice Can Change

Financing is not a permanent identity. A team can bootstrap until it has strong
retention, then raise to expand quickly. Early revenue can improve both the
founder's knowledge and negotiating position. Likewise, raising once does not
remove the obligation to build sound economics.

Model the next proof point, not an abstract lifetime policy. Write what must be
true in twelve months, how much cash it takes, and what happens if growth is
slower. Venture funding buys speed but adds dilution, governance, and pressure
for an outsized exit. Bootstrapping preserves optionality but can lose a market
where timing matters.

## Original Example: Two Inspection Products

A founder sells inspection-report software to independent elevator technicians.
Ten customers cover hosting and support; each new customer arrives through a
trade association. Bootstrapping lets her refine a durable niche.

Another team builds a sensor network that predicts elevator failures. It needs
hardware certification, thousands of installations, and enough fleet data to
train useful models. Revenue from ten pilots cannot fund the rollout. If the
retention and technical signal are strong, outside capital may convert a costly
data flywheel into an advantage. The same market supports two financing answers
because the companies have different capital mechanics.

## Try It Yourself: Build Two Runways

Choose a milestone that materially reduces risk, such as 20 retained customers
or a certified prototype. Then calculate two paths:

1. List the people, infrastructure, inventory, and months required.
2. Estimate customer cash available before the milestone.
3. Write what venture money accelerates that revenue cannot.
4. State the return-scale assumption a venture investor would need.
5. Name the signal that would make you switch paths.

If capital only pays for vague growth, the plan is not ready. If bootstrapping
requires missing a time-sensitive market, ownership alone is not a strategy.

## Three Things to Keep

- Venture funding fits companies where capital can plausibly create the outsized return a fund requires.
- Bootstrapping fits many valuable businesses and can precede a later raise when evidence justifies acceleration.
- Compare concrete paths to the next proof point, including speed, dilution, control, and downside.

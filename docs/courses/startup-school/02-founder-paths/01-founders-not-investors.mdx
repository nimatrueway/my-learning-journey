# Why Founders Shouldn't Think Like Investors

An investor can reject a company, place ten other bets, and still have an
excellent year. A founder cannot diversify Monday across ten startups. The job
is to choose one problem, learn faster than anyone else, and make it work.

Confusing those jobs produces founders who can explain a market but cannot name
one person who urgently needs their product.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 24-minute source video is optional

## Two Jobs, Two Kinds of Evidence

Public-market investors, bankers, consultants, and venture capitalists are
trained to compare opportunities. Market size, comparable companies,
fundraising trends, acquisition multiples, and expert surveys help them decide
where capital belongs. Those tools are legitimate for that job.

An early founder has a different bottleneck. With no product or customers, a
ten-year market forecast contains more assumptions than facts. The company may
pivot, the technology may change, and customers may reveal a narrower but more
valuable problem. The founder needs evidence close enough to change this week's
work: a user attempting the workflow, a painful workaround, a prototype used
twice, or money changing hands.

The distinction is not "analysis bad, instinct good." It is about sequence.
First prove that you understand a real problem and can create value. Later,
market structure and financing strategy become increasingly important.

## The Fifteen-Slide Company With No Customers

Caldwell and Seibel describe a pattern from YC interviews. A founder arrives
with a slide deck that presents the startup as though it were already a large
company. There is a market-opportunity slide, a polished competitive grid, and
perhaps fifteen slides of analysis. Then the interview reaches the basic
question: does anyone use it? The answer is no.

The deck is not merely premature. It can conceal the founder's missing point of
view. After twenty minutes, the listener has heard what investors say about the
category but not what the founder believes, has observed, or cares enough to
discover. The founders have optimized for sounding investable before becoming
useful.

YC's early-stage test, as described in the episode, is more elementary: does
the idea make sense, do the founders know anything specific about it, can they
build something, and can they get any customers? That standard feels almost too
simple. Its strength is that each question can be answered by action rather
than vocabulary.

## Why the Investor Voice Gets So Loud

Investor thinking is unusually visible now. Funds publish market maps, partners
post theses, and startup courses often teach pitching and market selection.
This content is also marketing: investors want strong founders to know their
firm. A founder can absorb the language so completely that imagined investor
preferences replace personal observation.

That creates a peculiar loop. The founder chooses a fashionable category to
appeal to investors, then uses investor commentary as proof that the category
is promising. No customer entered the loop.

A better first pass starts with unfairly local knowledge. What work have you
done? Which users can you reach today? What failure have you seen repeatedly?
Which claim can you test without permission? These questions do not guarantee a
large company. They produce information that belongs to this company rather
than to a market report.

## Original Example: Compliance From the Floor

Imagine two founders considering software for food-factory compliance. One
downloads an industry report, calculates a multibillion-dollar market, and
builds a dashboard for "AI-powered operational assurance." The other spent
three summers in a family factory and watched supervisors photograph paper
temperature logs before audits.

The second founder interviews five supervisors, learns that missing signatures
cause more rework than missing readings, and tests a phone form that refuses an
incomplete shift handoff. Three supervisors use it for ten shifts. That evidence
is tiny beside the market report, but it identifies a user, moment, behavior,
and mechanism. It is a foundation the first founder does not yet have.

## Try It Yourself: Replace One Market Slide

Take the slide or paragraph where you justify your market. Keep it for later,
but replace it today with a one-page field record:

1. Name one reachable person and the exact moment the problem occurs.
2. Record what they do now, including time, money, risk, and who else is involved.
3. Write your non-obvious belief about why the workaround persists.
4. Choose an artifact you can deliver within 48 hours.
5. Define behavioral evidence, such as a second use, referral, or payment.

Do the test before revising the market narrative. If you cannot reach one user,
your immediate problem is access, not market sizing.

<details>
<summary>🧭 When investor-style analysis becomes useful (optional)</summary>

Market structure matters when deciding whether venture financing fits, how an
incumbent may respond, or whether the economics can support the company. It can
also expose regulation or concentration risk before an expensive build. The
caveat is timing and authority: use top-down analysis to challenge customer
evidence, not to manufacture conviction that no customer behavior supports.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Investors compare many opportunities; founders must create concentrated evidence for one problem.
- A polished market story cannot substitute for a specific belief, a reachable user, and observed behavior.
- Sequence the work: prove nearby value first, then use market analysis to test scale, competition, and financing.

# Why You Should Leave Your FAANG Job

A big-tech job can teach excellent engineering and finance a future risk. It can
also make the next vest date feel like the only rational time to decide, forever.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 20-minute source video is optional

## The Brand Changed Because the Company Changed

Caldwell and Seibel recall a time when Google was an roughly 80-person startup,
not the established career destination it later became. The name stayed Google
while the work, risk, and organizational context changed. “Work at Google” is
therefore not one career experience across eras, teams, or roles.

Evaluate the actual job: what decisions do you own, how close are you to users,
which systems do you understand end to end, and what happens when you ship? A
large company can offer rare scale problems and strong colleagues. It can also
specialize a person so narrowly that startup product, sales, recruiting, and
operations remain unfamiliar.

## Transfer the Skill, Not the Infrastructure

The episode warns that big-company standards can distort an early startup. Work
that reaches even beta at Google may be ten times what a startup MVP requires.
That rigor is rational when millions of users, mature infrastructure, and
reputational risk are present. A startup often needs a small, reversible release
to learn whether ten users care.

Ask which part transfers. Debugging discipline, security judgment, and clear
interfaces may transfer. Internal deployment systems, approval layers, and
assumptions about specialist support may not. Founders must operate without the
infrastructure that made their previous role productive.

Credentials also do less work than expected. The hosts mock the idea that an
ex-Googler presents a badge at a special investor door. Relevant experience can
earn attention, but customers and investors still evaluate the company.

## Retention Incentives Move the Decision

Big companies design compensation to retain employees. Four-year equity grants,
refreshers, promotions, and bonuses continually create another valuable date.
That is not sinister; it is the product working as designed. A future founder
should still decide deliberately rather than treating every unvested amount as
money already owned.

Compare staying and leaving over a defined horizon. Include cash, equity
probability, benefits, learning, scope, people, family needs, runway, and
reversibility. Do not convert “more ownership” into guaranteed startup value.

## Original Example: The Infrastructure Staff Engineer

A staff engineer owns reliability for one layer of a global payments system. She
wants to found a billing startup and assumes scale expertise is sufficient. A
six-week side prototype reveals the missing skills: interviewing finance teams,
choosing a narrow workflow, pricing, and shipping without internal platforms.

She does not quit immediately or abandon the idea. She saves twelve months of
personal runway, joins customer calls through a permitted volunteer project, and
sets a review date after the next vest. The decision is based on a tested gap,
not contempt for her job or worship of startup risk.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Transfer Map

List five abilities your next role requires. Mark each current skill as directly
transferable, context-dependent, or missing. For one missing skill, run a lawful,
low-risk test such as a customer interview, weekend prototype, or conversation
with three recent leavers. Then compare staying and leaving at the same twelve-
month date, including personal runway and obligations.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Leaving is not the advanced answer (optional)</summary>

Health insurance, immigration, caregiving, debt, and labor-market conditions can
dominate the decision. A high-ownership internal role may teach more than a weak
startup. Review employment agreements and intellectual-property boundaries with
qualified counsel before side work or departure.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Judge the actual team, scope, and stage; the same company name can describe radically different work.
- Transfer engineering judgment while shedding infrastructure and production standards that block a small MVP.
- Treat vesting as a retention incentive and compare options on one horizon after testing the alternative.

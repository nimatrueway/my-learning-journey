# What Basic Game Theory Teaches Us About Startups

Some games redistribute a fixed prize. Others make the prize larger through
cooperation. A startup’s business model chooses a game even when nobody writes
the rules down.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 19-minute source video is optional

## Start With the Transfer

Caldwell and Seibel use a bet as the clean zero-sum case. If two people wager
$10, one person’s gain is the other person’s loss. Poker behaves similarly
before fees: the money a winner receives came from other players. The activity
may be consensual entertainment, but it does not create additional financial
value for the players as a group.

A positive-sum transaction leaves the participants collectively better off. A
business pays for software because saved labor or increased revenue exceeds the
price; the software company earns money for producing that improvement. The
surplus need not be equal, and competitors can still lose. The key is that the
customer does not have to be mistaken for the company to win.

Map the core transaction with four questions:

1. What does each party give?
2. What does each party receive immediately?
3. What happens after repeated use?
4. Does company revenue rise when the customer’s outcome improves?

## Information Asymmetry Can Hide the Game

The episode is especially skeptical of businesses whose growth depends on FOMO,
windfalls, or fooling the next participant. A sophisticated interface does not
change the payoff structure. If early users can profit only because later users
overpay or misunderstand risk, the product has transferred value while calling
the transfer innovation.

Zero-sum incentives spread internally too. A sales commission based only on
contract value can reward deals that churn immediately. A marketplace can boost
short-term fees by making refunds painful, while teaching buyers not to return.
Metrics then celebrate extraction before delayed losses arrive.

Add delayed effects to the map: refunds, churn, support cost, reputation,
regulation, employee behavior, and whether the same customer willingly repeats
the transaction. Repetition is a strong test because deception consumes the
conditions required for another deal.

## Positive-Sum Is a Design Constraint

Good intentions do not align incentives. Pricing and measurement do. A payments
provider charging per successful transaction generally grows with merchant
activity. A recruiting service paid only for placements has some alignment, but
may still push poor matches unless retention affects payment.

Look for a metric shared across the boundary. Customer revenue, time saved,
successful completion, durable placement, or avoided loss can connect the
company’s reward to value delivered. Then protect informed consent: users should
understand price, risk, and alternatives well enough to choose.

## Original Example: The Collections Assistant

A startup helps clinics collect unpaid invoices. Version one charges 25 percent
of every amount collected, including money patients would have paid anyway. It
also recommends aggressive reminders because more contact correlates with more
fees. Clinics gain cash, but patient trust and staff time are absent from the
optimization.

Version two charges a platform fee plus a smaller success fee only on invoices
recovered after the normal process, caps contact frequency, and tracks patient
complaints and clinic retention. The game is not perfectly aligned, but the
largest rewards now depend more closely on incremental clinic value.

## Try It Yourself: Draw the Second Transaction

Choose your main revenue event. Draw the customer, company, partner, and any
affected third party. Record what each gains or loses today. Then draw the same
transaction after twelve months of repetition. Circle any party whose loss grows
while company revenue grows. Change one price, commission, default, or metric so
improving that party’s outcome also improves the company’s.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Not every loss proves exploitation (optional)</summary>

Competitors lose bids, insurance pools redistribute risk, and entertainment can
be worth paying for. Positive-sum analysis is not a claim that every participant
must profit. Ask whether users understand the exchange, receive the value they
chose, and can sustain the transaction without hidden harm.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A zero-sum game transfers a fixed prize; a positive-sum business creates surplus beyond the price paid.
- Add delayed and third-party effects, because extraction often appears as churn, distrust, or harm after revenue is booked.
- Pricing and metrics should make company success rise with an informed customer’s durable success.

# Save Your Startup During an Economic Downturn

When financing tightens, optimism is not a cash-flow model. The useful question
is not whether the company can raise again. It is what happens if it cannot.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 36-minute source video is optional

## Default Alive Is Not the Same as Profitable

The episode uses Paul Graham’s distinction. A profitable company earns more than
it spends today. A default-alive company may still burn cash, but its current
cash, growth, and spending trajectory reach profitability before the bank account
reaches zero. If the trajectory does not, it is default dead unless something
material changes.

Start with four numbers: unrestricted cash, monthly revenue, monthly expenses,
and recent growth. Net burn is expenses minus revenue. Simple runway is cash
divided by net burn, but the default-alive calculation also models how revenue
and costs change. Use conservative scenarios; a spreadsheet that assumes growth
because survival requires it is a wish.

For example, $600,000 at $100,000 monthly net burn gives roughly six months of
static runway. Reducing burn to $60,000 gives roughly ten months. That extra time
only matters if the company uses it to improve revenue or reach break-even.

## Avoid the Fatal Pinch

The episode references Graham’s “fatal pinch”: low runway, weak growth, and a
fundraising plan that depends on investors overlooking both. By the time the
problem feels undeniable, layoffs, sales experiments, and financing all become
harder. Founders lose choices because they delayed the socially painful decision.

Set trigger dates while options remain. Examples include: pause hiring below
twelve months of runway, begin a financing process only with a stated milestone,
or execute a reduction plan if two consecutive growth reviews miss the base
case. Triggers prevent every review from becoming a fresh negotiation with hope.

## Cut to a Company That Can Still Win

Reducing every expense equally can preserve cash while destroying the growth
engine. Identify the smallest team and capabilities required to serve current
customers, maintain the product, and test the highest-value growth channel.
Remove speculative layers before core learning capacity.

The episode discusses companies that allowed burn to climb dramatically and then
treated small reductions as decisive. Percentages can hide the absolute cash
outflow. Review dollars, months gained, implementation cost, and impact on the
path to profitability.

## Original Example: The Two Runways

A workflow startup has $1.2 million, $80,000 in monthly revenue, and $180,000 in
expenses. Static net burn is $100,000, or twelve months. Its plan assumes a new
sales team doubles revenue in six months, but the existing founder-led process
has not yet closed repeatable deals.

The founders model a base case with flat revenue and a tested case with modest
growth. They pause three planned hires, reducing expenses to $140,000 and runway
to twenty months at current revenue. They protect the two engineers supporting
retained customers and give founder-led sales eight weeks to prove a channel.
The cut buys a decision window, not a victory announcement.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Three-Case Runway Table

For flat, base, and downside cases, enter cash, monthly revenue, monthly expense,
growth, net burn, and break-even month. State the evidence for each growth rate.
Then model one expense change and one revenue action. Set the calendar date when
you will act if the base case is missed. Have a finance professional review the
model before consequential decisions.

<details>
<summary>🧮 Runway models are estimates, not treasury advice (optional)</summary>

Collections timing, taxes, debt, severance, annual contracts, restricted cash,
and one-time costs can materially change runway. Growth can also require spending.
Use qualified financial, legal, and employment advice for actual reductions,
financing, insolvency, and cash-management decisions.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Default alive means the modeled trajectory reaches profitability before cash reaches zero, not that the company is profitable today.
- Set cash and growth triggers early, because the fatal pinch removes options precisely when action becomes unavoidable.
- Reduce burn enough to change runway while protecting the capabilities needed to retain customers and test growth.

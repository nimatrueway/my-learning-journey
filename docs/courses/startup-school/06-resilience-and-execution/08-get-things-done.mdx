# How Future Billionaires Get Sh*t Done

A calendar can look full while preventing the work that matters. The problem is
not meetings themselves. It is mixing two incompatible units of time without
deciding which one the current bottleneck needs.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 20-minute source video is optional

## Maker Work Needs Contiguous Time

Building software, designing a product, writing a sales narrative, or analyzing
a difficult dataset requires loading context and holding it. Caldwell and Seibel
describe maker time in large blocks, sometimes an entire eight-hour day. Three
short meetings distributed across that day can prevent a maker from beginning a
risky migration even though the meetings consume only an hour on paper.

Protecting maker time means more than declining invitations. Define the output,
remove routine notifications, gather dependencies first, and leave enough space
to finish or reach a safe stopping point. A blank calendar without a deliverable
can become luxurious procrastination.

Context switching also creates unfinished inventory. A half-written migration,
an interrupted pricing analysis, and an unanswered customer proposal each must
be loaded into memory again before progress resumes. Count those restarts when
evaluating a meeting. Its cost is not only thirty scheduled minutes; it may also
include the abandoned block on either side. This is why batching short decisions
can recover more production time than the calendar arithmetic suggests.

## Manager Work Is Real Production Too

Managers create leverage through decisions, context, hiring, feedback, and
unblocking other people. Their day may rationally contain many short meetings.
A founder who insists on maker mode while six teammates wait for decisions is
not focused; they are the bottleneck.

The episode describes YC as deliberately leaving substantial maker time rather
than filling the batch with classes. Yet office hours and visible KPIs provide
manager-style checkpoints. The combination matters: founders do the work, then
use concise coordination to inspect whether it reaches users and grows.

Batch manager work into predictable windows. Require each recurring meeting to
produce a decision, owner, or changed plan. Put core metrics somewhere visible
so meetings do not repeatedly reconstruct basic company state.

## Focus Has Decoys

Social media, mentoring, advising, networking, and side projects can all feel
productive. The episode treats them as possible hedges: activities that protect
a founder’s identity if the main company fails. They may be worthwhile, but they
should compete honestly for scarce time.

Name the company’s current constraint and ask whether an activity changes it.
The answer can be indirect, but it should be explicit. A recruiting dinner may
matter when hiring is the constraint; the same dinner may be avoidance when five
customers are blocked by a broken workflow.

## Original Example: The Founder Context Switch

A technical founder schedules customer calls whenever prospects are available,
then tries to code between them. Neither activity works: prospects receive
rushed discovery, and engineering tasks remain half-started.

She moves calls to Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, reserves Monday and
Wednesday mornings for a defined onboarding release, and keeps Friday for team
decisions and metrics. Urgent customer failures still interrupt. Ordinary
coordination no longer does. After two weeks, she reviews shipped work and sales
cycle time rather than celebrating calendar neatness.

## Try It Yourself: Price Tomorrow’s Fragments

Label every calendar item maker, manager, recovery, or unrelated. For each maker
block, write the deliverable and subtract thirty minutes of context loading after
an interruption. Move or batch one meeting to recover at least two contiguous
hours. For manager items, record the person or decision unblocked. At day’s end,
compare planned outputs with actual outputs and adjust once.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Not every role controls its calendar (optional)</summary>

Support, operations, caregiving, and incident response can be interruption-heavy.
Use rotations, handoffs, service windows, or smaller focus blocks rather than
pretending interruptions do not exist. The principle is deliberate allocation,
not one ideal schedule for every person.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Maker work needs contiguous context and a deliverable; nominally free minutes between meetings are not equivalent.
- Manager work creates leverage when it produces decisions, context, ownership, or an unblocked teammate.
- Schedule against the current company constraint and judge focus by output, not calendar purity or visible activity.

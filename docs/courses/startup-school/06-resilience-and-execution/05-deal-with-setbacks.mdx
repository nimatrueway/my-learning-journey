# How To Deal With Setbacks

A setback is an event. Within minutes, a founder can turn it into a theory of
personal worth, company destiny, and everything that will fail next. Recovery
starts by separating those layers again.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 22-minute source video is optional

## Rejection Is Specific, Even When It Feels Total

Caldwell and Seibel discuss fundraising rejection and YC rejection because both
invite identity-level interpretations. A “no” may mean this investor, this pitch,
this stage, this market, or this application did not clear a threshold. It does
not automatically explain which one.

Record the event before debating its meaning. What exactly was declined? What
reason was given? What changed in cash, timeline, product access, or team? Then
seek comparable evidence. Ten investors repeating the same traction objection
deserve more weight than one vague pass. A rejected YC application can be
followed by building, talking to users, and applying again; the email does not
prevent those actions.

## Beware the Magical Deal

The episode names a founder fantasy: close one partnership or financing and the
credits will roll. The deal is imagined as an ending to uncertainty. In reality,
it can fail before signing, create difficult obligations after signing, or simply
reveal the next operating problem.

This fantasy makes setbacks larger because the founder assigned the deal every
future benefit. Replace it with explicit effects. If the customer signs, revenue
increases by a stated amount, implementation work begins, and concentration risk
changes. The company still needs other customers, product reliability, and cash
management.

Planning this way also reveals options when the deal fails. Which benefit was
lost, and what other action can produce part of it?

Set a review point after the immediate response. The first day is for containing
cash, customer, or safety consequences. The next review asks whether the event
was isolated or part of a repeated pattern. Without that second step, founders
either overreact to one bad outcome or keep calling a series of warnings bad
luck. Resilience needs both emotional recovery and an honest update to the model.

## Launch Is a Repetition, Not a Referendum

The hosts note that founders expect a launch to resemble the rare launch stories
visible online. Most launches receive little attention. That is why launching
early matters: it removes the fantasy and begins a sequence of releases,
feedback, and distribution attempts.

A quiet launch can answer whether onboarding works for ten real users. A broken
launch can expose capacity or support failures. Neither result is pleasant, but
both are more specific than “the market rejected us.” Keep launching after
fixing the diagnosed failure.

## Original Example: The Cancelled Retail Rollout

A retailer cancels a 50-store rollout two weeks before signing. The startup’s
forecast assumed the full contract, and the team initially says the enterprise
strategy has failed.

The facts are narrower: a new finance leader froze all vendor expansion; the
three-store pilot still improved stock accuracy; runway falls by six weeks. The
founders pause hiring, ask the pilot champion for a reference, package the pilot
results, and contact five similar retailers. They also stop counting unsigned
contracts in hiring plans. The setback changes cash decisions and sales evidence
without erasing what the pilot proved.

## Try It Yourself: Run a 24-Hour Reset

Fold a page into four boxes: verified facts, interpretations, losses, and options.
Put every sentence in exactly one box. Circle interpretations supported by more
than one comparable signal. Choose one action that preserves cash or safety and
one action that creates new evidence. Complete the smaller action within 24
hours, then schedule the larger decision after the initial emotional spike.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Some events require containment before learning (optional)</summary>

Security incidents, lawsuits, threats, insolvency, harassment, and serious health
events may require immediate professional help and preservation of evidence.
Do not use reframing to minimize harm or improvise beyond your competence.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A rejection is evidence about a particular attempt until repeated, comparable signals establish a broader pattern.
- No financing, partnership, or launch ends company uncertainty; define the benefit instead of assigning it magic.
- Separate facts, interpretations, losses, and options before choosing the next cash-preserving and evidence-producing actions.

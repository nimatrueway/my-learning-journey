# Secrets You Can Learn From Your Customers

Customers rarely dictate a good roadmap. They reveal it indirectly through workarounds, embarrassment, side conversations, and the parts of the job they assumed you would never care about. Those signals appear when founders get close enough to see the work.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 15-minute source video is optional

## Care Produces Access

The episode's "secret" is almost suspiciously ordinary: care about customers, spend time with them, and become useful enough that they tell you what is really happening. The advantage is difficult for a large organization to copy because every layer between decision-maker and user filters detail.

Close contact reveals more than stated feature requests. Watch where the user hesitates, which spreadsheet remains open beside the product, whom they call for approval, and what they apologize for. A workaround is often a product requirement wearing work clothes.

Trust changes the quality of evidence. A customer who expects a sales pitch gives polite answers. A customer who has seen the founder solve a real problem may reveal a risky process, an unofficial workaround, or an edge case that determines whether the product can become essential.

## Episode Case: Airbnb Enters the Home

The episode explains the familiar Airbnb photography story through customer intimacy rather than growth theater. The founders were emailing early hosts and noticed poor listing photos. The same homes also looked poor on competing sites, so the hosts did not necessarily understand photography as the bottleneck.

The founders offered to visit homes and take better pictures. The trip solved an immediate merchandising problem, but entering the home created a richer interaction. One host, after the lights were arranged and the photos were taken, offered notes from years of hosting. That knowledge would not have emerged from an email asking which feature to build.

The intervention also carried risk. Hosts could reuse the photographs elsewhere. Airbnb gave value without contractual certainty because the relationship and learning were worth more than controlling the asset. The founders learned what good listings required and what experienced hosts knew about the job.

## Ask About the Last Time

Broad questions invite aspiration: "Would you use automated pricing?" Specific questions recover behavior: "Walk me through the last time you changed a weekend price." Follow the sequence, artifacts, people, and exceptions. Ask what happened next and what the failure cost.

Observation is stronger still. A user's memory compresses repeated friction into "it is fine." Watching the workflow exposes the six exports hidden inside that phrase.

## Original Example: The Vet's Sticky Note

A scheduling startup interviews veterinary clinics about appointment reminders. Staff say reminders work well. During observation, the founder sees a sticky note beside the monitor listing animals that become dangerous when anxious. The receptionist manually calls those owners and schedules quieter arrival times.

The hidden job is not merely reducing no-shows. It is preparing safely for particular patients. The startup adds a visible handling flag, consented owner instructions, and a pre-visit task for staff. A dashboard would have reported successful reminders while missing the higher-stakes workflow.

## Try It Yourself: Conduct an Artifact Interview

Recruit one active user and ask them to bring the artifacts from the last completed job: messages, forms, notes, exports, or screenshots. Have them reconstruct the event chronologically. Do not pitch or correct them. Mark every handoff, duplicate entry, private note, and moment they leave your product. End by asking which step they would be embarrassed to show a manager. Write one problem statement from observed behavior, then verify it with two users from different segments.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Depth can produce a beautiful wrong answer (optional)</summary>

One vivid customer can dominate judgment. Sample deliberately across roles, sophistication, and value. Treat a story as a mechanism hypothesis, then look for the same trigger and consequence elsewhere. Respect privacy when observing sensitive work, minimize collected data, and obtain permission before recording or sharing artifacts.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Customer care creates the trust and access needed to uncover workarounds and high-stakes edge cases.
- Reconstructing a recent event and observing artifacts produces better evidence than asking for hypothetical features.
- Deep anecdotes become roadmap evidence only after their mechanism repeats across relevant users.

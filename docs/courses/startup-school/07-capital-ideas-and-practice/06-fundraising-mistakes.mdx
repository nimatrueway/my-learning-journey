# YC Founders Made These Fundraising Mistakes

A fundraising round is a project with an objective, a calendar, and an end. When
it becomes the founder's default activity, investors replace customers as the
audience and meetings replace company progress as the score.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 8-minute source video is optional

## Decide What the Money Proves

Start with the milestone capital will finance. It might be regulatory approval,
a production deployment, 18 months of product work, or expansion after strong
retention. State the amount, runway, use of funds, and evidence expected at the
end. “Grow faster” is not specific enough to evaluate.

Then decide whether the company is ready to be measured. Investor conversations
surface metrics, market assumptions, and team gaps. That can be useful, but a
weak metric introduced too early may become the entire conversation. The episode
notes that once a company has revenue, investors will judge the revenue. A small
number without growth or retention can be less persuasive than founders expect.
The answer is not to hide facts. It is to know what the facts currently show.

## Source Case: 140 Conversations and One Check

One YC founder note in the episode reports speaking with 140 investors and
receiving only one check. The number is striking, but the diagnosis cannot stop
at “fundraising is hard.” A process that large may include poor targeting, weak
signals, meetings spread over too much time, or failure to update the pitch from
repeated objections.

Volume helps only when observations feed the next decision. After each meeting,
classify the result: wrong fund stage, no relevant thesis, unclear problem,
unconvincing traction, team concern, timing, or no decision. If 20 relevant
investors independently reject the same premise, scheduling 120 more identical
meetings is not persistence. It is avoidance of the company problem.

## Investor Attention Is Not Customer Pull

The episode describes founders treating investors almost like the customer.
Investor praise is emotionally powerful and can create introductions, but it is
not product-market fit. The buyer's usage, payment, and retention remain closer
to the business.

This creates a severe opportunity-cost problem. A founder spending the week in
pitch meetings is not improving onboarding or speaking with users. Caldwell and
Seibel contrast fear-based decision-making with customer obsession: when runway
feels short, founders may chase a round because it promises safety, even if
revenue and customer demand are the more credible route forward.

Staying lean changes that negotiation. Lower burn and real revenue do not make
fundraising unnecessary in every company, but they create time and alternatives.
They also make ownership visible as a cost. Capital is purchased with dilution
and sometimes control; it is not free validation.

## Original Example: The Endless Friday Round

A workflow startup takes one investor call every Friday for four months. The CEO
rehearses the pitch each week, but urgency never forms and product planning is
continually interrupted. Ten investors say retention is unclear, yet the team
keeps refining market-size slides.

The CEO pauses the process for six weeks. The team improves activation, measures
cohort retention, and closes three annual contracts. They then schedule twelve
relevant investors across ten business days, share the same data room, and set a
decision date. A shorter process cannot guarantee a round, but it preserves
company momentum and makes feedback comparable.

## Try It Yourself: Design a Round With a Stop Rule

Create a one-page process plan. Write the milestone funded, amount, minimum
runway after closing, use-of-funds categories, and the metric expected before the
next raise. List only investors whose stage, check size, geography, and thesis
fit. Schedule meetings in the smallest realistic window.

Assign another founder or leader to protect customer and product work. Define
two stop rules: one for success, such as sufficient committed capital, and one
for reassessment, such as 15 qualified passes with the same objection. At the
reassessment point, choose explicitly among improving evidence, changing terms,
reducing burn, pursuing revenue, or ending the process.

## Three Things to Keep

- Tie the amount raised to a concrete milestone and know what current metrics honestly demonstrate.
- Batch relevant meetings, classify repeated objections, and protect product and customer work during the round.
- Lower burn, revenue, and a written stop rule preserve alternatives when investor interest does not convert.

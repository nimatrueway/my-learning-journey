# Why Investors Can't Fix Your Company

An investor can recognize a pattern in ten minutes. You still have to discover
whether that pattern describes your company. Money, reputation, and a place on
the cap table do not transfer the founder's responsibility for getting that
answer right.

⏱️ **~8 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 24-minute source video is optional

## Advice Arrives With a Lens

Caldwell and Seibel organize the episode around recurring kinds of advisers.
Someone trained in finance naturally reaches for financing, budgets, and
projections. A former big-company executive may recommend processes or senior
hires that worked inside an established organization. A famous operator may
generalize from the unusually successful company they know best. An influencer
understands distribution, while a junior investor or scout may be learning the
role at the same time as the founder.

None of these people must be foolish or malicious for their advice to be wrong.
Experience determines which variables they notice first. Incentives matter too.
A fund wants returns across a portfolio; an influencer protects an audience; an
executive may be most fluent in the stage where they previously worked. Recover
the model behind a recommendation:

1. What problem does this person think we have?
2. Which past experience makes that diagnosis feel familiar?
3. What company evidence would support or contradict it?
4. Who bears the cost if the recommendation fails?

This converts authority into a testable claim. It also prevents the opposite
mistake, dismissing useful advice merely because the adviser lacks perfect
context.

## Source Case: Five-Year Numbers Before Product-Market Fit

The episode opens with a deliberately awkward question to a pre-product-market-
fit founder: where are the five-year financial projections? Caldwell uses it as
an example of stage mismatch. Projections can become useful later, but a company
that does not yet know who reliably wants the product cannot sensibly forecast
five years of revenue with much confidence.

The finance-oriented investor is applying a real skill to the wrong bottleneck.
More capital, more spending, or a detailed spreadsheet cannot repair weak user
demand. At that stage, nearby evidence is usually conversations, usage,
retention, and willingness to pay. A projection can describe assumptions, but
it cannot validate them.

The episode's other adviser types extend the same point. Founders may rush to
put a famous person on the cap table because that person appears to offer access
or distribution. Yet the famous investor still sees only a slice. A scout can
be enthusiastic and helpful while lacking operating history. An adviser offers
a useful lens, not a magical ability to fix the company.

## Separate Input, Decision, and Ownership

A practical advice process has three roles. The adviser supplies an input. The
founder makes a decision using company context. A named owner runs the decision
and watches the result. Blurring those roles creates two bad escape routes:
blind obedience when the advice fails, or selective name-dropping when the
founder already wanted a particular answer.

Write recommendations in causal form. Instead of “hire a VP of Sales,” record:
“A VP of Sales will make our enterprise pipeline repeatable because founder-led
calls already close consistently.” The team can now inspect the premise. If the
founders have not closed a repeatable set of deals, an executive hire may add
cost before the sales motion exists.

## Original Example: The Portfolio Pattern Trap

A seed investor tells a scheduling startup to move upmarket because another
portfolio company grew through hospital contracts. The startup's evidence says
something narrower: independent clinics activate in one day and retain, while
hospital security reviews stall every pilot.

The founders do not vote on whether the investor is smart. They split the claim.
Hospitals may have greater contract value, but the current product lacks the
compliance and implementation capacity to reach it. They keep clinics as the
primary segment and run one paid hospital discovery engagement with explicit
security milestones. The investor's pattern produces an experiment, not an
order.

## Try It Yourself: Build an Advice Ledger

Take one consequential recommendation received this month and create a
six-column row: recommendation, adviser lens, assumed bottleneck, supporting
evidence, contradictory evidence, and decision owner.

Then spend 20 minutes on the following:

1. Rewrite the advice as “action X should improve outcome Y because mechanism Z.”
2. Add one observation from customers or operations that is closer to the work.
3. Choose the smallest reversible test, with a date and a failure threshold.
4. Record the founder decision even if it rejects the advice.

Repeat the ledger for conflicting recommendations. The point is not to average
opinions. It is to expose why reasonable people disagree and decide which model
best fits the company's present facts.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Accountability is not isolation (optional)</summary>

Founders can be overconfident, emotionally attached, or trapped in local detail.
A strong investor may notice hiring trouble, financing risk, or a repeated
failure mode before the team does. Invite challenge and share enough context for
the challenge to improve. Keep the decision and its consequences explicit.

This lesson is educational and is not individualized investment, financial, or
legal advice. Financing decisions and governance duties depend on the company,
jurisdiction, and documents; use qualified independent professionals where
those consequences matter.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Investor advice reflects genuine experience plus the blind spots and incentives attached to that experience.
- Stage-matched operating evidence should decide whether a recommendation fits, especially before product-market fit.
- Turn advice into a causal claim, run a bounded test, and keep responsibility for the decision with the founders.

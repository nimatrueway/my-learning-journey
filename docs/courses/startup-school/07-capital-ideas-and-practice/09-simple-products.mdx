# Simple Products That Became Big Companies

A simple product is not a collection of fewer half-features. It is the shortest
credible path from one user's painful moment to a completed result. That path can
support a large company because adjacent products become easier to add after the
first result earns trust and repeated use.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 21-minute source video is optional

## Scope Around an Outcome

Feature lists encourage teams to compare themselves with mature companies. A
new search product sees Google's mail, maps, office suite, cloud, and advertising
and imagines it needs a platform. The episode calls this tendency “Googlitis.”
It mistakes a company's current breadth for its starting point.

Define the first product with four boundaries:

1. **User:** one recognizable person with authority to act.
2. **Moment:** a recurring situation in which the problem becomes urgent.
3. **Outcome:** a result the user can verify, not an intermediate dashboard.
4. **Quality bar:** the minimum reliability, trust, or speed that makes the result useful.

Everything required to complete that path belongs in scope. Features that serve
another user, another moment, or a hypothetical future platform wait.

## Source Case: OpenSea Did Not Build the Whole Stack

The episode describes OpenSea after a pivot in YC's 2018 batch. For roughly one
to two years, the team worked on a simple website that let people buy and sell
NFTs. It barely worked at first, but the founders shipped improvements frequently
and stayed focused on the transaction.

They did not build every adjacent component. In particular, users relied on a
third-party wallet rather than an OpenSea wallet. That omission preserved effort
for the marketplace use case. As buyers and sellers gathered, the focused
transaction could create network effects. Building an entire crypto suite first
would have delayed the activity that made the marketplace valuable.

The case also corrects compressed startup history. A company can appear to arrive
suddenly after years of narrow iteration. Simplicity is compatible with hard,
persistent work; it is not a promise of instant success.

## Source Case: Gusto Began as ZenPayroll

Gusto now offers payroll, benefits, org charts, retirement products, and other HR
tools. The episode returns to its beginning as ZenPayroll, which did one thing:
payroll. At the time, a small business might call ADP, receive an office visit
to sign paperwork, and call again to confirm running payroll. ZenPayroll moved
that essential workflow online.

Payroll is narrow in category but complete in consequence. Employees must receive
the correct money on time. Once a provider repeatedly earns that trust, benefits
and other employment workflows become plausible adjacencies. Starting with the
whole modern Gusto suite would have multiplied compliance and operational risk
before proving the first relationship.

Amazon's early book store supplies the same contrast. Founders imitate everything
Amazon does now instead of noticing that it began with a much narrower catalog.

## Simplicity Must Preserve Ground Truth

A simple interface can conceal complex operations, but it cannot fake the core
result. A hardware company that renders a product it cannot manufacture has not
simplified; it has removed the truth. A marketplace with staged listings has not
proved liquidity. A payroll demo that never moves money has not completed payroll.

Choose one ground-truth event: an item changes owners, an employee gets paid, a
document is accepted, or a user returns to perform the job again. Measure that
event before adding breadth.

## Original Example: One Permit, Start to Finish

A construction startup proposes a platform for permits, inspections, contractor
payments, and materials. It cuts scope to residential heat-pump permits in one
city. The product collects the required fields, checks the packet, submits it,
and reports acceptance or corrections.

Inspection scheduling and payments remain outside the product. The narrow path
is still complete because the contractor receives an accepted permit packet.
After 30 successful submissions, observed correction patterns can justify the
next city or permit type.

## Try It Yourself: Make the Completed-Outcome Cut

Write your target user and triggering moment at the top of a page. Underneath,
list every step required for a verifiable result. Circle the single ground-truth
event that proves completion.

Now place each planned feature into one of three groups: required for completion,
required for safety or trust, or adjacency. Remove one adjacency from the next
release. Test the remaining path with five users and record completion rate,
time, failure reason, and whether they return for the same job.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Narrow can still be technically deep (optional)</summary>

Payments, payroll, medical workflows, and hardware may require substantial
invisible correctness to deliver one outcome. Do not remove safety, compliance,
or reliability merely to advertise a smaller MVP. Simplicity concerns product
scope; the quality bar follows the consequence of failure.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Scope the first product around one user, urgent moment, verifiable outcome, and consequence-appropriate quality bar.
- OpenSea and ZenPayroll omitted broad adjacencies while repeatedly completing a focused transaction or payroll job.
- Add breadth in response to observed use and trust, not by copying the current feature map of a mature company.

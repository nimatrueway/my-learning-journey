# Where Do Great Startup Ideas Come From?

Great startup ideas rarely arrive as untouched inventions. More often, founders
notice a real problem, understand why existing attempts are unsatisfying, and
act when a changed capability or behavior makes a better approach possible.
The idea looks obvious only after the company explains it to the world.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 21-minute source video is optional

## Look for Workarounds, Not Brainstorms

A workaround proves that someone already spends time, money, risk, or attention
to obtain an outcome. Spreadsheets glued to email, repeated copy-and-paste, a
specialist hired to navigate one form, or a marketplace transaction completed
off-platform can all mark demand that existing products serve poorly.

For each candidate problem, ask:

1. Who experiences it often enough to notice?
2. What do they do today, including choosing to do nothing?
3. Why have incumbents left the problem unsolved?
4. What changed recently in cost, technology, regulation, or behavior?
5. Why is this team positioned to learn faster than a casual observer?

The final question is not a demand for credentials. Founder advantage may come
from living the problem, building a new capability, or having unusual access to
the first users.

## Source Case: Airbnb Was Not the First Lodging Marketplace

The episode emphasizes that products resembling Airbnb existed before Airbnb.
The founders did not invent the abstract idea of strangers arranging lodging.
They encountered the need directly, used their own product, and improved details
that made a fragile transaction work, including the payment experience and the
quality of listings.

That distinction matters. “Someone tried this” is not proof an idea is dead,
just as “no one has tried this” is not proof it is good. Study the earlier
constraint. Airbnb's opportunity depended on making trust, presentation, and
transaction completion better at a moment when online behavior was changing.
The founders' proximity let them see which details blocked adoption.

## Source Case: Coinbase Made Access the Product

Bitcoin already existed when Coinbase began. The episode frames the opportunity
around how difficult it was for an ordinary person to acquire and hold it. The
startup idea was not “invent cryptocurrency.” It was to make access to an
existing network dramatically easier.

That is a recurring idea pattern: a new underlying capability appears, but the
interface, workflow, or trust layer prevents broader use. Infrastructure shifts
create many such openings. The startup must still prove that easier access leads
to sustained behavior, not merely curiosity.

Stripe fits a related pattern. Developers already accepted payments online, but
the process was painful. The Collison brothers were close to the problem as
programmers and built around a simpler developer experience. Existing payment
companies did not invalidate the idea; their friction supplied the opening.

## Timing Is a Mechanism

“Too early” and “too late” are incomplete diagnoses. Name the dependency. A
product may require smartphone penetration, lower model inference cost, a legal
change, a new distribution channel, or customers newly willing to trust an
online transaction. Then test that dependency directly.

Being contrarian is also insufficient. An idea that everyone rejects may be a
hidden opportunity or simply wrong. The useful combination is a non-obvious
belief plus nearby evidence that can strengthen or falsify it.

## Original Example: Voice Notes Become Maintenance Records

Apartment maintenance supervisors receive dozens of voice notes from technicians.
They replay them, type summaries, attach photos, and update work orders at night.
Generic transcription has existed, but newer low-cost speech models can handle
noisy recordings and domain vocabulary well enough to structure a first draft.

A founder shadows two supervisors and manually turns 50 notes into records. The
test is not whether transcription works in a demo. It is whether supervisors
approve drafts faster than typing, whether required details survive, and whether
the property system accepts the output. The changed capability opens the door;
workflow evidence decides whether there is a company.

## Try It Yourself: Keep a Seven-Day Workaround Log

Record every repeated workaround you observe for one week. Capture the user,
desired outcome, current method, frequency, cost, and visible frustration. Do
not write solutions yet.

At week's end, choose one entry and find three prior products or internal tools.
Name their likely blocker and one condition that may have changed. Deliver the
outcome manually for one user. Ask for a second use or another costly commitment.
The result should update the idea, not merely confirm your enthusiasm.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Personal pain can be a market of one (optional)</summary>

Living a problem improves discovery but can distort frequency and willingness
to pay. Expand beyond peers who share your background. Check buyers, substitutes,
market structure, and retention. Conversely, market size slides cannot rescue a
problem founders understand only from reports.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Repeated workarounds reveal outcomes people already pursue and constraints existing products tolerate.
- Airbnb, Coinbase, and Stripe improved access or transaction details around existing behaviors rather than inventing their categories from nothing.
- Name the changed condition, research earlier attempts, and test the desired outcome with reachable users.

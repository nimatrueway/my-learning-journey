# Understanding Investor Terms & Incentives

A financing offer is not one number. It is a bundle of cash commitment,
ownership, governance, future rights, conditions, and relationships. The
headline valuation can be excellent while the actual path to closing remains
weak or the downside allocation becomes expensive.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 10-minute source video is optional

## Translate Every Term Into a Scenario

Founders often focus on valuation because it is visible and comparable. Yet a
term matters through what it changes. Ask how much money is firmly committed,
what ownership results on a fully diluted basis, who approves major decisions,
what happens in another round, and how proceeds are allocated in a modest exit.

The exact vocabulary varies by security and jurisdiction. The operating habit
does not: never accept a term you cannot explain in plain language across a good,
ordinary, and bad outcome. Use standard documents where appropriate, maintain a
current capitalization model, and have independent counsel explain deviations.

## Source Case: A $3 Million Sheet With $1 Million Attached

The episode gives a concrete example of valuation distracting from commitment.
An investor offers a term sheet at an attractive valuation for a $3 million
round but intends to invest only $1 million. The founder must still find the
remaining $2 million. The document feels like a completed deal, but most of the
financing risk remains with the company.

That difference affects runway and leverage. If the round cannot close without
the missing capital, the founder may spend weeks filling it, accept weaker terms
under pressure, or discover that the apparent signal does not persuade others.
The right comparison is not merely valuation A versus valuation B. It includes
committed dollars, closing conditions, time, and probability of completion.

## Source Case: “Come Back With a Lead”

Caldwell and Seibel decode another common message: an investor says they are
interested once the founder finds a lead. In their experience, founders should
usually treat that as a no, not as committed demand. The investor has preserved
an option to join later if someone else performs the diligence and supplies the
signal.

The episode explains the incentive. A true lead may want enough allocation to
reach an ownership target and can sometimes fill much or all of the round. Once
that lead exists, other investors may also want to participate. The early
“interest” therefore creates little value unless it includes a specific check,
conditions, and decision process.

This is not a moral accusation. Smaller funds may genuinely need to invest
alongside others. The practical point is to distinguish warm language from a
commitment when planning runway.

## Read Incentives Beside Documents

Terms shape behavior after closing. Ownership targets can influence check size.
Follow-on rights can affect future allocation. Board or consent rights can alter
who controls major decisions. Investor portfolio strategy can affect appetite
for risk, additional capital, or an early exit.

Reference checks reveal how those rights are used. Speak with founders whose
companies struggled, raised a difficult next round, and received an acquisition
offer. A pleasant pitch meeting tests salesmanship; a downside reference tests
partnership behavior.

## Original Example: Two Offers at the Same Price

Offer North and Offer Cedar use the same headline valuation. North commits the
entire round on standard documents and requests one board observer. Cedar commits
one-third, requires the founder to fill the rest, and adds approval rights over
the annual budget and senior hires.

The valuation comparison says “tie.” A scenario table does not. Cedar leaves
closing risk and changes operating control. North may still be the wrong partner,
but the founders now know which questions require diligence and legal review.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Three-Scenario Term Map

For a real or hypothetical offer, create rows for committed cash, dilution,
closing conditions, governance, future participation rights, information rights,
and downside economics. Create columns for strong growth, a difficult next round,
and a modest exit.

Write each cell in plain language. Mark unknowns rather than guessing. Add a
founder reference question for every material right, then prepare a separate
list for qualified counsel. The output is not a substitute for legal analysis;
it is a tool for noticing what you do not understand.

## Three Things to Keep

- Compare firm cash commitment and closing conditions alongside valuation, dilution, and governance.
- Treat “come back with a lead” as uncommitted interest unless the investor specifies an actual decision and check.
- Model terms across multiple outcomes, reference-check behavior, and use qualified independent counsel.

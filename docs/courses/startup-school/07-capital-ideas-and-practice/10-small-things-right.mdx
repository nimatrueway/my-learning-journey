# How To Change The World? Get The Small Things Right

A mission describes the world a founder wants. A company still needs a mechanism
that changes what particular people do on Tuesday morning. Large impact is built
from correct local details: incentives, permissions, trust, workflow, sales, and
delivery.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 18-minute source video is optional

## Moral Certainty Does Not Create Demand

The episode begins from a YC founder's retrospective: the team built a product
because it believed the world should work a certain way, not because a customer
wanted to buy what it built. The motive may be generous. The operating error is
substituting “people should want this” for evidence that they will change behavior.

Founders often compress famous stories into heroic willpower. They see leaders
who appeared to force the universe toward electric cars or a new computing
interface. Missing from that summary are years of engineering, manufacturing,
distribution, hiring, financing, and customer decisions. Vision selects a
direction; it does not repeal implementation.

Debating a reluctant user is especially weak. Sales can clarify value and help
someone act on an existing need. It rarely creates a need from nothing. A person
who visits a car lot already has more purchase intent than a random passerby.
Confusing persuasion with demand encourages founders to blame the pitch forever.

## Source Case: “Remove the Dealer” Skips the System

Caldwell and Seibel use car and home sales as examples. Buying a car is unpleasant,
so a founder proposes a website that removes dealers. Buying a house is complex,
so another proposes removing realtors. The disliked intermediary appears to be
the problem.

Research may reveal that the intermediary performs several jobs: sourcing supply,
financing, inspection, negotiation, paperwork, local compliance, risk transfer,
or simply being available when a buyer is ready. Some jobs may be inefficient
and deserve replacement. Deleting the role in a slide does not delete the work.
The startup must decide which jobs it will automate, absorb, or redesign and who
will trust it to do so.

This is why simplistic engineering efficiency can fail in the physical world.
Software can reduce coordination cost, but property, regulation, inventory, and
human incentives remain. A serious mission gets more specific as research
deepens.

## Map Behavior Before Features

For every claimed impact, draw a causal chain. Who discovers the product? Who
uses it? Who pays? Who grants access? Who loses revenue, discretion, or status?
What action must each party take, and why is that action preferable now?

A school product may delight teachers but require district procurement, student
privacy review, administrator configuration, and parent trust. “Improve education”
does not answer any of those questions. Watching a teacher prepare tomorrow's
lesson may reveal a small entry point that a broad reform proposal misses.

Measure outcomes at the smallest honest level. If the mission is cheaper home
energy, begin with installation time, approval rate, financing completion, or
verified energy use for a defined customer. Do not claim system-level impact from
downloads or press coverage.

## Original Example: The Food-Waste Routing Company

A team wants to eliminate restaurant food waste by matching surplus meals with
shelters. Interviews reveal that restaurant managers will donate, but cannot
predict quantity until closing. Shelters need arrival before a fixed time and
cannot accept every food type. Volunteer drivers are unreliable on short notice.

The first product is not a national marketplace. The team serves one district,
three restaurants, one shelter, and a paid evening driver. It standardizes food
categories, confirms quantity at 8 p.m., and measures successful pickups before
the shelter cutoff. The mission remains large, but the local mechanism can now
fail in observable ways.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Stakeholder Field Map

Choose one mission claim and list every party required for it to occur. For each,
record current behavior, incentive to adopt, cost of changing, authority, and
the evidence you have rather than assume.

Interview at least one skeptical stakeholder, not only the beneficiary. Observe
one current workflow from start to finish. Then select the smallest outcome that
requires the chain to work in reality. Set a date, owner, and failure measure.
If the test fails, identify the broken link before adding features or expanding
the impact claim.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Incumbent explanations are not vetoes (optional)</summary>

Intermediaries can defend rents and experts can mistake history for necessity.
Research the job and incentive, then test a better mechanism. Do not preserve a
bad system because it is complex. Do not claim complexity disappeared because
the mission is righteous.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- A mission must become a causal chain of specific stakeholder behaviors, incentives, and measurable outcomes.
- Removing a disliked intermediary requires replacing the useful jobs and trust that role currently supplies.
- Research the full workflow, then prove one local mechanism before expanding the product or impact claim.

# Should You Follow Your Passion?

“Follow your passion” sounds helpful because difficult work needs motivation.
As an idea-generation rule, though, it can send founders searching for customers
who will justify a favorite technology. A better starting point is a real problem
close enough to investigate. Passion can grow from progress and usefulness.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 19-minute source video is optional

## Passion for a Tool Is Not Demand

The episode names a recurring failure mode: a solution in search of a problem.
A founder becomes excited about virtual reality, a marketplace, or a new model,
then cycles through industries asking where it might fit. Each rejected use case
feels like a messaging problem because abandoning the tool feels like abandoning
the founder's identity.

Reverse the order. Start with people whose work you can observe. Find a repeated
cost, delay, risk, or frustration. Understand the current workaround and why
existing vendors tolerate it. Only then ask which solution shape fits.

This does not require founders to be emotionally neutral. Curiosity, taste, and
technical obsession can create unusual insight. The discipline is to attach
that energy to an outcome users already attempt, not to demand applause for a
preselected implementation.

## Source Case: New Passion Can Follow Real Work

Caldwell and Seibel contrast people waiting to discover a perfect calling with
people who encounter concrete problems in daily life. The latter can see whether
their work helps. As skill increases and users respond, the work itself becomes
more compelling. Motivation is partly an output of competence and progress, not
only an input founders must possess before starting.

The episode also challenges the search for approval. A founder can spend months
asking mentors, friends, or online audiences whether an idea sounds meaningful.
Approval offers emotional safety but little product evidence. Choosing a small
piece of real work creates feedback: someone uses it, pays, returns, complains,
or ignores it.

This explains why broad humanitarian problems are not automatically better
startup ideas. Caring about education, climate, or health is admirable, but a
category-sized passion does not identify a customer, workflow, buyer, or wedge.
The founder still needs a specific problem they can reach and learn from.

## Build a Motivation Portfolio

Long startup efforts need more than one fuel source. **Problem motivation** is
care for the user or outcome. **Craft motivation** is enjoyment of becoming good
at the work. **Progress motivation** comes from observable improvement. **Team
motivation** comes from people you respect. External rewards such as money and
status may help, but they are volatile and delayed.

Ask which sources remain during a boring month. A founder who loves machine
learning but hates the customer's domain may resist the unglamorous interviews,
data cleanup, and support that determine whether the model matters. That is a
fit problem worth discovering early.

## Original Example: From Drones to Roof Inspections

Priya loves autonomous drones and initially pitches “drones for every industrial
site.” Instead of building a general platform, she shadows commercial roof
inspectors. They spend hours marking moisture damage on large roofs, repeat
dangerous walks, and produce reports manually for insurers.

Priya first tests the outcome without autonomy: a licensed operator captures
images, and she manually assembles one annotated report. Inspectors care about
consistent evidence and faster reports, not drone sophistication. Her technical
passion remains useful, but the product roadmap now follows an observed job.
If customers do not value the report, better autonomy will not save the idea.

## Try It Yourself: Run a Problem-Proximity Sprint

List three problems you have witnessed firsthand in the last month. For each,
name the person affected, frequency, current workaround, measurable cost, and a
user you can contact today. Choose the one with the strongest observation and
access, not the most impressive market label.

Spend two hours watching or reconstructing the workflow. Then produce one tiny
artifact that improves the outcome: a cleaned report, scheduling script, manual
comparison, or clickable flow. Ask for the next concrete commitment. Finally,
score your motivation before and after the work and write what changed it.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Demand does not obligate you (optional)</summary>

A real market is not a command to spend ten years in it. Personal values,
health, family constraints, and interest matter. The point is to test personal
fit against the real work rather than an imagined mission. Declining a valuable
problem you dislike is honest; pretending a favorite solution has demand is not.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Begin with an observed problem and reachable users before committing to a favorite solution shape.
- Motivation can deepen through competence, useful progress, and customer response rather than arriving as a perfect calling.
- Test both demand and personal fit by doing a small piece of the real work now.

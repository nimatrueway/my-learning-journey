# Compound Interest & User Growth Calculator

Your brain extrapolates in straight lines. Compounding does not care. The same
formula that quietly grows a savings account is the one that makes 5% weekly
user growth look boring for a year and then absurd. This calculator shows both,
next to the straight line your intuition drew.

⏱️ **~6 min** · calculator and interpretation included

## The Formula

One number drives everything: each period, the current total is multiplied by
$(1 + r)$, where $r$ is the growth rate per period.

$$
A = P\,(1 + r)^{n}
$$

- $P$ — the starting amount (money or users).
- $r$ — the rate **per compounding period**, as a decimal.
- $n$ — the number of periods.
- $A$ — what you end up with.

For example, 100 users growing 5% per week for 52 weeks:
$100 \times 1.05^{52} \approx 12{,}643$ users. The linear version of the same
first week — adding 5 users every week — reaches 360.

## What the Two Modes Assume

**Compound interest mode** compounds monthly and adds the contribution at the
end of each month, so the final balance is

$$
A = P\,(1 + \tfrac{r}{12})^{n} + c \cdot \frac{(1 + \tfrac{r}{12})^{n} - 1}{\tfrac{r}{12}}
$$

where $c$ is the monthly contribution and $n$ is months. Real accounts differ:
fees, taxes, variable returns, and contribution timing all move the answer. A
steady 7% is a modeling assumption, not a promise — real market returns arrive
as a jagged sequence that merely averages out to something like it.

**User growth mode** assumes the weekly rate holds every week. That is the
strongest assumption on this page. Sustained percentage growth gets harder as
the base grows, because a constant *rate* requires an ever-larger *absolute*
number of new users each week. Treat long horizons at high rates as an upper
bound, not a forecast.

## The Doubling-Time Shortcut

The rule of 72 estimates doubling time: divide 72 by the percentage rate.
At 7% annual return, money doubles roughly every $72 / 7 \approx 10.3$ years.
At 5% weekly growth, users double roughly every $72 / 5 \approx 14.4$ weeks.
The calculator shows the exact value, $\ln 2 / \ln(1 + r)$; the rule of 72 is
the version you can do while someone is still talking.

## Try It Yourself

Set the interest mode to 10,000 starting, 0 monthly contribution, 7% for
40 years, and note the final balance. Now halve the time to 20 years. The
balance falls to far less than half — compounding is heavily back-loaded, which
is why starting early beats starting big. Then switch to user growth, compare
5% and 7% weekly over two years, and watch a 2-point difference become a
multiple, not a margin.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The actual math (optional)</summary>

Why is doubling time $\ln 2 / \ln(1 + r)$? Solve $(1 + r)^{n} = 2$ by taking
logarithms: $n \ln(1 + r) = \ln 2$. For small $r$, $\ln(1 + r) \approx r$, so
$n \approx \ln 2 / r \approx 0.693 / r$ — which is the "rule of 69.3". The
convention of 72 survives because 72 divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, and
12, and because it slightly corrects for the approximation error at typical
interest rates.

The contribution term in the money mode is a geometric series: a contribution
made $k$ months before the end grows to $c(1 + \tfrac{r}{12})^{k}$, and summing
over all months gives the closed form above. The calculator computes it
iteratively month by month, which produces the same result and also yields the
full curve for the chart.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Compound growth multiplies by $(1 + r)$ each period, so the gap between the curve and the straight line widens forever.
- Time in the formula is an exponent: halving the horizon costs far more than half the growth, and small rate differences compound into multiples.
- Divide 72 by the percentage rate for a quick doubling-time estimate; constant-rate projections are assumptions, not forecasts.

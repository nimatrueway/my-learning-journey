# BMI & Baby Growth Calculator

Adults and older children use BMI-related screening. Babies need a different
view: sex-specific growth standards that compare weight, recumbent length, and
exact age. Choose the mode that matches the person being measured.

⏱️ **~5 min** · calculator and interpretation included

## The Formula

With weight in kilograms and height in metres:

$$
\mathrm{BMI} = \frac{\mathrm{weight\ (kg)}}{\mathrm{height\ (m)}^2}
$$

For example, a person who weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall has a BMI of
$70 / 1.75^2 \approx 22.9$.

## What the Adult Chart Means

For adults age 20 and older, common screening thresholds are:

- Below 18.5: below the healthy-weight range.
- 18.5-24.9: healthy-weight range.
- 25.0-29.9: overweight range.
- 30.0 and above: obesity range.

These are screening categories, not diagnoses. BMI can misrepresent people with
high muscularity, pregnancy, age-related changes in body composition, or health
risks that are not captured by weight and height.

## Why the Calculator Does Not Invent a Percentile

Adult BMI uses fixed thresholds rather than age percentiles. For children and
teenagers age 2-19, BMI is interpreted against official age- and sex-specific
growth references. A valid pediatric percentile needs age in months and the
appropriate reference population; age, height, and weight alone cannot produce
an honest percentile.

## What Baby Growth Mode Shows

For babies and toddlers under 2, the calculator does **not** report BMI. Choose
girl or boy according to the sex-specific WHO reference, then enter exact age in
weeks or months, recumbent length, and weight. It reports:

- **Weight for length:** how weight compares with babies of the same length.
- **Length for age:** how recumbent length compares at the same age and sex.
- **Weight for age:** how weight compares at the same age and sex.

Each chart shows the WHO 15th, 50th, and 85th percentile curves. The red point
is the entered measurement. Week values are converted to fractional months
before interpolating the official monthly WHO LMS standards.

The dashed purple line extends the measurement's current WHO z-score across the
full chart. It answers “where would the same relative position sit elsewhere on
this reference?” It does **not** predict the child's future growth. Move the
cursor or a finger along a chart to inspect the 15th, median, 85th, and same-z
projection values at any age or length.

Growth is a trajectory, not a single score. Accurate recumbent length is hard to
measure, premature infants may need corrected age, and clinical interpretation
depends on repeated measurements, medical history, feeding, and development.

For ages 2-19, use an official BMI-for-age growth chart with sex and exact age.
The BMI mode calculates the number but intentionally withholds a percentile.

## Try It Yourself

In baby mode, switch between weeks and months and confirm that the plotted point
does not jump. Then change recumbent length by 1 cm and watch weight-for-length
move while weight-for-age stays fixed. This is why measurement technique and the
choice of indicator matter.

<details>
<summary>🧭 Limits of BMI (optional)</summary>

BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle, locate body fat, explain a weight
change, or estimate an individual's metabolic health. A clinician may combine it
with growth history, waist measurement, blood pressure, laboratory results,
medications, symptoms, and personal context.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Metric BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared.
- Adult categories are screening thresholds, not population percentiles or diagnoses.
- Under 2, use sex-specific WHO weight-for-length, length-for-age, and weight-for-age trajectories instead of BMI.

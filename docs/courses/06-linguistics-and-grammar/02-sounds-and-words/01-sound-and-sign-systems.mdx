Say *pat*. Now say *bat*. One sound changed, and you got a different word.

But not every tiny pronunciation change gets a new dictionary entry. Which
differences count? Each language has its own answer. Let's listen like detectives.

## Pat, bat: one small change, a new word

Say *pat* and *bat* slowly. Keep the vowel and final sound the same. The first
sound changes, and so does the word. Now we have a useful comparison, not
just two recordings that happen to sound different.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The sound science: phonetics, phonemes, and minimal pairs</summary>

For a typical `[p]`, close both lips, block the air, then release it. The vocal
folds do not vibrate during the closure in this basic description. That gives
us three questions: **where** is the blockage, **how** does air move, and
**what are the vocal folds doing**? Their technical names are place, manner,
and phonation. Studying how signals are made and perceived is **phonetics**.

For vowels, ask how high and how far forward the tongue is, and whether the
lips are rounded. The **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** gives us
symbols for sounds, instead of relying on spelling. English `sh` uses two
letters for the sound written `[ʃ]` in IPA. Letters are not sound counters.

*Pat* and *bat* differ in one sound, in the same position, and have different
meanings. That is a **minimal pair**. It supports treating `/p/` and `/b/`
as different **phonemes**: sound categories that can distinguish words in
the variety we're studying. This is **phonology** territory: how a language
organizes its signals into meaningful contrasts and patterns.

</details>

## A puff of air with two possible jobs

Hold a hand near your mouth and say *pin*, then *spin*. In many English
varieties, the first *p* has a stronger puff of air. The sound context helps
predict the puff; English normally does not use that puff alone to turn one
otherwise identical word into another.

Hindi can use aspiration to distinguish words. Same puff, different job!
Never assume another language uses English's sound categories.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Aspiration, allophones, and why pin/spin isn't a perfect test</summary>

The puff is **aspiration**, written `[pʰ]`. These English pronunciations can
be **allophones**, different versions of one phoneme. *Pin/spin* is not a
minimal pair testing aspiration alone: *spin* also adds `/s/`.

When related sounds appear in different, predictable places, linguists call
that **complementary distribution**. It helps support an allophone explanation.
Still, collect enough examples: missing overlap in a tiny sample could be luck.

</details>

## Mother or horse? Mandarin needs the pitch

| Mandarin form | Dictionary-style meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *mā* | mother |
| *mǎ* | horse |

The other sounds match, but the pitch pattern changes the word. Not every
pitch change means "I'm asking a question." This is why copying English's
habits can send our cat to the wrong dictionary entry.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Tone, intonation, length, and allowed sound combinations</summary>

| Dimension | Comparison worth investigating |
| --- | --- |
| Lexical tone | Mandarin, Yoruba, and Navajo use pitch contrasts despite different genealogies |
| Vowel or consonant length | Japanese distinguishes lengths that English spelling does not reliably represent |
| Click consonants | Found in several southern African families and also in languages such as Zulu through contact |
| Phonotactics | Languages differ in which consonant clusters and syllable patterns they permit |

Mandarin `mā` means "mother" and `mǎ` means "horse" in these dictionary-style
forms. Change the tone, change the word. **Tone** uses pitch for word or
grammar distinctions. **Intonation** uses pitch across an utterance, for
emphasis or some question patterns. A language can use both. The marks here
are pinyin tone marks; tones can sound different in connected speech.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes comparing *pat/bat* and *pin/spin*. What changes? What stays
the same? Which pair changes only the first sound, and which also adds *s*?
Then explain the Mandarin mother/horse example to a friend: why would an
English spelling-based guess miss something important?

<details>
<summary>🤓 Categories are not waveform slices (optional)</summary>

Sounds flow into one another. Your mouth prepares the next sound before it
finishes the current one; this is **coarticulation**. A recording is not a row
of perfectly separate sound bricks. Stress, syllables, tone, and timing also
matter. Which units best explain those patterns is a question to test, not
settle from a spelling chart.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. English *pat/bat* shows a sound difference that changes the word.
2. The puff in *pin/spin* follows context in many English varieties; the pair also differs by *s*.
3. Mandarin *mā/mǎ* shows that pitch can distinguish words, not just questions.

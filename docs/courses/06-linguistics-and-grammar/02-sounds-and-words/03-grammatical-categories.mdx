"We're having dinner."

Lovely! Wait, does *we* include you? English lets that question hang. Some
languages offer different versions of "we" so the dinner invitation is clearer.
Let's see what else grammar can ask a speaker to specify. 🍽️

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~10 min** · includes 5 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

<Meme top="The cat announced: we are sharing dinner." visual="🐱🍽️" bottom="An inclusive pronoun would have prevented this very exclusive meal." />

## Indonesian settles the dinner invitation

| Indonesian "we" | Is the listener included? |
| --- | --- |
| *kita* | yes |
| *kami* | no |

English *we* leaves that question to context or an extra explanation. Indonesian
offers two different pronouns. The cat would like the choice settled before
the food arrives, not after the plates are empty.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The technical names: clusivity, person, and number</summary>

This inclusive/exclusive distinction is **clusivity**. **Person** tracks
speaker, addressee, and others. **Number** distinguishes values such as singular,
plural, or **dual** for two. Pronoun and verb-agreement options need not match.

</details>

## English can change the view without changing the past

Compare "We walked" with "We were walking." Both are past. The second puts
us inside an activity underway. "We might walk" adds a possibility instead.
Three useful questions: **when, what view of the event, and what possibility?**

<details>
<summary>🤓 Tense, aspect, mood, and the perfect/perfective trap</summary>

English can add "I saw it happen" to explain how someone knows. Another language
may build that information into a verb form. The interesting question is not
"Can the speakers understand this idea?" It is **how do they express it, and
when does the grammar require it?** No past-tense ending does not mean no yesterday.

## When? What view of the event? What possibility?

Compare "We walked" and "We were walking." Both are past, but the second gives
an inside view of an activity underway. **Tense** helps locate a situation in
time. **Aspect** tells us how the event is presented.

Two common aspect labels: **perfective** gives a whole-event view;
**imperfective** often shows an ongoing or habitual event without that whole-event
view. Perfective does not simply mean "past" or "finished forever." **Perfect**
is a different label, linking an earlier situation to a reference time, as in
some uses of English "has arrived." Check how each language uses these labels.

"We might walk" asks about possibility, not just time. Possibilities, wishes,
necessities, and commands bring in **mood** and **modality**. Forms can combine
tense, aspect, and mood; grammars often shorten that bundle to **TAM**.

</details>

## Turkish asks: how do you know Ahmet came?

| Turkish example in WALS | Contrast illustrated |
| --- | --- |
| *Ahmet gel-di* | Ahmet came; direct evidence |
| *Ahmet gel-miş* | Ahmet came; indirect evidence |

The endings help signal an information-source difference in WALS's comparison.
English can add words such as "I saw him" or "apparently." Those are teaching
paraphrases, not extra words literally hiding inside each Turkish ending.
Real usage has more nuance, and this is not a true/false switch.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Evidentiality is not the same as certainty</summary>

**Evidentiality** marks information source or type. **Epistemic modality**
concerns confidence or likelihood. The two can interact, but hearing something
from a reliable source can be convincing and direct perception can mislead.
In WALS's Turkish analysis, these endings combine past-related and evidential
contributions. They are not universal labels meaning "true" and "false."

</details>

## French: the little word changes with the noun

Compare *un livre*, "a book," with *une table*, "a table." The article changes
with the noun's grammatical group. A table is not a woman and a book is not
a man: the grouping belongs to the grammar, not a biography of the objects.

English gives another useful comparison: "a notebook" versus "the notebook."
Can the listener identify which notebook? That question differs from when
an event happened or who was invited to dinner.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Gender, noun classes, counting classifiers, and definiteness</summary>

Nouns can belong to groups that affect the forms of other words. That is the
core of **grammatical gender** and **noun-class agreement**, not a direct
sorting of people by social gender. Swahili noun classes affect agreement
beyond the noun. Meanings such as living things or shape can help organize
classes, but history and exceptions also matter. Learn the patterns, not just
a noun's prefix.

Mandarin and many Southeast Asian languages use **numeral classifiers** in
counting constructions. These can group things by kind or shape. Their grammar
is not simply the same as noun-class agreement; a language can have both systems.

Finally, "a notebook" differs from "the notebook." **Definiteness** concerns
whether the listener can identify what you mean. **Specificity** concerns
whether you have a particular thing in mind; that is not the same question.
Languages can use articles, endings, pointing words, other constructions, or
context. WALS counts some of these more broadly than a school grammar might.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes writing two dinner invitations: one includes the listener,
one does not. How would English and Indonesian make that clear? Then explain
what "We were walking" tells you about who, when, and the activity. Add "I saw it" or
"someone told me" versions. Which information is in the grammar, and which
did you add with extra words?

<details>
<summary>🤓 Categories are comparison tools, not identical boxes (optional)</summary>

Two forms translated as English past tense might differ in how long ago,
what view of the event they give, or how the speaker knows. A comparison needs
a clear definition of what counts as "past." Grammatical distinctions can
draw attention to things in a task, but they do not prove that speakers cannot
think about anything their grammar leaves unmarked.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Ask how a language expresses an idea, and when its grammar requires the information.
2. When, what event view, what possibility, and how you know are different questions.
3. Other languages may mark who is included, how many, and how nouns group in unfamiliar ways.

"We left" does not tell you whether the listener was included. "Someone arrived"
does not tell you how the speaker knows. Languages differ in which distinctions
they routinely encode in grammar, not in what their speakers can understand.

## Meaning available versus grammar required

Any language can use paraphrase, context, or lexical expressions to communicate
many distinctions that another language marks grammatically. Compare English
"I saw it happen" with a grammatical evidential. The question is **where,
how, and when a distinction is encoded**, including whether it is obligatory
in a specified construction. "No tense inflection" does not mean "no concept of time."

**Tense** locates a situation relative to a reference time. **Aspect** describes
its temporal perspective or internal organization. **Perfective** typically
presents an event as a whole; **imperfective** presents it without that whole-event
view, often as ongoing or habitual. Perfective is not simply "past" or "completed
forever," and **perfect** is a different category associated with anteriority
and relevance to a reference time. Definitions and interactions vary by language.

**Mood** and **modality** concern possibilities, necessities, wishes, commands,
and related statuses. A possibility is not a time. Grammatical forms can combine
tense, aspect, and mood, which is why grammars often speak of a **TAM system**.

## Evidentiality is not a truth meter

**Evidentiality** encodes the source or type of information: witnessed,
inferred, or reported, depending on the system. Turkish *Ahmet gel-di* and
*Ahmet gel-miş* illustrate a direct versus indirect contrast in WALS chapter
77's treatment. `gel-` means "come"; the endings combine past-related and
evidential contributions in that analysis. They are not universal one-to-one
labels for "true" and "false," and actual Turkish usage has additional nuances.

**Epistemic modality** concerns confidence or likelihood. It can interact
with evidentiality, but knowing the source does not guarantee truth or confidence.
A reliable report can be believed strongly; direct perception can be misleading.

## Person and number are not just English pronouns

**Person** distinguishes speaker, addressee, and others. **Number** may distinguish
singular, dual (two), plural, or other values in specific paradigms. **Clusivity**
distinguishes inclusive "we" (including the addressee) from exclusive "we"
(excluding the addressee). Indonesian `kita` versus `kami` is an accessible
comparison; English `we` leaves that distinction to context or paraphrase.
Pronoun and verbal-agreement inventories need not match perfectly.

## Nouns: classes, counting, and identifiability

**Grammatical gender** is an agreement-class system, not a direct classification
of social gender. **Noun classes** can be numerous and partly associated with
animacy, shape, or other meanings, yet contain lexical and historical exceptions.
Swahili noun classes affect agreement beyond the noun itself. Memorizing a
class prefix alone does not describe the whole agreement pattern.

**Numeral classifiers**, familiar from Mandarin and many Southeast Asian
languages, occur in counting constructions and can classify referents by
conventional categories such as shape or kind. They are not simply the same
mechanism as noun-class agreement. A language may have both types of systems.

**Definiteness** concerns identifiable reference in context; **specificity**
concerns a particular intended referent and is not identical to definiteness.
English has articles such as *the* and *a*. Other languages use affixes,
demonstratives, other constructions, or leave the distinction implicit.
WALS chapter 37 explicitly uses broader coding criteria than some school grammars.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes making a feature profile for English and Indonesian: how
do you distinguish inclusive from exclusive "we"? Then annotate "We were
walking" for person, number, tense, and aspect. Write a witnessed and a reported
version using English paraphrases. Mark which contributions are grammatically
encoded and which are additional lexical information.

## Recap 🐾

1. Languages differ in grammatical encoding and obligation, not simply in available ideas.
2. Tense, aspect, modality, and evidentiality answer different questions even when forms combine them.
3. Clusivity, number, agreement classes, classifiers, and articles broaden the comparison beyond English.

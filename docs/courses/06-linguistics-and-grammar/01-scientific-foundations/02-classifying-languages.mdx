## Three classifications, three questions

**Genealogical classification** asks which languages descend from a common
ancestor. English is Germanic; Persian is Iranian; both are Indo-European.
Arabic is Semitic within Afro-Asiatic. Borrowed Arabic words in Persian do not
make Persian Semitic, just as French loans did not make English a Romance language.

Evidence for ancestry includes **regular sound correspondences**, shared
grammatical innovations, and reconstruction across many words and forms.
English *father* and Latin *pater* illustrate one correspondence, but one similar
word is not enough. Inherited words are **cognates**; a similar-looking borrowed
word is not evidence for common ancestry between the borrowing and donor languages.

**Typological classification** compares defined structural features: dominant
word order, tone, case marking, or how words package morphemes. Turkish and
Japanese both commonly place objects before verbs and use substantial suffixing.
Those similarities do not establish a Turkic–Japonic ancestor. Treat the disputed
"Altaic" proposal as a proposal, not a settled family created by shared suffixes.

**Areal analysis** asks how contact shapes neighboring languages. A **Sprachbund**
is an area where languages share structural features through interaction,
even across genealogical boundaries. Balkan languages provide a classic case:
Albanian, Greek, and Romanian belong to different Indo-European branches, yet
share features associated with long contact. Contact and inheritance can operate together.

## First stops on the family map

These are examples, not an exhaustive list or a ranking by importance.
Regions describe historical concentrations, not where all speakers live today.

| Family or branch | Some languages | Initial region to explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indo-European | English, Spanish, Persian, Hindi | Europe and South Asia |
| Afro-Asiatic | Arabic, Hebrew, Hausa, Amharic | Northern Africa and western Asia |
| Atlantic-Congo, within broader Niger-Congo proposals | Swahili, Yoruba, Zulu | Sub-Saharan Africa |
| Sino-Tibetan | Mandarin, Tibetan, Burmese | East Asia and Himalayan regions |
| Dravidian | Tamil, Telugu, Kannada | South Asia |
| Turkic | Turkish, Kazakh, Uzbek | Across Eurasia |
| Uralic | Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian | Northern and central Eurasia |
| Austroasiatic | Vietnamese, Khmer | Mainland Southeast Asia |
| Kra-Dai, also called Tai-Kadai | Thai, Lao | Southeast Asia and southern China |
| Austronesian | Indonesian, Tagalog, Māori, Malagasy | Island Southeast Asia, Pacific, Madagascar |
| Japonic and Koreanic, separate families | Japanese; Korean | East Asia |
| Pama-Nyungan | Warlpiri, Pitjantjatjara | Much of Australia |
| Mayan | K'iche', Yucatec Maya | Mesoamerica |
| Quechuan and Aymaran, separate families | Quechua varieties; Aymara | Andes |
| Eskimo-Aleut | Inuktitut, Kalaallisut, Unangam Tunuu | Arctic regions and Aleutians |

**Papuan** is a geographical cover term for many non-Austronesian families and
isolates, not a single demonstrated family. **Khoisan** likewise should not be
treated as one established family merely because some languages have clicks.
A **language isolate**, such as Basque, has no demonstrated genealogical
relatives under the classification used; it does not mean primitive, unchanging,
or unstudied. Internal subgrouping and some larger proposed families remain debated.

## Counts and boundaries are decisions too

There are thousands of languages, but no timeless exact count. Documentation,
classification, language change, and decisions about language versus dialect
affect totals. Mutual intelligibility is evidence, not an all-purpose boundary:
it can be asymmetric, affected by exposure, or gradual along a dialect continuum.
Political labels and community identities matter alongside structural analysis.

Signed languages have independent genealogies. ASL is historically connected
to French Sign Language, whereas British Sign Language has a different history.
Shared surrounding English does not make ASL and BSL varieties of one language.
Nor does a database's administrative "Sign Language" grouping establish one family.

<summary>🤓 Trees, waves, and reconstruction (optional)</summary>

A family tree models successive divergence; a wave model captures innovations
spreading across overlapping communities. Both abstract away from complicated
histories. An asterisk on a reconstructed historical form means unattested
reconstruction, not ungrammaticality. Check that convention before reading a
historical paper. Deep-time proposals require evidence that rules out borrowing
and chance resemblance; superficial similarity becomes easier to find as comparisons grow.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Genealogy tracks ancestry; typology tracks defined features; areality tracks contact.
2. Shared scripts, borrowed words, and structural similarities alone do not prove a family.
3. Family boundaries, language counts, and historical models include uncertainty and conventions.

Surprise: English and Persian are distant cousins. Persian and Arabic share
many words and related writing systems, but belong to different families.

So what makes two languages "related"? Their words? Their grammar? Their
alphabet? Time to unpack three different maps. The cat has packed one too many
highlighters. 🌍🐱

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~10 min** · includes 5 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

## Map 1: who are your relatives?

English belongs to the Germanic branch; Persian belongs to the Iranian branch.
Both branches sit in the **Indo-European family**. Arabic belongs to the Semitic
branch of **Afro-Asiatic**. Cousins do not have to look alike today, and neighbors
do not automatically share grandparents.

Compare English *father* with Latin *pater*. The meanings match, and the sounds
give us something to investigate. One pair is a clue, not a family reunion
invitation. We need a whole set of matching patterns before declaring ancestry.

<details>
<summary>🤓 How linguists establish a family</summary>

This is **genealogical classification**. Words inherited from a common ancestor
are **cognates**. Researchers compare regular sound correspondences, shared
innovations, and reconstructable earlier forms across many words and grammatical
patterns. A similar-looking pair alone cannot rule out borrowing or coincidence.

</details>

## Map 2: how does your grammar work?

Turkish and Japanese often put the object before the verb. Both also build
many forms by adding endings. Those are useful similarities even though they
do not prove a shared ancestor.

Think "feature checklist," not "family tree." We will inspect the Japanese
sentence "John read the letter" and Turkish *ev-ler-de*, "in the houses," in
later lessons. One comparison asks where the verb goes; another asks what
the endings do. Neither similarity is a birth certificate.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The feature map has a name: typology</summary>

**Typology** compares structural features, such as word order, tone, case,
and word-building. Similar features need not come from one ancestor. Proposed
"Altaic" groupings are disputed; shared endings and verb-final order do not
settle that historical question.

</details>

## Map 3: who have you been talking to?

Persian uses many words borrowed from Arabic. That does not make it a Semitic
language, any more than borrowing a recipe changes your grandparents.
Words, writing tools, and sometimes grammatical patterns can travel between
communities.

The simple question is **who influenced whom?** Neighbors can share things
without being close relatives. Relatives can influence each other too.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Contact, areas, and overlapping histories</summary>

**Areal analysis** studies patterns across a region. A **Sprachbund** is an
area where languages share structures through contact. The Balkans offer
examples involving Albanian, Greek, Romanian, and other languages from
different branches. Contact and inherited structure can work together; a map
alone cannot decide which explanation is right.

</details>

## First stops on the family map

Here are some starting points, not all the families and not a league table.
The regions help plan our tour; speakers also live in many other places.

<details>
<summary>🌍 Open the family map: fifteen starting groups</summary>

| Family or branch | Some languages | Initial region to explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indo-European | English, Spanish, Persian, Hindi | Europe and South Asia |
| Afro-Asiatic | Arabic, Hebrew, Hausa, Amharic | Northern Africa and western Asia |
| Atlantic-Congo, within broader Niger-Congo proposals | Swahili, Yoruba, Zulu | Sub-Saharan Africa |
| Sino-Tibetan | Mandarin, Tibetan, Burmese | East Asia and Himalayan regions |
| Dravidian | Tamil, Telugu, Kannada | South Asia |
| Turkic | Turkish, Kazakh, Uzbek | Across Eurasia |
| Uralic | Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian | Northern and central Eurasia |
| Austroasiatic | Vietnamese, Khmer | Mainland Southeast Asia |
| Kra-Dai, also called Tai-Kadai | Thai, Lao | Southeast Asia and southern China |
| Austronesian | Indonesian, Tagalog, Māori, Malagasy | Island Southeast Asia, Pacific, Madagascar |
| Japonic and Koreanic, separate families | Japanese; Korean | East Asia |
| Pama-Nyungan | Warlpiri, Pitjantjatjara | Much of Australia |
| Mayan | K'iche', Yucatec Maya | Mesoamerica |
| Quechuan and Aymaran, separate families | Quechua varieties; Aymara | Andes |
| Eskimo-Aleut | Inuktitut, Kalaallisut, Unangam Tunuu | Arctic regions and Aleutians |

Watch out for labels that look like families but are not. **Papuan** covers many
non-Austronesian languages around New Guinea, not one proven family. Clicks do
not make all so-called **Khoisan** languages one established family either.

Basque is a **language isolate**: no relatives have been demonstrated in the
classification we're using. It still has history and a full grammar. "We have
not established relatives" does not mean "this language came from nowhere."
Some branch arrangements and bigger family proposals are still debated.

</details>

<details>
<summary>🤓 Why there's no timeless exact language count</summary>

Thousands. But an exact count needs a date and some decisions about what counts
as a language versus a dialect. Documentation changes; languages change too.
"Can the speakers understand each other?" helps, but is not a magic dividing
line. Understanding can depend on exposure, work better in one direction, or
fade gradually across neighboring communities. Identity and politics also matter.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes making a tiny passport for Persian, Turkish, and Arabic.
Give each a family label, a grammar feature to investigate, and a writing-system
label. Underline what you can support; write "not sure yet" where needed. Can a
shared script tell you how verbs change with their subjects? Explain why not.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Trees, waves, and reconstruction (optional)</summary>

A tree shows languages splitting into branches. A wave model shows changes
spreading through overlapping communities. Real histories can need both pictures.
In historical work, an asterisk on a reconstructed form means "our proposed
earlier form," not "bad grammar." Long-range family proposals must rule out
borrowing and chance matches. Search enough words and you will find coincidences!

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Use three maps: ancestry, grammar features, and contact between communities.
2. A shared script, borrowed word, or grammar feature does not prove a shared ancestor.
3. Family labels and language counts need evidence, dates, and room for uncertainty.

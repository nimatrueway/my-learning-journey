"Of the islands" takes three English words. Latin can fit it into one.

Where did the other words go? Nowhere! Latin packs the meaning differently.
Let's open the word and see what is inside. 🐱🔬

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~9 min** · includes 5 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

## Latin: one word, several jobs

Compare these real forms from the Leipzig Glossing Rules:

| Language | Form | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Latin | *insularum* | of the islands |
| French | *aux chevaux* | to the horses |

Latin's `-arum` ending tells us both "more than one" and, here, an "of"
relationship. Two jobs in one ending. French *aux* similarly packs information
that English spreads across *to the*. Counting English words will not tell us
how the other language built its phrase.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Read the three-line label under the Latin example</summary>

```text
insul-arum
island-GEN.PL
'of the islands'
```

The first line shows the Latin form and proposed split. The second is a
**gloss**, labeling each piece. `GEN` is **genitive**, the category behind
the "of" translation here; `PL` is plural. The dot says one piece has both
labels. The third line gives natural English meaning, not Latin structure.

A **morpheme** is an analyzed piece with a meaning or grammatical job. It
need not be a whole word or have a separate English translation. Glosses
help us see the analysis without pretending every language is English.

</details>

## Indonesian: where did "are" go?

Another published example, this time from Indonesian:

> **Mereka di Jakarta sekarang.**
>
> They are in Jakarta now.

The four Indonesian words give *they*, *in*, *Jakarta*, and *now*. There is
no separate word corresponding to English *are* in this example. Nothing is
missing from the message. Indonesian does not need English's exact recipe
for this sentence. The translator supplies natural English, not a photocopy.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Hyphens, dots, equals signs, and abbreviation keys</summary>

| Notation | What it normally communicates |
| --- | --- |
| `root-affix` / `ROOT-LABEL` | A proposed split into pieces; match the hyphens on both lines |
| `word=clitic` | A proposed clitic attachment; match the equals signs on both lines |
| `GEN.PL` | Several labels for one source element |
| `1SG`, `2PL`, `3SG` | First-person singular, second-person plural, third-person singular |
| `PST`, `PRS`, `NEG`, `Q` | Past, present, negation, question marker |
| `NOM`, `ACC`, `ERG`, `ABS` | Case labels, explained in the alignment lesson |

A **clitic** leans on another word for its pronunciation but often acts more
like a separate word in sentence structure than an ordinary ending does.
Figuring that out takes evidence; a space or equals sign cannot settle it.

Keep the author's abbreviation key nearby. `S` might name the participant in
"the bird sleeps," not "singular." Labels can vary. The cat's rule: read the
key before confidently decoding the wrong thing.

Here is a real equals-sign example from West Greenlandic, also published in
the Leipzig rules:

```text
palasi=lu  niuirtur=lu
priest=and shopkeeper=and
'both the priest and the shopkeeper'
```

The repeated `=lu` means "and" here. The equals sign marks a clitic boundary
in this analysis, not a detachable English-style word.

You might see someone label singular with a **zero marker**: a grammatical
job with no spoken piece of its own. That is a claim about the wider system,
not a reason to add an invisible ending whenever a table has an empty slot.

</details>

Keep what was said, what it meant, and how you explain it on separate lines.
Researchers can agree on the words but disagree about their labels. Save the
original example and its source so someone else can check your explanation.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes explaining *insularum*, *aux chevaux*, and *Mereka di
Jakarta sekarang* without using technical labels. Circle what English spreads
across more words. Then open the first fold and explain why the dot in `GEN.PL`
does not mean that Latin has two separate endings.

<details>
<summary>🤓 What a transcription leaves out (optional)</summary>

Ordinary spelling, a list of contrastive sounds, and a detailed pronunciation
record are different views. `/p/` usually names a phoneme; `[pʰ]` records
a pronunciation detail, here a puff of air. We will unpack those next.
A written line cannot capture every detail of actual speech. Keep the recording
when available; an English translation cannot replace it.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Latin *insularum* fits "of the islands" into one word; count the original, not the translation.
2. Indonesian's example has no separate *are*, even though natural English needs it.
3. Extra labels can help explain the pieces, but they are not the original evidence.

"I didn't see nobody."

An editor might reach for a red pen. A linguist asks: **Who says this, and
what do they mean?** In several English varieties, the negative words work
together to mean that no person was seen. The speakers have a pattern,
even if a particular publication wants different wording.

Today we're investigating patterns, not handing out grammar parking tickets. 🐱🔍

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~10 min** · includes 5 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

## One book. Two books. Case closed?

Start with these real English forms, listed in the British Council's grammar
reference. No invented words to memorize.

| One | More than one |
| --- | --- |
| book | books |
| school | schools |
| friend | friends |

What changes? Add `-s`. Our first guess: **add `-s` to talk about more than one.**

It's a useful guess. But three examples cannot promise that every English
noun works this way. The cat has already printed a certificate. Too soon.

## A child walks in and ruins the shortcut

One child. Two **children**. Not the form our simple `-s` rule predicted.

Does English have no rules? Of course not. Does our first rule cover every
word? Also no. Better: **many nouns add `-s`; some use other forms.**

Now try *foot → feet*. Even the inside of a word can change. Exceptions are
not an excuse to throw away the notebook. They are the reason to keep using it.

## What would you check next?

Look up another word, ask a speaker, or find examples in conversation and
writing. Keep the sentence around the word, not just a spelling list.

Suppose your three-row table has no *feet*. That does not mean English lacks
the word. It means your table did not include it. **Not checked** and **not
possible** are very different notes.

The same habit works when we leave English: collect real examples, make a
prediction, and let the next example change your mind.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The technical names: rules, guesses, and evidence</summary>

An editing recommendation is **prescriptive**. An account of the system
speakers use is **descriptive grammar**. The negatives in the opening illustrate
**negative concord** in the varieties where they express one negative meaning.

Our testable guess is a **hypothesis**. *Children* is a **counterexample** to
the claim that every noun simply adds `-s`, not to the claim that many do.
Asking speakers for examples is **elicitation**; searching a collection of
written or recorded language means using a **corpus**. Neither one short
recording nor one speaker's reaction can describe every variety.

**Grammaticality** concerns the system's rules; **acceptability** concerns
how a sentence feels in a situation. Length or unfamiliarity can make a
grammatical sentence hard to process. An asterisk often marks a form judged
ungrammatical in the variety described; ask what evidence supports it.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes sorting *books*, *schools*, *children*, and *feet* into
"fits add-s" and "needs another explanation." Write your improved rule in
one sentence. Then look up *woman* in the linked reference before revealing
its plural to a friend. What would the original shortcut have predicted?

<details>
<summary>🤓 Why testing a new word matters</summary>

A list of memorized forms and a working word-building rule can both explain
familiar examples. A **productivity test** asks whether speakers can apply
a pattern to a new item. That is a research method, not a reason to replace
our real-language examples with a fictional language. The best explanation
must handle both familiar forms and further evidence.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. *Books* supports the add-s guess; *children* shows why it needs limits.
2. An exception improves the explanation. It does not mean the language has no system.
3. A word missing from your table is not a word missing from the language.

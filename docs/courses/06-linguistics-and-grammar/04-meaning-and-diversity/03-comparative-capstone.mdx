Three languages do something the same way. A world rule? Hold that headline.
They might be three close relatives, and the next language might disagree.

For our final stop, you'll build a language profile and test a tiny claim.
The cat has a clipboard. You have veto power over its dramatic announcements. 📋

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~19 min** · includes 12 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

## Make a language passport, not a single label

Choose a language or variety from the [world-language atlas](../world-language-atlas.md).
Give it separate notes for family history, community and period, sounds,
word-building, sentence patterns, grammatical categories, and meaning.
One label cannot do all those jobs.

For each note, write **what you think is true**, **the example or source**,
**where the claim applies**, and **how sure you are**. "Read follows the letter
in this Japanese statement" is safer than "every sentence works that way."
"These nouns have no visible case
contrast" does not mean "nobody can tell who did what." Keep "we don't have
the data" separate from "we checked and it isn't there."

| Profile field | A useful evidence question |
| --- | --- |
| Family and contact | Which classification are you using? What borrowing history is known? |
| Sounds | What pair or pattern shows that a difference counts? |
| Word-building | Which word forms show pieces doing one job or several? |
| Sentences and participants | Which sentence type and participants did you check? What marks them? |
| Categories | What must speakers mark, what can they add, and what can context supply? |
| Meaning | Which meanings come from ordinary pieces, and which need a learned whole expression? |

For an idiom or other non-compositional expression, add its wording, rough
literal translation, learned meaning, and appropriate setting. Try a word
swap or a grammatical change. A dictionary can confirm a meaning without
answering every question about how the expression behaves.

## Can you catch the cat's world rule?

English says "see the woman" and "in the house." The action comes before
the thing seen; *in* comes before the house. Our cat makes a leap: "When the
action comes first, relation words must always come first too!"

Check these **real comparison cases from WALS chapter 95**. The columns
report dominant patterns, not every possible sentence or relation word.

| Language | Action and object | Relation word and noun phrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English | action first | relation word before |
| Chalcatongo Mixtec | action first | relation word before |
| Koyra Chiini | action first | relation word after |
| Arawak | action first | relation word after |
| Nivkh | object first | relation word after |
| Persian | object first | relation word before |
| Tigre | object first | relation word before |

Koyra Chiini and Arawak disagree with the cat's rule. In the published Koyra
Chiini examples, *guna*, "see," precedes the woman phrase; *ra*, a location
marker, follows the house phrase. Both pieces are worth looking at, not just
the English translation.

These seven languages were picked to show contrasting patterns. They are
**not a representative world sample**. The wider WALS survey finds strong
tendencies; a table deliberately containing unusual cases is not a shortcut
to world percentages. "Often" survives evidence that defeats "always."

<details>
<summary>🤓 The real Koyra Chiini forms and the VO/OV terminology</summary>

From WALS chapter 95, example 3, citing Heath (1999b: 103, 124):

```text
har di o guna woy di
man DEF IMPF see woman DEF
'The man sees the woman.'

ay too huu di ra
1SG arrive house DEF LOC
'I arrived in the house.'
```

**VO** means verb before object; **OV** reverses that order. A **preposition**
precedes its noun phrase; a **postposition** follows. Koyra Chiini has a few
prepositions too: WALS codes its dominant strategy, not their total absence.
Among our four VO rows, two have dominant prepositions. Among the three OV
rows, two do. Those fractions describe this deliberately selected table,
not the full survey or an ancestry-controlled world estimate.

</details>

## How do we make a better comparison?

Define what counts before counting. Name the variety and sentence type.
Include different families and regions, keep the sources, and explain your
choices. More easy-to-find relatives do not automatically make a better sample.
Report exceptions and uncertainty. Would another sensible definition change
the answer? Check that too.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Sampling, missing data, family relationships, and feature definitions</summary>

WALS helps organize features, but different features cover different languages.
Combine two tables and you may lose languages with one missing value.
Glottolog helps find family classifications and sources; it does not hand you
the grammar of every family member. Useful tools, not an autopilot for conclusions.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend twelve minutes on two tasks. First, find the four action-first rows
and explain why Koyra Chiini challenges the cat's rule. Then choose two languages
from different atlas regions. Draft notes for family, word order, word-building,
and one meaning feature. Reuse the lessons' examples where they fit, and leave
unknowns honest. Finish with one shared feature, one difference, and one
question you would love better evidence for.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Finding a pattern is not yet explaining it (optional)</summary>

Two features might appear together because of ancestry, contact, how people
process language, shared routes of change, or our counting rules. Statistical
models can help handle family and area relationships, but their assumptions
also need checking. "Unique" requires ruling out all relevant alternatives;
"rare in this sample" is a smaller, usually safer claim. Our tiny fractions
teach sampling, not the probabilities of features in unstudied languages.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Keep separate feature notes, with examples, sources, limits, and uncertainty.
2. Always, often, an exception, and unknown are four different things.
3. Families, regions, missing data, and counting rules can change the apparent pattern.

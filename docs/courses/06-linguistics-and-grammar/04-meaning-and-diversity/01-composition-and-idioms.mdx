Someone trying to solve a French riddle says they "give their tongue to the cat."
Don't call a vet. They are giving up guessing.

You translated every word correctly. You still missed the message. How does
that happen? Welcome to the part of grammar where the whole can surprise you. 🐱💬

## Usually, the pieces help us build the meaning

Read "the purple notebook under the chair." You probably understand it
without having memorized that exact phrase. The words and their arrangement
let you build its meaning. No special notebook legend is needed.

The arrangement matters. "The child followed the dog" and "The dog followed
the child" contain the same words, but tell different stories. This is not
just adding dictionary definitions into a bag.

## Sometimes, you need to know the whole expression

Our French riddle phrase needs a learned meaning that ordinary word meanings
do not predict. It means giving up guessing, not mailing a tongue to our
research assistant. Knowing the whole expression matters here.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Compositionality, semantics, and where the special meaning lives</summary>

**Compositionality** builds meaning from parts and their arrangement.
**Semantics** studies linguistic meaning. A **non-compositional** expression
has a meaning not predictable from the ordinary part meanings and combination
rules we are assuming. It still has grammar. A stored whole-expression meaning
or a special construction rule can combine normally with surrounding material.

</details>

## A small cross-language tour

These are established dictionary expressions, not recorded conversations.
The literal column is a rough teaching translation; the final column gives
the learned sense documented in the sources below.

| Language | Expression | Rough literal rendering | Conventional interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| English | *kick the bucket* | strike a bucket with the foot | die, in an informal idiomatic use |
| French | *donner sa langue au chat* | give one's tongue to the cat | stop trying to guess the answer |
| Spanish | *tomar el pelo* | take the hair | tease or fool someone |
| Japanese | *neko o kaburu*, 猫を被る | wear a cat | feign innocence |
| Mandarin | *mǎmǎhūhū*, 马马虎虎 | horse–horse–tiger–tiger | careless, or so-so in relevant uses |

An idiom can also be literal in the right scene: someone really could kick a
bucket. Context matters, as does whether an expression is casual, rude, or
appropriate for the situation. These examples do not prove that every language
has the same idiom types, or that a whole culture thinks in cats and buckets.
They give us expressions to compare carefully.

## Strong tea is not another tongue-delivery mystery

English *strong tea* mostly means what its words suggest. It is a familiar
combination, not necessarily a phrase whose meaning you must learn separately.
Frequently appearing together does not automatically make words a mystery.

Call a negotiator "a bulldozer" and you invite a comparison. A new comparison
does not have to be a memorized expression either.

Now say "It is cold here" beside an open window. Someone might close it.
You did not need *cold* to mean "close the window" in the dictionary. They
worked out your likely intention from the situation.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Idiom, collocation, metaphor, pragmatics, polysemy, and ambiguity</summary>

An **idiom** has a learned, conventional meaning; its transparency varies.
**Collocation** is preferred co-occurrence, as in *strong tea*. **Metaphor**
invites a comparison; some become conventional. **Pragmatics** studies context
and use. **Polysemy** gives one word related senses. **Structural ambiguity**
gives a phrase different groupings, as in the binocular example. These are
different questions, and "hard to translate" does not settle any of them.

</details>

The French expression is attested; the English descriptions are ordinary

## Can you change the wording and keep the meaning?

Try *kicked the bucket*: the verb can change tense. But *kicked the pail* does
not normally keep the idiom's meaning. "The bucket was kicked" usually favors
a literal reading, outside special contexts. Other idioms tolerate different
changes. Test word substitutions, tense, extra modifiers, and passive forms
with speakers and text examples. An idiom is not necessarily a frozen sentence.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Surprising meanings beyond sayings: present use versus history</summary>

The same puzzle can happen in ordinary words and grammatical combinations,
not just colorful sayings: familiar pieces may not predict today's whole
meaning. Keep three questions separate: how did it arise, can speakers guess
its meaning now, and where does the current grammar allow it?

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes explaining the French and Japanese idioms to a friend
who has never heard them. For one expression, propose a word swap and a tense
change. Which do you expect to keep the idiom's meaning? Mark what you would
need to check with speakers. Then sort *strong tea* and *It is cold here*:
preferred wording, a learned surprising meaning, or a clue from the situation?

<details>
<summary>🤓 Can grammar itself have a surprising whole meaning? (optional)</summary>

WALS chapter 115 discusses Chalcatongo Mixtec expressions involving negative
indefinites. Haspelmath considers them non-compositional, but explicitly says
more information is needed to confirm this. It is a possible grammatical
example, not just a proverb, and not a settled claim. Familiar-looking negative
pieces are not enough to predict the whole. The analysis also depends on which
word meanings and construction rules we assume. A special meaning in one
construction need not make ordinary composition useless everywhere else.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. Usually, word meanings and their arrangement let us build the whole meaning.
2. An idiom may need a learned whole-expression meaning, but it still has grammar.
3. Preferred word pairs, metaphors, ambiguity, and clues from context are different puzzles.

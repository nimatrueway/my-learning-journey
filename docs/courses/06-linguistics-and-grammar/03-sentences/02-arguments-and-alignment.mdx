"The window was broken by the child."

The window comes first. Did it break itself? No. Blaming the first noun would
already get us into trouble, before we even leave English. Let's follow the
participants without falsely accusing the window.

⏱️ **Reading + practice: ~10 min** · includes 5 minutes of practice; folds are optional extra.

## English: she sleeps, but someone sees her

| English example | Form for the same female participant |
| --- | --- |
| She slept. | *she* |
| She saw him. | *she* |
| He saw her. | *her* |

Sleeping alone and doing the seeing both use *she*. Being seen uses *her*.
The form helps us track the participant's job. But *the child* stays the
same in these positions. Even English does not give every noun the same wardrobe.

## Hunzib groups participants another way

These two examples are published in WALS, from a grammar of Hunzib, a language
of the eastern Caucasus:

| Hunzib | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *kid y-ut'-ur* | The girl slept. |
| *oždi-l kid hehe-r* | The boy hit the girl. |

Here *kid* means "girl"; it is not the English word *kid*. The girl's word
stays plain whether she sleeps or is affected by someone else's action.
The boy's form carries `-l` when he does the action in the second example.
That is a different grouping from English *she/her*. Same tracking problem,
different grammar solution.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The labels: roles, subjects, objects, S, A, and P</summary>

In "the child broke the window," the child starts the action and the window
is affected. The usual role names are **agent** and **patient**. Other stories
have someone feeling, receiving, or knowing something; not every verb tells
us about a physical action.

**Subject** and **object** name jobs in the grammar. We identify them from
the language's patterns, not just the story. In our passive sentence, the
affected window gets the subject job.

Core participants linked to a predicate are **arguments**. Extra information,
such as a time or place added to an event, can be an **adjunct**. For comparing
basic participant-marking patterns, use three labels:

- **S:** the only core participant in "the child slept": the child.
- **A:** the doer-like participant in "the child broke the window": the child.
- **P:** the affected participant in that sentence: the window. Some books call it **O**.

The sleep example is **intransitive**; the break example is **transitive**.
These labels help compare patterns without pretending English's subject rules
are the rules of the world.

</details>

<details>
<summary>🤓 Accusative versus ergative, with the real Hunzib gloss</summary>

Compare *she slept*, *she saw him*, and *he saw her*. English uses *she* for
S and A, but *her* for P here. Grouping S with A is **nominative–accusative
alignment**. Full nouns work differently: *the child* keeps the same form.
So even English needs more than one little table.

Now imagine grouping S with P instead, and marking A differently. That is
**ergative–absolutive alignment**. Basque and many Caucasian and Australian
languages offer real cases to study. The relevant A case is called **ergative**;
the S/P case is **absolutive**. "Ergative" does not mean past tense or the subject
of every verb. Check which construction uses it.

The Hunzib forms above have this gloss in WALS:

```text
kid  y-ut'-ur
girl CL2-sleep-PST
'The girl slept.'

oždi-l  kid  hehe-r
boy-ERG girl hit-PST
'The boy hit the girl.'
```

The girl as S and P has no overt case suffix here; the boy as A has the
ergative `-l`. `CL2` labels noun-class agreement on the sleep verb. Case
marking, agreement, and syntax are separate comparisons: these examples do
not establish that they all use exactly the same grouping.

</details>

## Latvian: the endings help too

Two more WALS examples: *Putn-s lidoja*, "The bird was flying," and
*Bērn-s zīmē sun-i*, "The child is drawing a dog."

Notice `-s` on the flying bird and the drawing child, versus `-i` on the dog
being drawn. This is another real way to mark participant jobs. Do not assume
English pronouns are the only way a language can make the distinction.

## Spanish adds a small marker before Juan

Compare the published pair *María vio a Juan*, "Mary saw John," and
*María vio el libro*, "Mary saw the book." The person gets *a* in this
comparison; the book does not. Which objects get marked is another question
worth testing, not something we can settle by counting nouns alone.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Split alignment, object marking, and agreement</summary>

Check noun-case forms, participant markers on verbs, and sentence behavior
separately. They need not group S, A, and P in exactly the same way. The pattern
can also change with tense, aspect, person, whether something is living, or
clause type. That is **split alignment**. Hindi's perfective constructions
show why aspect and object-marking conditions matter together.

Objects themselves may get different marking depending on what they refer to.
This is **differential object marking**. Spanish personal `a` and Persian `rā`
are good comparison stops, but they do not follow identical rules.

**Agreement** or **indexing** can put participant information on the predicate.
Case forms, these verb markers, and word order can work together. A language
does not have to choose just one way of telling us who did what.

</details>

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes circling the participant forms in the English, Hunzib,
and Latvian examples. Which jobs get matching forms? Write the grouping in
ordinary words first. Then open a technical fold to add the label. Finally,
explain why starting the window sentence does not make the window the doer.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Other alignments and measurement scope (optional)</summary>

Some systems distinguish all three labels: **tripartite**. Others split the
S participants into different groups, often according to the predicate's
meaning: **active–inactive**. **Neutral** marking does not distinguish the
three in the feature we're checking. WALS separates noun case, pronoun case,
and verbal marking. Read its counting rules before treating one map color
as the language's behavior in every situation.

</details>

## Recap 🐾

1. A participant's role in the story is not always its job in the grammar.
2. English *she/her* and Hunzib's plain *kid* show different participant groupings.
3. Check nouns, pronouns, verb markers, and clause types: their patterns can differ.

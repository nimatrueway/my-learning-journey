## Separate meaning roles from grammatical relations

An **argument** is a participant selected by a predicate in an analysis.
An **adjunct** adds information such as time or location without the same
selection relationship. **Agent** describes a prototypical initiator;
**patient** describes a prototypically affected participant. Experiencers,
recipients, and other roles show that not every predicate is a physical action.
**Subject** and **object** are grammatical relations diagnosed through a
language's patterns, not direct synonyms for agent and patient.

To compare basic alignment, use three role labels:

- **S:** the sole core argument of a canonical intransitive predicate, as in "the child slept."
- **A:** the more agent-like argument of a canonical transitive predicate, as in "the child broke the window."
- **P:** the more patient-like argument of that transitive predicate, the window. Some books use **O** instead.

These labels are comparison tools. They let us ask which arguments receive
the same treatment without first assuming English's subject behavior everywhere.

## Two familiar grouping patterns

**Nominative–accusative alignment** groups S with A and treats P differently.
English pronouns illustrate it: *she slept*, *she saw him*, *he saw her*.
*She* can be S or A, whereas *her* is the object form here. English full noun
phrases do not show the same overt case contrast: *the child* keeps its form.

**Ergative–absolutive alignment** groups S with P and treats A differently.
Basque and many Caucasian and Australian languages provide important cases.
The **ergative** case marks A in the relevant construction; the **absolutive**
marks S/P. Ergative does not mean "past tense," "aggressive," or "the subject
of every verb." Its distribution is an empirical question.

The following is a **constructed case-marking system**, not Basque or Warlpiri:

```text
dara       sleep
child.ABS  sleep
'The child sleeps.'

dara-ka    pano        see
child-ERG  bird.ABS    see
'The child sees the bird.'
```

The child as S has the same unmarked form as the bird as P. Only A receives
`-ka`. `.ABS` labels a case analysis of the unsegmented form; it does not
introduce an overt suffix. The English translation does not determine the case system.

## A language can align differently in different places

Compare **case marking on noun phrases**, **person indexing on verbs**, and
**syntactic behavior**, such as which arguments can be omitted across coordinated
clauses. A language can be ergative in case without having identical syntactic
alignment. **Split alignment** can depend on tense/aspect, person, animacy,
or clause type. Hindi's perfective constructions illustrate why aspect and
object-marking conditions matter; a single blanket label misses their interaction.

**Differential object marking** means objects are marked differently depending
on factors such as animacy, definiteness, or specificity. Spanish's personal
`a` and Persian `rā` are useful comparison cases, but their distributions
are not identical. **Agreement** or **indexing** can express participant
features on a predicate. Case, indexing, and word order can cooperate rather
than competing to be a language's only way to identify participants.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Spend five minutes labeling S, A, and P in the two constructed clauses. Predict
the case forms for "The bird sleeps" and "The bird sees the child." Keeping
the same roots, the predictions are `pano sleep` and `pano-ka dara see`.
Then explain why the passive window example prevents equating subject with agent.

of the predicate. Neutral marking does not distinguish the three by the feature

## Recap 🐾

1. Semantic roles and grammatical relations must be distinguished before comparing arguments.
2. Accusative groups S/A; ergative groups S/P, for a specified marking or behavior.
3. Pronouns, nouns, agreement, and clause types can reveal different or split alignments.

# Part XIV: Why First Tries Feel Easier for Some People

⏱️ **~12 min** · folds are optional extra

Two people face the same unfamiliar task. One says, “Interesting, let me poke
at it.” The other wants three more courses, a guarantee, and perhaps a signed
note from the future.

This difference is real, but it is not a division between brave people and
weak people. People bring different nervous systems, histories, skills,
resources, and interpretations to the unknown. More importantly, uncertainty
tolerance is not fixed. It can be trained through small attempts that teach:

> I do not need to know how this ends. I need a useful next move and a way to
> recover.

## Why Some People Tolerate Uncertainty Better

There is no single “uncertainty gene” or personality type. Several influences
stack together:

- **Temperament and current state.** People differ in threat sensitivity, and
  anyone becomes less flexible when sleep-deprived, chronically stressed,
  depressed, or already overloaded. The same person may enjoy uncertainty on
  Saturday and hate it during a bad month.
- **Learning history.** If early mistakes brought humiliation, punishment, or
  chaotic consequences, ambiguity can predict danger. If experiments were met
  with guidance and repair, ambiguity is more likely to predict learning.
- **Prior mastery.** Experience teaches recurring structures. An expert still
  lacks the answer, but knows how to inspect the problem, find feedback, and
  recover. What looks like fearlessness is often a large library of recovery
  scripts.
- **Identity stakes.** A first try is heavier when its result is expected to
  reveal intelligence, worth, belonging, or future potential. The same task is
  lighter when it is allowed to be one sample from a learning process.
- **Resources and reversibility.** Time, money, health, social support, and
  second chances change the actual risk. Some apparent boldness is supported
  by a safety net. Do not turn unequal downside into a character comparison.
- **Culture and incentives.** Families, teams, and institutions teach whether
  questions are welcomed, imperfect drafts are normal, and bad news can be
  surfaced without punishment.

So “they are naturally confident” is usually an incomplete explanation. They
may have lower threat sensitivity, more hidden reps, a safer environment, more
slack, or a better procedure for what happens next.

<details>
<summary>🧠 Intolerance of uncertainty (optional)</summary>

Psychology uses **intolerance of uncertainty** for a tendency to react
negatively to the possibility that important information is missing. It is
associated with worry and anxiety across several conditions, but it is not a
diagnosis by itself. The problem is not simply preferring clarity. It is
treating “I cannot know yet” as unacceptable, dangerous, or impossible to
handle.

Anxiety can then recruit reassurance seeking, checking, overplanning,
procrastination, or avoidance. These reduce distress briefly, which teaches the
brain that escaping uncertainty kept it safe. The relief reinforces the loop.
Gradual exposure works in the other direction: remain with a manageable unknown
long enough to act and discover that uncertainty can be carried without first
being eliminated.

</details>

## Why First Tries Are Easier for Some People

### They expect a probe, not a performance

A first attempt cannot both discover the terrain and demonstrate polished
mastery. People who begin readily tend to assign it the first job: reveal the
shape of the problem. People who freeze often assign it the second: prove that
they were capable all along.

Use the distinction from
[Part II](/courses/performance-foundations/part-2-acting-despite-fear): the work
is an experiment and the result is information. “Can I make one request reach
the API?” is startable. “Can I prove this entire product deserves to exist?” is
an identity referendum wearing a task's clothing.

### They trust recovery more than prediction

Confidence is often described as expecting success. A more durable version is
**recovery confidence**:

> If this goes badly, I can regulate the hit, inspect what happened, ask for
> help, change the plan, and take another step.

You rarely control whether the attempt works. You can strengthen what happens
after it does not. This reduces the demand for a guarantee before action.

### They have hidden repetitions

The colleague who “just tries things” may have made fifty adjacent attempts.
They recognize which parts are reversible, where errors appear, whom to ask,
and when to stop. Their first try in this exact situation is not their first try
at being confused.

Do not compare your first visible rep with somebody else's accumulated process.
Borrow the process instead: small scope, fast feedback, specific questions,
and a review after the attempt.

### They do not wait for discomfort to leave

Readiness is frequently inferred from emotion: “If I still feel anxious, I must
need more preparation.” But anxiety is compatible with adequate preparation.
The practical question is not “Do I feel certain?” It is “Is the next step
bounded enough to take responsibly?”

That is [micro-exposure](/courses/performance-foundations/core-techniques#micro-exposure):
act with uncertainty present, then let the outcome update the prediction.

## The Mental Tools

### 1. Split risk from uncertainty

**Risk** has identifiable outcomes whose likelihood can be estimated.
**Uncertainty** means important outcomes or probabilities are not yet known.
Use the right response:

| Situation | Useful response |
| --- | --- |
| Known downside, estimable likelihood | Reduce exposure; compare expected costs; insure where sensible |
| Missing information that a small test can reveal | Run the cheapest informative probe |
| Missing information no test can reveal yet | Set a review date; act from values and current evidence |
| Severe or irreversible downside | Slow down; seek expertise; build safeguards before acting |

Calling everything “fear” can make a legitimate hazard sound psychological.
Calling everything “risk” can make discomfort sound like analysis. Name which
one you actually have.

### 2. Sort the decision by reversibility

For a **two-way-door decision**, mistakes are cheap to reverse: draft privately,
test with five users, try a workflow for one week. Move with partial
information and learn.

For a **one-way-door decision**, consequences are difficult to undo: a large
financial commitment, a medical choice, a public action with lasting effects.
Gather more evidence, consult qualified people, and install safeguards. The
lesson is not “always leap.” It is “spend caution where the downside deserves
it.”

### 3. Replace “What will happen?” with “What will this teach?”

Outcome questions demand prophecy. Learning questions produce experiments:

- Instead of “Will people like my work?” ask “Which part confuses three target
  readers?”
- Instead of “Am I good at public speaking?” ask “Can I deliver the opening
  clearly without rushing?”
- Instead of “Is this the right career?” ask “What does a ten-hour project
  reveal about my interest in the daily work?”

The question should be narrow enough that even an unwelcome answer has value.

### 4. Use a minimum viable first try

Reduce **scope**, not learning value. A useful first try has:

1. One question.
2. A time, money, or audience cap.
3. A visible action that can produce feedback.
4. A stopping rule.
5. A scheduled review.

Preparation that changes one of these fields may be useful. Preparation that
only postpones contact with reality is likely avoidance. Part IX's rule still
applies: [learn inside the build](/courses/performance-foundations/part-9-from-learning-to-doing).

### 5. Write the recovery plan before the prediction wins

Complete this sentence:

> If **[specific setback]** happens, then I will **[regulate]**, inspect
> **[evidence]**, ask **[person or source]** one specific question, and decide
> **[next step or stopping rule]** at **[time]**.

Example: “If the demo fails, I will take ten minutes, save the error output,
ask the engineer who owns the integration one narrow question, and choose a
retry or rollback by 3 p.m.”

This is not pessimism. It transfers confidence from an unknowable result to a
rehearsable response.

### 6. Keep a decision journal

Before a meaningful attempt, record:

- What you know and what remains unknown.
- Your rough prediction and the evidence behind it.
- The decision rule you used.
- What would change your mind.

Afterward, record what happened and what the attempt taught. This prevents
**hindsight bias**, the mind's habit of pretending an outcome was obvious once
it is known. It also builds an honest library of first tries: failed outcomes
can coexist with sound decisions, and lucky outcomes can coexist with reckless
ones.

### 7. Train with uncertainty on purpose

Choose small, safe unknowns and drop one reassurance ritual:

- Send a low-stakes message after one careful review instead of six.
- Begin a tutorial-sized build before understanding every dependency.
- Ask a question without first apologizing for it.
- Let someone else choose a minor plan without researching all alternatives.

Grade the rep on whether you carried the unknown and acted responsibly, not on
whether you felt calm. Increase difficulty gradually. Forcing a huge leap can
teach overwhelm; repeated manageable reps teach capacity.

<details>
<summary>🧠 The useful mechanisms (optional)</summary>

**Self-efficacy** is belief in one's capacity to organize and execute actions
needed for a situation. Albert Bandura identified mastery experiences as a
particularly strong source: successful coping on real tasks changes belief
more reliably than generic encouragement. Small attempts matter because they
produce credible evidence of action and recovery.

**Metacognitive approaches** distinguish a thought from the response to it.
“Something may go wrong” can remain present without triggering hours of worry
or checking. The target is not a perfectly optimistic forecast; it is flexible
control over attention and behavior.

**Exposure to uncertainty** reduces reliance on avoidance and reassurance. The
aim is not to prove that feared outcomes never occur. It is to learn that not
knowing is tolerable, action remains possible, and setbacks can be handled.

**Psychological flexibility** describes staying in contact with present
experience while choosing behavior guided by values. In plain language: fear
gets a seat, not the steering wheel.

</details>

## When the Tool Is Not “Push Through”

Uncertainty tolerance is not a command to ignore danger, suppress emotion, or
make every bet. Pause and seek appropriate support when the downside includes
safety, health, legal exposure, essential finances, or harm to others. Ask for
qualified expertise when the uncertainty exceeds your competence.

Persistent worry, compulsive checking, repeated reassurance seeking, panic, or
avoidance that substantially limits work or life may deserve help from a
mental-health professional. Evidence-based therapies can target intolerance of
uncertainty and anxiety directly. That is not failing the exercise; it is using
the right tool for the scale of the problem.

## Try It Yourself: Five First-Try Reps

For one week, choose five low-cost tasks you have delayed because you do not
know how they will go.

1. Write the genuine unknown in one sentence.
2. Mark the decision as reversible or difficult to reverse.
3. For a reversible choice, define a 15-to-60-minute probe. For a difficult
   one, define the next evidence-gathering step instead.
4. Write one recovery move and a stopping rule.
5. Act before seeking additional reassurance.
6. Log what happened in your
   [Evidence File](/courses/performance-foundations/core-techniques#evidence-file),
   including evidence that you recovered, adapted, or asked effectively.

At the end of the week, do not ask, “How many worked?” Ask, “How many unknowns
did I convert into information without making the result a verdict on me?”

## Three Things to Keep

- Differences in uncertainty tolerance reflect temperament, state, history, skill, identity stakes, resources, and environment, not a simple courage ranking.
- People who start readily often trust a bounded process and their ability to recover more than they trust a positive prediction.
- Name the unknown, classify reversibility, run the cheapest informative probe, and preselect the recovery move.

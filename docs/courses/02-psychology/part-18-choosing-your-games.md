# Part XVIII: Choosing Which Games to Play

A thread about naming a folder consumes an afternoon. Nobody's pay or safety
depends on the name, yet backing down feels strangely expensive. Meanwhile,
the project that might help actual users has not moved.

⏱️ **~12 min** · includes 5 minutes of rehearsal and practice; folds are extra

Why can something small become so competitive? And how do you leave without
merely trading "I must win this argument" for "I must build something enormous
to prove I matter"?

## Small practical stakes, large psychological stakes

"When the stakes are low, competition is savage" is a provocative observation,
not a general law. High-stakes competition can also be fierce; many low-stakes
activities are friendly or cooperative.

One useful distinction is between **practical stakes** and **felt stakes**:

| Practical question | Possible felt question |
| --- | --- |
| Which folder name helps people find things? | Will people think my judgment matters? |
| Who gets credit for one minor suggestion? | Am I respected here? |
| Who wins a casual game? | Am I falling behind someone like me? |

The felt question can make a small decision emotionally expensive. That does
not prove everyone involved is insecure. Sometimes a seemingly minor detail
really does affect access, fairness, usability, or accumulated recognition.
Investigate before calling something petty.

## Why these contests can become sticky

Research on competition and social comparison emphasizes that intensity
depends on more than the material prize. Relevant factors include whether
the ability matters to your identity, how similar or close the rival is, and
where you stand relative to a meaningful rank or standard.

An original illustration: a stranger publishes an excellent article and you
enjoy it. A colleague with your role publishes one and you feel behind. The
article's objective value did not change; the comparison became personally
relevant. This illustrates the framework, not a prediction about everyone.

Other possible loops are worth testing in your own situation:

- **Visible score:** replies or likes make winning easy to notice while useful
  long-term work gives slower feedback.
- **Immediate relief:** defending yourself can briefly quiet the feeling of
  being dismissed, encouraging another reply.
- **Investment:** "I already spent an hour" becomes a reason to spend another,
  even though the earlier hour cannot be recovered.

These are candidate explanations, not a diagnosis of a colleague or an
excuse to dismiss feedback. Ask which pattern the evidence actually supports.

Now change one fact: the proposed name exposes private client information.
The same "just leave" response no longer fits. Safety and confidentiality need
an appropriate correction or escalation, not a lesson in being above it all.

## A four-question stakes check

Before your next reply, ask:

1. **Consequence:** What changes for people, money, safety, rights, or work
   quality if nobody hears from me again?
2. **Actionability:** Is there a specific error to fix, decision to make, or
   boundary to enforce?
3. **Marginal value:** Will this next action add evidence or protection, or
   repeat what is already on record?
4. **Displacement:** What am I not doing while I stay in this exchange?

This is a decision aid, not a validated psychological test. Use proportionate
responses: one clarification for a misunderstanding, an agreed decision-maker
for a stalled work choice, documentation and appropriate support for repeated
exclusion or harassment.

Do not use "low stakes" to dismiss another person's concern. Repeated small
credit losses can affect a career; a small charge can matter on a tight
budget. Ask about consequences rather than assigning importance from outside.

For online boundaries, use the existing
[Online Hate and Annoying Replies](/courses/performance-foundations/part-15-online-hate-and-annoying-replies)
lesson. Here the added skill is choosing where your effort goes next.

## Replace the contest with a contribution

"Do something big" becomes useful when it means **create an effect you value**,
not **become too impressive to feel small**. Bigger projects still involve
competition, boring work, disagreement, and uncertain outcomes. There is no
arena where insecurity automatically disappears.

Define the alternative with three concrete parts:

- **Who benefits?** Name a person or group, not an imaginary audience applauding.
- **What improves?** Describe an observable change in their situation.
- **What is the smallest honest test?** Choose an action that can produce
  feedback, not just a grand announcement.

For example: "Help two teammates answer a recurring support question using a
one-page guide, then check whether they can use it without me." That is not
a world-changing claim. It is a real effect you can test. Meaningful work can
grow from small evidence; small does not mean petty.

**Petty describes a poor use of effort relative to consequences, not a small
task or an unimportant person.** Caring for a baby, maintaining a home, and
doing reliable work can be deeply consequential without public prestige.

## Tiny example: give the afternoon a different destination

In this fictional example, Alex has written three replies defending a minor
design preference. They notice the next draft adds no new information.
Alex posts: "My concern is findability; either option works if search finds
both. I'll follow the team's decision."

Alex then uses twenty available minutes to test whether a teammate can find
the document. The result may support either name. The change is from defending
a preferred answer to investigating the actual problem.

Alex might still feel annoyed. Leaving is a behavior you can choose before
the desire to win disappears. If the team repeatedly denies Alex fair input,
that is a separate pattern to address, not something a personal mantra fixes.

## Do not turn ambition into a second exhaustion machine

If ordinary life already feels like an endless shift, adding a startup may
be a fantasy of freedom rather than an affordable next step. A business can
add obligations, financial uncertainty, and people depending on you.

Use [the responsibility-overload lesson](/courses/performance-foundations/part-17-responsibility-overload)
first when recovery is the bottleneck. Then choose a **bounded** contribution:
one small test within genuinely available time, with no essential care or
sleep displaced. If there is no capacity this week, recovery is a legitimate
choice. You do not have to earn relief by producing something exceptional.

For longer-term direction and standards beyond applause, continue with
[Build an Internal Compass](/courses/performance-foundations/part-13-internal-compass).
For actually shipping rather than endlessly preparing, use
[From Learning to Doing](/courses/performance-foundations/part-9-from-learning-to-doing).

## 🧪 Try it: exit one loop, name one useful effect

Spend about four minutes writing, plus one minute on the earlier rehearsal:

1. Choose one recurring contest. Write its practical consequence and its
   felt meaning separately.
2. Decide: clarify, protect, cooperate, or leave. Write the next action in
   one sentence.
3. Name what the contest displaces. Include rest or family time if that is
   the honest answer.
4. If capacity exists, define one beneficiary, one useful effect, and one
   test you can fit into an existing free block.
5. Set a stopping point. Review evidence afterward, not whether you finally
   felt more important than someone else.

Doing the project itself is additional time, not part of this reading badge.

<details>
<summary>🧠 What the competition evidence does and does not say (optional)</summary>

Garcia, Tor, and Schiff's 2013 review organizes competition through a social
comparison framework. It distinguishes individual factors, such as relevance
and similarity, from situational factors, such as proximity to a rank
standard and the number of competitors.

This supports asking why a particular comparison becomes salient. It does
not establish an inverse law between prize size and hostility, nor prove
the motive behind a specific argument.

"Bikeshedding" is a common informal label for spending disproportionate time
on an accessible minor detail while harder decisions receive less attention.
Treat it as a prompt to check priorities, not a scientific diagnosis or
permission to disregard necessary implementation details.

The stakes check and contribution test are original teaching heuristics.
They are not experimentally validated as a combined intervention.

</details>

## 🐾 Three things to keep

- A small practical prize can carry large felt stakes, but low stakes do not
  universally cause fierce competition.
- Choose your next response by consequences, new information, protection,
  and opportunity cost, not by the need to look victorious.
- Redirect effort toward a concrete useful effect; meaningful ambition must
  fit real capacity and does not outrank care or recovery.

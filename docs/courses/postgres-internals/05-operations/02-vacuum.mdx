## Cleanup waits for visibility horizons

Updates and deletes leave obsolete tuple versions. Vacuum can remove versions
no relevant transaction still needs, clean index entries, maintain free-space
and visibility information, and freeze sufficiently old transaction metadata.
Page pruning can reclaim some local space earlier; it does not replace all of
vacuum's duties. ANALYZE updates planner statistics and is a distinct operation,
though VACUUM ANALYZE requests both.

A long-lived snapshot can retain old versions even when ordinary writes continue.
Prepared transactions, replication slots, and standby feedback can also affect
retention horizons. Inspect the actual horizon holder before declaring vacuum
broken. A low dead-tuple estimate is not a guarantee of healthy XID age, and a
large table is not by itself proof of pathological bloat.

Plain VACUUM generally makes space reusable in the relation. It may truncate
empty tail pages under suitable conditions, but does not generally compact all
live rows and return every internal hole to the OS. VACUUM FULL rewrites the
table, requires extra working space and an AccessExclusive lock, and is not
the routine cure for normal steady-state churn. Plan disruptive rewrites explicitly.

## Wraparound is about age, not calendar time

Raw tuple XIDs have 32 bits. PostgreSQL interprets their order with wraparound-aware
rules, so sufficiently old unfrozen references cannot be kept forever. Freezing
records that old committed tuple origins can be treated as ancient without
depending on an ambiguous old XID. Modern PostgreSQL can mark a tuple frozen in
flags without physically replacing its visible xmin value with one magic number.

Monitor transaction age and maintenance progress, not just days since the last
vacuum. A high-write-rate database consumes XIDs much faster than a quiet one.
MultiXact IDs have their own age/maintenance concerns. Anti-wraparound vacuum
exists to protect correctness; disabling autovacuum is not an acceptable response
to a busy maintenance process. Extreme risk can cause PostgreSQL to refuse work
to avoid data loss.

```sql
SELECT datname, age(datfrozenxid) AS xid_age
FROM pg_database ORDER BY xid_age DESC;
SELECT relname, n_live_tup, n_dead_tup, last_autovacuum,
       last_autoanalyze, n_tup_upd, n_tup_hot_upd
FROM pg_stat_user_tables WHERE schemaname = 'course_lab';
VACUUM (VERBOSE, ANALYZE) course_lab.products;
SELECT * FROM pg_stat_progress_vacuum;
```

Statistics are estimates and cumulative counters with update/reset behavior.
The progress query can be empty because the tiny vacuum has already finished;
empty does not mean it failed. Execute VACUUM outside an explicit transaction.
The lab must not attempt to consume billions of XIDs to "test" wraparound.

## Try it yourself 🧪

Keep a Repeatable Read snapshot in A after selecting product 1. Update the
product in B and commit. Run VERBOSE vacuum in B, then confirm A still sees
its original version. End A and vacuum again. Record what changed without
expecting a particular table-size reduction from two rows. Restore stock to 10.
Then inspect database XID ages and explain why those values cannot be converted
to an exact number of safe days without a workload-rate estimate.

## Recap 🐾

1. Vacuum reclaims only versions outside all relevant retention horizons and maintains more than free space.
2. Reusable internal space and a smaller filesystem allocation are different outcomes.
3. Freezing and XID/MultiXact age are correctness requirements, not optional cosmetic cleanup.

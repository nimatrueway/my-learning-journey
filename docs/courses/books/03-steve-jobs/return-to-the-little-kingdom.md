# Return to the Little Kingdom: How Apple and Steve Jobs Changed the World

Michael Moritz

## Prologue

Michael Moritz opens by recalling the collision course that first brought him into Steve Jobs's orbit: a 1982 Time magazine profile of Jobs, mangled by an editor in New York, that left both journalist and subject furious. Jobs banished him from Apple, and Moritz finished his leave to write the original edition of this book, The Little Kingdom, before moving on to become, in his words, "half the entire workforce" of a small publishing venture, and later a partner at Sequoia Capital, the firm that had backed Apple in its earliest days.

Looking back after twenty-five years investing in young companies, Moritz reflects that Jobs's true distinction was never his title of CEO but his identity as a founder — "there is no greater distance known to man than the single footfall that separates a CEO from a founder." He describes Jobs as willful, obdurate, and mesmerizing, a man who turned modern electronics into objects of desire, and who is perhaps unique in having founded Apple twice: once in a garage with Wozniak, and once again, alone, after returning from the wilderness of NeXT to rescue the company that had fired him. Moritz closes by pointing readers toward Jobs's 2005 Stanford commencement address and its parting exhortation, borrowed from the Whole Earth Catalog: "Stay hungry. Stay foolish."

## Introduction

Moritz explains why he chose to chronicle Apple Computer while it was still young enough for the truth to be recoverable: companies, like people, put their best foot forward, but by the time official histories are commissioned, myth has usually replaced memory. Apple was Silicon Valley's most precocious child, rocketing from a living room to a billion dollars in sales faster than any company in Fortune 500 history, minting more than a hundred millionaires along the way. He wanted to know whether the folksy publicity surrounding Silicon Valley — relaxed offices, abolished hierarchy, egalitarian founders — matched the private reality, and to examine the years before Apple went public, when its founders' personalities still shaped the company directly.

The chapter then drops into a present-day scene from 1982: a two-day retreat for the Macintosh division at a windswept Pacific resort, where Steve Jobs, barefoot and cross-legged on a table, delivers his gospel to a room of young engineers and marketers. "It's Not Done Until It Ships," he tells them, unveiling a mock-up of a future Mac and predicting the team will one day look back and call these "the good old days." He needles a colleague — "Can we ship your party?" — jokes about besting rival Adam Osborne, and listens to venture capitalist Ben Rosen warn that IBM, not any small rival, is the real threat looming over Apple's future. The retreat, with its stock-option talk, factory tours, and Kool-Aid enthusiasm, captures Apple at the hinge between its garage-born informality and the corporate machine it was fast becoming.

## Boomtown by the Bay

Before it was Silicon Valley, the stretch of towns between San Jose and San Francisco was still largely orchards and quiet Main Streets, each town jealously distinct from its neighbors, each with its own snobberies. Sunnyvale changed fastest of all once Lockheed's Missile Systems Division arrived, growing sixfold after the war and boasting that a new worker showed up every sixteen minutes — "Boomtown by the Bay," its Chamber of Commerce liked to say. Families flooded in chasing Lockheed jobs, and the sharper among them studied maps of the quirky, non-contiguous Cupertino School District, whose boundaries wandered far outside Cupertino itself and made certain houses sell at a premium.

Among the arrivals was Jerry Wozniak, a Cal Tech-trained engineer who settled his family, including a son named Stephen, in a Sunnyvale subdivision that happened to fall within that prized school district. Meanwhile, at the opposite end of the Peninsula, Paul and Clara Jobs adopted their first child, a boy they named Steven, and wheeled him in his pram past the fog and gray gulls of San Francisco's Sunset District. Neither family could have guessed that this administrative accident of a school district boundary would one day put their two sons, born of such different circumstances, on the same street.

## Super Secret Sky Spies

Lockheed's Sunnyvale missile division turned the sleepy suburb into a company town wrapped in Cold War mystique — rumors of spy satellites, secret Polaris missiles, and a computer supposedly clever enough to play tic-tac-toe. Jerry Wozniak, a former Cal Tech tailback, spent his days on classified projects and his evenings coaching Little League and playing golf, while his wife Margaret, a wartime shipyard electrician turned Republican Party organizer, kept the household disciplined. Their son Stephen showed an early, almost allergic susceptibility to electronics: assembling a voltmeter kit in fifth grade, earning a ham radio license in sixth, and even helping stage a photo op for Richard Nixon's 1962 campaign as the only licensed ham operator at his school.

Wozniak's obsession deepened through science fairs. After building a simple electronic tic-tac-toe game with his father's help, he progressed to a homemade "Ten Bit Parallel Adder-Subtracter," a hand-wired arithmetic unit built from a magazine diagram, which won prizes at both the school and regional science fairs — a Bay Area Science Fair third-place finish that earned him his first-ever airplane ride. One prank went further than intended: rigging a fake bomb with an oscillator in a friend's locker, which sent the school principal sprinting for the football field before Wozniak spent a night in Juvenile Hall, returning to a standing ovation from his classmates. The chapter closes on a boy already fluent in a language almost no one around him could read.

## Carburetors and Microphones

Steven Jobs's early childhood moved with his father's fortunes: a machinist and part-time car-flipper, Paul Jobs shuttled the family from South San Francisco to Mountain View chasing shorter commutes and better schools. Steven was a difficult, curious child — he once jammed a bobby pin into an outlet and, on another occasion, had his stomach pumped after mixing ant poison in a homemade chemistry set. A neighbor's carbon microphone, which produced sound with no visible amplifier, baffled and fascinated him enough that he pestered the Hewlett-Packard engineer next door until he understood how it worked, a formative lesson in refusing to accept a mystery at face value.

Where his father found meaning restoring old cars in the driveway, young Steven found none in carburetors, and instead grew curious about the previous owners of each vehicle. Financial setbacks — a failed stint in real estate that forced Paul Jobs to refinance the house — left their mark; asked by a teacher what he didn't understand, nine-year-old Steven answered, "I don't understand why all of a sudden we're so broke." A sympathetic teacher, Imogene Hill, bribed him with five dollars to finish a workbook and got him skipped a grade, but by sixth grade he was miserable enough at Mountain View's rough Crittenden School that he gave his father an ultimatum: move, or he wouldn't go back. The family relocated once more, this time to Los Altos, landing inside the same coveted Cupertino School District that had drawn the Wozniaks a few years earlier.

## The Cream Soda Computer

At Cupertino's newly opened Homestead High School, electronics teacher John McCollum scavenged discarded parts from Fairchild, Raytheon, and Hewlett-Packard to stock a classroom that became, in effect, a private laboratory for a handful of obsessive students. Wozniak thrived there, absorbing Ohm's law and circuit theory while also indulging his taste for pranks — famously planting a fake bomb, and building a device that could disrupt his college dormitory's television reception just to torment professors giving lectures over closed-circuit TV. McCollum arranged for Wozniak and his younger friend Allen Baum to spend a school year visiting a nearby company, GTE Sylvania, where they got their first hands-on exposure to a real mainframe, the IBM 1130, learning FORTRAN and discovering, to Wozniak's own surprise, that a "compiler" was software rather than a piece of hardware.

The pair devoured minicomputer manuals mailed in response to trade-magazine reader cards, developing connoisseurs' opinions about which machines — the Data General Nova above all — were elegantly designed and which were clumsy. After a disastrous freshman year at the University of Colorado, where Wozniak ran up a stack of Fs and jammed dormitory televisions for sport, he returned home to Cupertino's De Anza Community College and resumed his correspondence course in machine design, eventually landing a summer programming job at a small computer company called Tenet.

The culmination of all this self-education was the Cream Soda Computer, built in 1970 with his neighbor Bill Fernandez in a corner of the Fernandez family garage, squeezed between the water heater and the clothes dryer. Scavenging chips donated by sympathetic engineers at Intel, Intersil, and Signetics, and fueled by an epic quantity of Cragmont cream soda (whose bottle deposits helped fund the last few parts), Wozniak designed the logic while Fernandez handled the timing circuits and patient, wire-by-wire assembly. The finished machine could multiply two four-bit numbers entered on switches and display the answer in blinking lights — a modest feat that meant everything to its builders. It was in that garage that Fernandez first introduced Wozniak to a quiet, watchful younger boy named Steven Jobs, who came away impressed: "He was the first person I met who knew more electronics than I did." The machine's public unveiling for a local newspaper ended in anticlimax when a faulty power supply chip, its origin traced to a neighbor's gardening favor, blew out every integrated circuit on the board just as the photographer arrived.

## The Conductor

Jobs, Wozniak, and Fernandez each nursed a private, introverted streak that made them seem odd to their peers but recognizable to one another. Fernandez had known Jobs since junior high, when classmates thought him strange; the two tinkered together with laser parts scavenged from Paul Jobs's employer, and Jobs took his own turn through John McCollum's electronics class and science fairs, though he never became a favorite the way Wozniak had. A cold call to Bill Hewlett himself landed teenage Jobs a summer job on a Hewlett-Packard assembly line, and he spent hours as a store clerk absorbing the going rates for parts at surplus emporiums like Haltek, a cavernous former airship hangar that felt, to one regular, "like walking around an immense tool kit."

But electronics was only one current in Jobs's adolescence. He fell for literature and film, played trumpet in the marching band, and co-founded a prankish "Buck Fry Club" with Wozniak and Baum that once hoisted a Volkswagen onto the school roof. He also met his first serious girlfriend, Nancy Rogers, whose parents' marriage was collapsing and who found in Jobs someone equally adrift. Together they skipped class, dropped acid in a wheat field — an experience Jobs later described in almost musical terms, as though he were "the conductor of this symphony with Bach coming through the wheat field" — and spent a bohemian post-graduation summer living together in a rented cabin, over his father's futile objection. To make ends meet the pair, along with Wozniak, took jobs in fuzzy costumes at a shopping-center fairyland, an image of ordinary teenage life that belied how differently all their paths were about to diverge.

## The Little Blue Box

A 1971 Esquire exposé on phone phreaking, handed to Wozniak by his own mother, set off a four-month obsession. Wozniak and Jobs scoured technical libraries for surviving telephone-company manuals, taught themselves the multi-frequency tones that controlled the long-distance network, and after failing with an analog oscillator, built a reliable digital "blue box" that could trick switching equipment into placing free calls anywhere in the world. Their first successful call, meant for Wozniak's grandmother, dialed a wrong number instead, leaving a bewildered stranger to hear Wozniak shouting in triumph anyway.

Determined to meet the legendary phone phreak Captain Crunch, they eventually tracked him to a Cupertino radio station and invited him to Wozniak's Berkeley dorm room, where John Draper — disheveled, missing teeth, utterly unlike the myth — spent an evening teaching them to "stack tandems," bouncing a call through switching stations across the country until it looped back to ring a phone across the hallway. Not long afterward, using the box from a roadside phone booth, the pair were stopped by police; Wozniak coolly explained the device away as "a music synthesizer" until the officers, satisfied there were no drugs involved, let them go — one remarking, "Too bad. A guy named Moog beat you to it."

What began as a private thrill became, at Jobs's urging, a small business: the two went door to door in Berkeley dormitories asking coyly for "George, the blue box guy," selling roughly two hundred units for about \$150 apiece and splitting some \$6,000 in profit. The venture had real risks. Draper himself was eventually convicted on wire-fraud charges, and Jobs once found himself staring down a customer's gun in a pizza-parlor parking lot before handing the box over. The blue box was never mass-produced or legal, but it taught its builders something durable: that a small, cleverly engineered device could humble one of the largest, most impregnable systems on earth — a lesson neither of them would forget once they turned to computers.

## Honey and Nuts

Jobs chose Reed College in Portland over Berkeley or Stanford, and his parents reluctantly drove him north, dropping him at a campus he wanted to face alone, "like an orphan from Kentucky." Reed was a hothouse of countercultural spirituality rather than politics, and Jobs fell in with Daniel Kottke, his closest friend, and Robert Friedland, an older, charismatic ex-convict and student-body president who introduced Jobs to Eastern philosophy and the Indian guru Neem Karolie Baba. Officially, Jobs dropped out after one semester, unable to justify the tuition his parents could barely afford, but he lingered on campus for another year and a half auditing whatever classes interested him — most consequentially a calligraphy course whose lessons in typefaces and spacing would surface years later in the Macintosh.

Money was perpetually short. Jobs subsisted largely on Roman Meal cereal, collected bottle deposits, and ate free Sunday dinners at the local Hare Krishna temple, all while pursuing an increasingly strict fruitarian diet inspired by the writings of Arnold Ehret, convinced that fasting and a mucus-free diet were paths to a kind of physical and spiritual clarity. Returning to California in 1974, he talked his way into a job at Atari after answering a classified ad, charming founder Nolan Bushnell's chaotic young company with sheer nerve despite having, by his own admission, no formal engineering training — "I wasn't really an engineer at all... I shone because everyone else was so bad."

Jobs then used Atari's money to fund the pilgrimage to India he had been dreaming of since meeting Friedland, traveling with Kottke to the Kumbh Mela festival, trekking to ashrams, and ultimately returning disillusioned by India's poverty and unimpressed by easy enlightenment: "Maybe Thomas Edison did a lot more to improve the world than Karl Marx and Neem Karolie Baba put together." A brief, equally disappointing stint at an Oregon primal-therapy retreat followed, driven partly by his need to make sense of having been given up for adoption. Back at Atari, Bushnell tasked Jobs with a bonus-driven challenge to minimize the chip count on a new game; unable to do the design himself, Jobs recruited Wozniak, who solved it brilliantly over four sleepless nights for a design that became Breakout — though Jobs quietly shortchanged his old friend on the truth about how much money Atari had actually paid.

## Buckets of Noise

On March 5, 1975, thirty-odd hobbyists answered a handbill and crowded into Gordon French's Menlo Park garage for the first meeting of what became the Homebrew Computer Club, organized by French and Fred Moore, two disenchanted veterans of the countercultural People's Computer Company. Moore, a lifelong advocate of alternative economics who believed "wealth is the synergy of multi-interdependent relationships," saw the club as one more expression of grassroots idealism; Lee Felsenstein, a onetime underground journalist, arrived hoping computers could power a Community Memory Project linking ordinary people the way his beloved, half-failed Resource One commune had tried to do with a salvaged mainframe.

The meeting's real spark, though, was a machine someone had brought to show off: the Altair 8800, a \$375 kit computer built around Intel's new 8080 microprocessor, celebrated on the cover of Popular Electronics as the dawn of a new era. Compared with room-sized mainframes of the previous decade, the Altair's tiny chip packed more computing power than the thirty-ton ENIAC, at a fraction of the size and cost — a leap that the assembled hobbyists grasped by instinct, speculating that such machines might someday run businesses, control home appliances, or even form neighborhood information networks. Word of the club spread by newsletter, typed at midnight by Moore on a borrowed IBM composer, and within eight months membership had swelled from thirty to three hundred, forcing meetings into the SLAC auditorium.

Wozniak found in Homebrew the audience he had always lacked — "the Homebrew meetings were the most important thing in my life" — a fortnightly gathering of kindred obsessives trading schematics, parts, and blunt criticism (a shoddy circuit was dismissed as "a bucket of noise"). Yet for all its communal ideals, the club remained a place of fierce individualists; as Felsenstein observed, everyone was mostly watching to see whether someone else might scoop their particular obsession. It was this unlikely intersection of hippie idealism, hobbyist rivalry, and a genuinely new kind of silicon chip that would, within two years, give Wozniak the raw material and the audience he needed to build something of his own.

## Stanley Zeber Zenskanitsky

Before Apple existed, Wozniak's talents were courted from two directions at once. Alex Kamradt, an eternally optimistic entrepreneur running a small time-sharing business called Call Computer, hired Wozniak to design a television-typewriter terminal for a new subsidiary, Computer Conversor, giving him 30 percent of the venture. Wozniak also landed a coveted engineering job at Hewlett-Packard's calculator division, where he thrived on doughnuts, open stockrooms, and colleagues' tolerance of his private eccentricities — building his own dial-a-joke answering machine under the alias "Stanley Zeber Zenskanitsky," reciting Polish and then Italian jokes to strangers until the phone company gave him a dedicated high-volume line. It was through that prank line that he met Alice Robertson, the San Jose student who became his first wife.

Meanwhile Jobs quietly took charge of turning Kamradt's Computer Conversor prototype into an actual product, working with an outside engineer, Robert Way, who found him an exacting, unsatisfiable taskmaster: "Nothing was ever good enough for him. He was the rejector."

The more consequential story, though, was technical: Wozniak's contrarian choice of microprocessor. While the Homebrew Club fixated on Intel's \$179 8080 chip, Wozniak — driven purely by price — bought Motorola's cheaper 6800, then abandoned it entirely after spotting a nearly identical chip at a trade show, the MOS Technology 6502, selling for just \$25. That decision to design around an oddball, inexpensive chip, combined with his decision to write his own version of BASIC for it, set the stage for everything that followed. Fueled by asthma-driven insomnia and the looming prospect of his own wedding, Wozniak spent the winter of 1975 hand-soldering a new computer around the 6502 and demonstrated it, to a largely indifferent Homebrew Club, in the fall of 1975.

The chapter closes on a contemporary vignette from the Macintosh lab: engineers Burrell Smith and Andy Hertzfeld, exhausted and sleep-deprived, chasing an elusive memory-refresh bug through a Sunday afternoon, their devotion to the machine already indistinguishable from obsession — the same intensity, transposed eight years later, that had once driven Wozniak alone in his Cupertino apartment.

## Half Right

In early 1976 Jobs began pressing Wozniak to sell printed circuit boards of his new computer to fellow hobbyists. Driving along Highway 85, Jobs proposed a name drawn from his fruitarian summers in Oregon: Apple Computer. To fund their share of the roughly \$1,300 needed, Wozniak sold his HP calculator and Jobs sold his Volkswagen bus — the same van his father had warned him was "a tired, gutless thing." On April Fool's Day 1976, Wozniak, Jobs, and a third partner, Ron Wayne — a middle-aged former slot-machine designer who drew Apple's first logo and then, spooked by the financial risk, sold back his 10 percent stake for \$800 within months — signed a partnership agreement forming Apple Computer Company.

The turning point came when Jobs walked into the Byte Shop, a Mountain View computer store run by Paul Terrell, who surprised him with an order for fifty fully assembled computers, cash on delivery. "I just saw dollar signs," Jobs recalled. Scrambling for parts on credit from suppliers who took a chance on the scruffy pair, Jobs and Wozniak turned the Jobs family home — and eventually the garage, once Paul Jobs conceded the space — into an assembly line, with Jobs's pregnant sister Patty paid a dollar a board to stuff components while soap operas played in the background. Wozniak, disdaining haphazard wire-wrapping, insisted on meticulous point-to-point soldering; Jobs, meanwhile, fought for a superior but pricier Intel memory chip over a cheaper alternative, a bet that paid off when Intel's chip became the industry standard.

Priced at \$666.66 — arrived at through simple markup arithmetic rather than any occult intention, as Jobs was forced to explain to superstitious callers — the Apple I sold modestly through Terrell's Byte Shops alongside a growing supply chain built on handshake credit from sympathetic distributors. Clara Jobs served as impromptu receptionist and confidante; Wozniak's young marriage buckled under the strain of all-night debugging sessions. By the fall, Commodore's founders offered to buy the fledgling company outright, but Jobs, suspicious of Commodore's reputation, and Wozniak, backed by his father's blunt insistence that "you don't deserve shit" from a lopsided split, turned the offer down and kept Apple independent.

## A Lot of Poop

Fresh off their unimpressive debut at a computer fair in Atlantic City, Wozniak returned to his Hewlett-Packard bench determined to add color graphics to the Apple — inspired by a rival hobbyist machine, the Cromemco Dazzler — while simultaneously halving the chip count by cleverly sharing one memory bank between the microprocessor and the video display. He also insisted on eight expansion slots against Jobs's preference for just two, a dispute Wozniak refused to lose. Around this design work orbited two teenage acolytes, Randy Wigginton and Chris Espinosa, who chauffeured by Wozniak to Homebrew Club meetings became early employees and lifelong company lore, while a public spat with Bill Gates — who accused hobbyists of stealing his BASIC software — exposed the growing tension between the club's free-information ethos and the commercial reality of software.

When representatives from Commodore Business Machines arrived at the garage offering to buy Apple outright for cash, stock, and salaries, Jobs's inquiries into founder Jack Tramiel's reputation for squeezing partners soured him on the deal, and the sale collapsed amid bitter arguments — including a confrontation in which Wozniak's father reduced Jobs to tears, insisting he "hadn't produced anything."

The chapter's most memorable figure is Rod Holt, the aging, chain-smoking, Marxist-leaning analog engineer whom Jobs recruited to design a power supply. Holt, a former motorcycle racer and radical political organizer with "a lot of poop" in both his hi-fi designs and his opinions, built Apple's first switching power supply — small, reliable, fan-free — a technical leap none of Apple's competitors matched. The chapter closes on a present-day marketing meeting where Apple executives, debating how to position Lisa and Mac against IBM, concede that "it costs a helluva lot to have a revolution," and that the company's advertising budget was nowhere near equal to its ambitions.

## Mercedes and a Corvette

Apple's founders, still amateurs playing in a world of seasoned semiconductor professionals, needed sponsors to be taken seriously. Jobs's persistent phone calls landed Apple an introduction to Regis McKenna, the low-key but formidably effective Palo Alto marketing man who had built Intel's image and who initially clashed with Wozniak over editing a technical article before agreeing to represent the fledgling company. On Nolan Bushnell's advice, Jobs also approached venture capitalist Don Valentine, who found Jobs unimpressive and the founders naively small in their ambitions, but who redirected them toward a candidate from his own Rolodex: Mike Markkula, a retired Intel and Fairchild marketing veteran who had made himself a millionaire before thirty and was living in comfortable Cupertino retirement.

Markkula was captivated by the Apple II prototype — "it was what I had wanted since I left high school" — and agreed to personally guarantee a \$250,000 bank loan in exchange for a third of the company, on the condition that Wozniak commit to Apple full time. When Wozniak wavered, torn between the security of his Hewlett-Packard paycheck and his skepticism that any company would pay him what Markkula insisted, Jobs staged an emotional campaign, breaking down in tears at the Wozniak family home to win over Stephen's parents. Markkula also insisted on hiring an outside president to mind operations, settling on Michael Scott, an old colleague from Fairchild days who ran manufacturing lines at National Semiconductor and who accepted the job largely out of boredom with corporate routine.

The chapter captures how differently each of the five founding figures — Jobs, Wozniak, Holt, Markkula, and Scott — were wired, united only by mutual need. It closes on a present-day scene in which Mac marketing managers struggle to explain the machine's name and purpose to the visiting European artist Jean-Michel Folon, whose translator bristles at corporate jargon like "user interface" while an Italian colleague cheerfully predicts that China, with its abacus-using population, will make a marvelous market for the computer.

## What a Motherboard

With the First West Coast Computer Faire looming in the spring of 1977, Jobs pushed to give the Apple II a polish none of its hobbyist-built rivals possessed. He first turned to Ron Wayne, the original Apple tie-breaker, for a case design, but rejected Wayne's Rube Goldberg-style creation — a Plexiglas-topped box with a rolltop tambour door — as clumsy. Unwilling to settle for a sheet-metal case like Processor Technology's Sol, Jobs hired industrial designer Jerry Mannock, a Hewlett-Packard refugee who had quit corporate life, traveled Europe, and was struggling to build a home design practice on \$100 profits a year. Mannock produced a sleek plastic housing modeled on Hewlett-Packard's calculators in three weeks, working to a punishing twelve-week deadline he only accepted because, as he put it, "I hadn't done this before so I didn't know any better."

Meanwhile Regis McKenna's young art director Rob Janov designed the now-iconic bitten, rainbow-striped apple logo, gouging a bite from the fruit so it wouldn't "look like a cherry tomato" — insisting, against Jobs's wishes for razor-perfect color separations, on a design that could actually be manufactured cheaply. Rod Holt again solved the power problem with an innovative switching supply smaller than a milk carton, and Jobs relentlessly hounded a former Atari colleague, Howard Cantin, to perfect the circuit board artwork, delaying production to have it digitized by computer for precision — driving Cantin to swear he'd "never work for him again."

Everything came down to the wire: keyboards failed every twenty minutes from static electricity, the first molded plastic cases arrived warped and had to be hand-sanded, puttied, and spray-painted beige, and business cards returned just two days before the fair opened. Markkula, meanwhile, staged Apple's booth with a backlit Plexiglas sign and steered Jobs to a San Francisco tailor for the first suit of his life. A January 1977 Homebrew Club survey found Apple's machines held just 3.3 percent of hobbyist installations, far behind IMSAI and Altair — a gap Markkula, Jobs, and McKenna hoped the Faire could start to close.

Amid the scramble, Wozniak and young recruit Randy Wigginton pulled off an elaborate prank, distributing spoof advertisements for a fictitious computer called the "Zaltair" throughout the fair — copy so extravagant, promising "BAZIC" software and an "eighteen-slot motherboard," that an unwitting Jobs, reading the flyer, exclaimed "Oh, my God! This thing sounds great" before realizing his own machine had been ranked, tongue-in-cheek, just behind it.

Though Apple's booth drew roughly three hundred orders and impressed engineers like Lee Felsenstein — who called the bare-bones display "highly simplistic and bold in its crudeness" — the fair's chief organizer, Jim Warren, later admitted Apple hadn't been the standout exhibitor, and Byte magazine's report on the event didn't even mention the company. That footnote was quickly forgotten as the Apple II, blending Wozniak's elegant logic board, Holt's patented power supply, and Mannock's case, began outselling every computer the company had made before.

## Up to Spec

Apple's real education began in a shingled office suite at 20863 Stevens Creek Boulevard, barely a mile from the founders' parents' homes, where Markkula drafted a business plan with the help of Syntex controller John Hall — who took the job for a \$4,000 fee after concluding privately that he "didn't believe the business plan" and declining Markkula's offer to become vice-president of finance. Around them the founding quintet — Jobs, Wozniak, Holt, Markkula, and Scott, whom Wigginton privately labeled The Rejector, The Hobbyist, The Fixer, The Pacifier, and The Enforcer — learned to coexist despite having almost nothing in common. Holt put it bluntly: "There wasn't much trust at all... It was a business not a family affair."

Scott, as president, clashed constantly with Jobs over petty details from laboratory bench colors to employee badge numbers. When Scott assigned Wozniak employee number one and Jobs number two, Jobs balked: "Am I number one?" "No. Woz is number one. You're number two." "I want to be number one," Jobs pressed. "Can I be number zero?" Scott also quietly imposed the fiscal discipline — forty-five-day credit terms on memory chips, a respected outside auditing firm, a shrewdly timed fiscal year-end — that kept the fragile company solvent, while accountant Gary Martin watched Jobs berate suppliers over price: "We were all asking, 'How can you treat another human being like that?'"

When the case subcontractor's tooling broke in September 1977, Apple came within weeks of running out of cash; Scott later called it "life and death for us." Jobs raced to a Pacific Northwest mold maker, offering a \$1,000-a-week bonus for early delivery, and the crisis passed. Even as friction persisted — Jobs's ferocious insistence on quality warred constantly with Scott's instinct to ship and Holt's demand that everything be "up to spec" — a genuine camaraderie took hold among a growing staff of engineers, teenagers, and refugees from larger companies, bonded by long hours, communal parties, and the shared thrill of watching a tiny business sell real computers to real strangers. Regular visitor and phone phreak John Draper, fresh out of prison, even designed a plug-in "Charlie Board" with Wozniak that could scan for free long-distance codes, until Draper was arrested again and Apple's management distanced themselves from him.

Personal lives frayed under the pressure. Wozniak's marriage collapsed into a stock-splitting separation agreement giving his wife 15 percent of his Apple stock, while Jobs's relationship with his high-school girlfriend Nancy Rogers, sharing a Cupertino house with Dan Kottke, disintegrated amid her insecurity — she hurled plates, scrawled obscenities on his walls — and his absorption in the company. "Steve didn't care that I was pregnant," she later said. "I had to get away from Steve, Apple, and people's opinions."

The chapter's climax is the race to build a floppy disk drive, a project Wozniak procrastinated on until Christmas 1977, ignoring pointed complaints from software pioneer Gary Kildall that Apple's cassette storage was "low-end hobbyist grade," before working around the clock for weeks to produce a strikingly elegant disk controller that used far fewer chips than any competitor's — a design Wozniak called "the favorite design of my life" and one that visibly widened Apple's lead over Commodore and Radio Shack. Lee Felsenstein, seeing it, said, "I nearly dropped my pants." The chapter ends with a glimpse of the more bureaucratic company Apple was becoming, via a sardonic in-house joke pinned up in the Mac lab asking how many Apple employees it takes to change a lightbulb — the punchline an absurdly long chain of committees, memos, and product managers.

## The Best Salesmen

Markkula understood, better than anyone at Apple, that perception was as valuable as product, repeating to Jobs, "You judge a book by its cover." He courted a small set of prestigious financiers — Venrock (the Rockefeller family's venture fund, brought in through Markkula's old Fairchild and Intel colleague Hank Smith), the legendary and famously discreet Arthur Rock, and eventually Teledyne chairman Henry Singleton — not primarily for their money but for the credibility their names lent a fledgling company nobody outside Silicon Valley had heard of. Rock, whose history included financing Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel and turning a stake in Scientific Data Systems into \$60 million, joined Apple's board after a chance viewing of a demonstration; Singleton was won over only after Scott personally hand-delivered him a disk drive and spent a day discussing assembly-language programming. Once the January 1978 financing closed, Apple was valued at \$3 million, with Venrock contributing \$288,000, Don Valentine \$150,000, and Rock \$57,600.

Regis McKenna's agency, initially so unsure Apple would survive that account executives privately called the founders "flakes," eventually bet fully on the company, with McKenna pushing Markkula to double the proposed \$300,000 advertising budget on the theory that a small company had to look and act big to be believed. Apple's earliest advertisements — a kitchen scene showing a housewife cooking while her husband balanced "biorhythms" and the checkbook — misjudged the market, as software pioneer Gary Kildall pointedly wrote to Jobs, complaining the ads promised software and disk drives that didn't yet exist. The company quickly pivoted toward businesses and dealers, forging alliances with Computerland's Ed Faber, Dow Jones's Carl Valenti, and Bell & Howell that gave it a distribution reach far beyond its size, while Wall Street analyst Ben Rosen, an early and devoted Apple user himself, became, in the words of venture capitalist Hank Smith, "one of Apple's best salesmen," feeding admiring quotes to The Wall Street Journal, Business Week, and Fortune.

The single most important development, though, arrived almost by accident: a Harvard MBA student named Daniel Bricklin, frustrated by tedious budget recalculations and borrowing an Apple II from Daniel Fylstra's small software company, Personal Software, wrote the first electronic spreadsheet with programmer Robert Frankston. VisiCalc, demonstrated to a bemused Markkula who "interpreted it as a checkbook program," was available exclusively on the Apple II for its first twelve months and, priced at \$100, gave businesses a concrete reason to buy the machine that no amount of advertising could have manufactured. Rosen predicted it could become "the software tail that wags (and sells) the personal computer dog," and by 1980 Scott estimated VisiCalc alone accounted for 25,000 of Apple's 130,000 computer sales — brewery owner Fritz Maytag calling it "just a miracle."

The chapter closes on a present-day breakfast at the Good Earth Restaurant where New York dealer Anthony Morris warns Apple's Mac marketing manager, Michael Murray, that he is about to start carrying IBM and DEC machines alongside Apple's, because "nobody has ever got fired for buying IBM" — a warning, delivered over coffee and cinnamon-scented vinyl booths, that IBM's disciplined, business-savvy sales machine was starting to erode Apple's dominance, and one that would prove prophetic.

## The Bozo Explosion

As orders multiplied following the 1978 disk-drive announcement, Apple ballooned from a garage operation into something resembling a real corporation, moving through a succession of buildings fifteen times larger than its first office and hiring waves of professional managers poached from Hewlett-Packard, National Semiconductor, and Intel. Manufacturing chief Roy Mollard, a ramrod-straight Liverpudlian from National, hired security guards, installed hidden microphones, scrapped casual Ping-Pong games, and declared his aim was "to hose out computers." Each imported culture brought its own habits — Hewlett-Packard's genteel planning and paperwork versus National's take-no-prisoners manufacturing ethos, versus Intel's rigid procedures under new personnel chief Ann Bowers — and friction between them, and between all of them and the young hobbyist engineers who had built the Apple II, became a constant undercurrent. Engineer Tom Whitney, arriving from Hewlett-Packard alongside briefly overlapping 6502-designer Chuck Peddle, introduced acronym-laden forms like ECOs and IDSs that programmer Chuck Mauro said were greeted with "guffaws."

Longtime programmers like Randy Wigginton bristled as newcomers dismissed their code as "spaghetti" and treated the Apple II itself as "a joke... not a real computer"; Chris Espinosa complained that the incoming men in "button-down shirts" should have stayed "extras in Cary Grant movies." Wozniak, having finished the disk drive, drifted into an odd mix of pranks — soluble green slime, fizzing sodas, Alka-Seltzer tablets pinned to restaurant menus — gambling trips to Reno, and general disengagement, more interested, as colleague Dick Huston put it, in being treated as "the Messiah" than in doing the unglamorous work of a maturing business.

Michael Scott's insistence on tight financial control and his personally designed management-information system, guarded by a password borrowed from his cat Baal, gave him near-total visibility into the company, down to individual unpaid invoices, and became one of his most durable contributions. But the friction between "amateur" hackers and "professional" managers curdled into open resentment as stock options, structure, and a formal divisional reorganization — in the fall of 1980, splitting Apple into units for disk drives, the Apple II and III, the Lisa project, manufacturing, sales, and service — created new status hierarchies almost overnight. Programmer Rick Auricchio, in the scrappier Personal Computer Systems division, felt the glamorous Lisa team viewed his group with contempt: "We felt that we were dull and boring... Without the right color badge and an escort, you couldn't get into the Lisa building. That was an insult."

By September 1980 Apple had grown from thirty employees to over a thousand in barely two and a half years — a hiring binge so frenzied it became known inside the company as "The Bozo Explosion," with payroll leaping from six hundred to twelve hundred employees in just twelve weeks. Executives tried to counteract the resulting anonymity and drift with lavish parties, ski trips, employee stock-purchase perks, Halloween pageants, and a committee-drafted list of corporate values modeled loosely on Hewlett-Packard's own credo, promising "Empathy," "Achievement/Aggressiveness," and "Team Spirit." Yet an unmistakable pecking order took hold regardless — visible in employee badge numbers stamped onto license plates, executive parking, and the private wealth some employees now possessed — prompting programmer Dick Huston to observe flatly, "I have never thought of Apple as an egalitarian place to work."

## The Platinum Credit Card

Apple's public stock offering on December 12, 1980 — priced at \$22 and closing its first day at \$29, the largest issue since Ford Motor Company went public in 1956 — transformed paper promises into staggering real wealth almost overnight, giving Apple a market value greater than Chase Manhattan Bank, Ford, or Lockheed combined many times over. Thanks to three stock splits, any of the "founders' shares" held since before April 1979 had multiplied thirty-two-fold; Jobs's 15 percent stake was suddenly worth \$256.4 million, Markkula's \$239 million, Wozniak's \$135.6 million, Scott's \$95.5 million. Rod Holt, the once-broke motorcycle-racing engineer, found himself sitting on \$67 million, and even Arthur Rock's original \$57,600 investment had grown to \$21.8 million.

The unevenness of who had received stock options and who had not opened deep wounds. Early employees like Dan Kottke, Bill Fernandez, and Chris Espinosa, who had done essential early work but were, as Espinosa put it, "too nice," "too Buddhist," or "too young" to negotiate aggressively, were left with nothing — Jobs, when Holt proposed the two of them jointly give Kottke some shares, simply replied, "Great! I'll give him zero." Wozniak, appalled by the imbalance, informally sold roughly 80,000 of his own shares to colleagues at a steep \$7.50 discount in what became known as the "Wozplan," while well-connected outsiders like financier Fayez Sarofim and Xerox Corporation, which bought 100,000 shares ahead of the offering, had far easier access to stock than Apple's own line workers.

The money reshaped lives unevenly. Wozniak gave away \$4 million in stock to his family and \$2 million to friends, bought a movie theater and a Beechcraft airplane, and eight weeks after the offering nearly died when his small plane crashed on takeoff near Scotts Valley, leaving him with amnesia, a fractured eye socket, and a smashed tooth. Jobs, by contrast, grew increasingly austere and isolated, living in a nearly unfurnished Los Gatos house — one photograph of guru Neem Karolie Baba propped by the mattress — embarrassed by wealth he didn't know how to spend ("You run out of things to buy real quick"), while privately fighting, and initially denying despite a UCLA blood test showing 94.41 percent probability of paternity, a child-support case involving his daughter with former girlfriend Nancy Rogers, born on Robert Friedland's Oregon farm and tellingly named Lisa — the same name later given to Apple's next computer.

The chapter closes with a glimpse of Jobs holding court at a Stanford dormitory, dressed in jeans and Wilkes Bashford tailoring, cross-legged on a coffee table, delivering his standard evangelist's pitch about giving computers to every school in the country and predicting Apple would one day sell half a million machines a month — even as he deflects questions about the stock price and jokes with students about LSD, revealing a young man equally comfortable as countercultural mystic and corporate spokesman.

## Welcome IBM, Seriously

Small-town newspapers across America — the East Aurora Advertiser, the Geneva Signal, the Blairstown Star Press — ran breathless items on Apples turning up in libraries, farms, and police stations, while imitators like Franklin's ACE 100 (later found in federal court to have copied Apple's operating system outright) and knockoffs in Taiwan named Apolo II and Pineapple testified to how large the company loomed. But flush with cash and celebrity, Apple also grew intoxicated with its own success, and an aggressive arrogance crept into how the company treated software partners, dealers, engineers, and the press — cutting off distributors who showed weakness, threatening engineer Chuck Mauro when he left to start his own peripheral company ("if we wanted to we could squash you like a bug"), and badgering the editor of a trade newspaper over an unflattering story about product plans.

Jobs, now vice president of research and development, ran product decisions almost entirely on instinct rather than planning — "you make a lot of decisions based on the fragrance or the odor of where you think things are going" — a style that produced both the company's greatest triumphs and its worst stumble: the Apple III, rushed to market on an absurdly optimistic ten-month schedule and "designed by committee," according to Wigginton, shipped riddled with hardware defects — a National Semiconductor clock chip that failed after three hours, shorting circuit boards, cases so fragile that engineers resorted to testing them by hitting them with rubber hammers or dropping them three inches to reseat loose chips. Only sixty-five thousand were ever sold in three years, and Jobs's own promotional posters boasting of 50,000 units became an embarrassment.

Far more consequential was Apple's encounter with Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, where more than \$100 million in research had produced a prototype computer called the Alto: a machine using a mouse, overlapping on-screen "windows," bit-mapped graphics, and a language called Smalltalk, technology far beyond anything Apple had contemplated. Jobs, initially resistant to visiting, became captivated once he saw it, immediately asking programmer Bill Atkinson how long it would take to bring similar software to the in-development Lisa computer ("Oh, six months," Atkinson guessed). The visit triggered a palace coup among Lisa's engineers and reshaped the entire direction of the machine, whose mouse-driven, one-button interface — refined down from Xerox's three-button original — would become the template for the Macintosh and, eventually, the entire personal computer industry. Pricing disputes were fierce; one manager recalled Jobs threatening, "If I have to I'll bring Woz in. Woz could do it for less."

Looming over all of this was the rival long feared and long dismissed: in 1981 IBM, ninety times Apple's size and with a history of eventually crushing any company that threatened its business, introduced its own personal computer, built with uncharacteristic speed and openness by outsourcing its microprocessor, its operating system (licensed from a small company called Microsoft), and even its word-processing software (adapted, ironically, by ex-phone-phreak John Draper from a program he had written for the Apple II). Apple's response — a full-page advertisement reading "Welcome, IBM. Seriously... We look forward to responsible competition" — dripped with a confidence Markkula and Jobs both voiced privately, Markkula insisting "short of World War III nothing is going to knock us out of the box" and Jobs vowing, "We're going to outmarket IBM. We've got our shit together." The bravado would prove badly misplaced.

The chapter closes on an unrelated, almost incongruous scene: Wozniak, having drifted from Apple's daily operations and remarried, sinking his fortune into an ambitious and ultimately troubled Labor Day rock festival staged in the California desert near Devore, complete with its own hot-air balloon bearing the Apple logo, chasing a vision of countercultural unity — "Unite Us in Song" — that had little to do with computers at all.

## Epilogue

Writing more than a quarter-century after the events of this book, Moritz surveys the graveyard of once-feared rivals — an alphabet running from Atari and Ashton-Tate to WordPerfect and Zenith Data Systems — and marvels that Apple not only survived but eventually eclipsed a giant like Sony, whose Walkman had once seemed the unassailable model for how a founder's vision could define consumer electronics for decades. He recalls a joke that circulated once the tables had turned: "How do you spell SONY? A-P-P-L-E." Three currents, he argues, favored Apple: the spread of computing into every corner of daily life, the advantage software-literate companies held over hardware-only rivals in designing consumer products, and the rise of cloud computing, which let firms like Google and eventually Apple itself deliver services once unimaginable on a personal machine. The company's near-collapse in the mid-1980s and 1990s is traced to a single recurring mistake: hiring outside professional managers to run a business built on a founder's instincts. John Sculley, recruited from Pepsi in 1983 on the theory that Apple needed "adult supervision," pushed Jobs out in 1987 and presided over a decade of rising sales but hollowing innovation, leaving behind forgettable products like the Newton before being replaced by Michael Spindler and then Gil Amelio, neither of whom could reverse Apple's decline against Microsoft's Windows and Intel-powered clones.

Jobs's own wilderness years, at the failed workstation maker NeXT and the animation studio Pixar, proved formative rather than wasted; when Apple acquired NeXT in 1996 mainly for its software, Jobs maneuvered his way back to the top within a year, inheriting a company nearly out of cash with no compelling products. His turnaround began not with a defensive "We're back" campaign but with the defiant "Think Different" ads celebrating outsiders and visionaries, followed by ruthless cost-cutting, the colorful iMac, and — decisively — the iPod, iTunes, Apple's retail stores, and finally the iPhone, each an evolutionary refinement of existing ideas rather than a wholly new invention, but executed with a polish no competitor could match.

Moritz closes by noting the singular difficulty of what Jobs achieved: building a company capable of mass-producing millions of complex, exquisite objects with the sensibility of an individual artist, and by posing the question that remained unanswered as he wrote — given Jobs's declining health, who could possibly inherit that instinct, and whether Apple could avoid becoming, like Sony after its founder's retirement, a cautionary tale of brilliance without succession.

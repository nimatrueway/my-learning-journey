# Founders at Work: Stories of Startups' Early Days

Jessica Livingston

## Foreword

Paul Graham compares startups to sprinters: their fastest, most productive period often comes immediately after the start, when only a few founders are present and the largest ideas take shape. Apple at the moment when every employee was either Steve Jobs or Steve Wozniak captures the intensity of that stage. Yet it looks almost nothing like the conventional image of business, with suits, conference tables, presentations, and thick reports.

Graham argues that this disconnect reflects a broader principle. When performance is weak, people often spend more energy on appearing impressive, and that effort can make real work harder. He recalls a car magazine making a production sports car faster by stripping away the decorative equipment added to make it look fast. Much of corporate professionalism, in his view, works like that unnecessary equipment. Formal clothes do not improve thought, and executives may have their best ideas at home on a quiet Sunday morning rather than in an office designed to signal productivity.

His own startup exposed the contradiction. Before outsiders visited, the team cleaned the office, dressed better, and tried to ensure that people were visible during normal hours. The actual programming happened amid clutter, in bad clothes, often at two in the morning. Graham even remembers programming in a towel. Despite producing results, the founders felt like impostors because their process looked unprofessional to visitors and investors. They had, in his analogy, built a Formula One car while worrying that it did not resemble an ordinary sedan.

Few people witness this reality because the dramatic growth usually occurs when a startup has only three or four people. By contrast, tens of thousands see the established practices of major corporations, so ceremony becomes mistaken for business itself. The interviews in this book are meant to correct that imbalance by opening a view into startups' first year and showing what concentrated productivity actually looks like.

Graham does not claim that large organizations can copy everything. Their scale brings more politics, coordination, and limits on individual decisions. Still, early startups offer a useful direction of travel. Instead of founders disguising themselves as corporations, established companies might learn to remove some performance theater and become more like startups. The result would look unusual by traditional standards, but could work considerably better.
## Introduction

Jessica Livingston began with a simple problem: the formative period of a startup can seem almost magical, yet usually only its founders witness it. She therefore asked successful founders to reconstruct those beginnings in their own words. Across their accounts, she looked for common qualities and found a picture that differs sharply from the mythology surrounding famous companies.

The founders were often unsure that they had discovered anything important. Some companies began almost accidentally, and many founders hesitated before committing to a business. Their confidence centered less on eventual greatness than on making something good or repairing something broken. Livingston identifies determination as their most consistent exceptional trait. Startup life brought uncertainty, isolation, slow stretches, and plans that repeatedly failed, but these founders kept building.

They also endured rejection from investors, journalists, and established companies. Innovation is admired in the abstract but frequently dismissed in concrete form because it does not fit familiar categories. Web-based email, now ordinary, once seemed commercially unpromising. The caveat is that perseverance alone is insufficient: because almost nothing proceeds according to plan, founders must also adapt rather than merely defend their first idea.

The interviews repeatedly show companies changing direction. PayPal began with encryption software and handheld devices, Excite with database search, and Flickr emerged from an online game. Startup formation is therefore closer to trial and error than to the growth of a perfect original seed. What helped founders choose among experiments was empathy for users. However often the product changed, they kept asking whether they were making something people would actually want.

Money mattered and successful founders gained financial freedom, but Livingston found pride in craftsmanship and a desire to affect the world to be stronger recurring motives. Many went on to equally ambitious projects rather than retiring from creation. Their behavior also reveals how different young startups are from established companies. Early productivity can look disorganized and unbusinesslike precisely because it is stripped of much of the ceremony associated with corporate work.

Livingston's aim is not to turn these histories into a guaranteed formula. Instead, she wants to preserve a shared fund of experience, including recurring problems and lessons founders wished they had known earlier. Retrospective success can make exceptional people seem like a separate species and conceal their original uncertainty. Seeing the rejections, pivots, accidents, and ordinary starting conditions may make entrepreneurship less daunting. The central invitation is not to imitate every decision, but to recognize that celebrated companies were built by people who once stood at an unclear beginning and learned by continuing.

That record may also help established organizations. If young startups appear unbusinesslike while producing unusually valuable work, conventional business could benefit from paying closer attention to what they do and discarding some habits that merely look productive.
## Max Levchin and PayPal

Max Levchin arrived in Silicon Valley in 1998 after studying security and starting several companies around college. While sleeping on a friend's floor, he attended a nearly empty Stanford lecture by Peter Thiel. Their conversations led Thiel to fund Levchin's handheld cryptography idea and become his cofounder and CEO.

The original thesis was wrong. Enterprises did not need Palm Pilot security libraries, and later products, including a digital wallet, found little demand. A Palm-to-Palm money transfer nevertheless attracted investors. Before a demonstration at Buck's restaurant, Levchin and two programmers worked five days almost without sleep so a \$4.5 million transfer would be real. It succeeded, after which Levchin fell asleep beside his omelet.

The decisive signal came from a neglected website demo. eBay sellers asked to use it while the team initially sent them away. PayPal eventually followed those users, iterated rapidly on web payments, and closed the handheld product at about 12,000 users while the website had roughly 1.2 to 1.5 million.

Growth exposed a less visible turning point: fraud losses exceeded \$10 million a month. Levchin and an intern built statistical and visualization tools, including IGOR, that helped investigators trace account networks and prioritize expected losses. Combining software with human judgment brought fraud under control and became an advantage competitors could not copy.

Levchin stresses adaptability and strong cofounders rather than foresight. PayPal changed plans about six times, benefited from luck, and survived because people noticed danger and acted. Peter and Levchin supported each other when one lost confidence. Later structure was unavoidable, but early on, an excellent team and willingness to abandon a cherished product mattered more than a fixed identity.
## Sabeer Bhatia and Hotmail

Sabeer Bhatia met Jack Smith while building PowerBooks at Apple, then joined him at FirePower Systems. Their first startup plan was JavaSoft, a browser-fronted personal database. The stronger idea arose when FirePower's firewall blocked their personal email, forcing them to exchange information on paper and floppy disks. If a browser could reach websites, they reasoned, it could provide email anywhere.

Because the concept was easy to copy, they pitched JavaSoft first and revealed webmail only to investors who questioned the business rather than dismissing their youth or hardware backgrounds. Draper Fisher Jurvetson supplied \$300,000. Bhatia later regretted a right-of-first-refusal clause that weakened leverage in later financing, showing how early terms can outlast funding relief.

Hotmail launched on July 4, 1996, and reached 100,000 subscribers in three months. Outgoing messages carried an invitation to get a free account. The team worried that altering email might feel intrusive, but the line turned users into distribution. Hourly pager reports showing signups rising from hundreds to thousands energized the company.

Rapid adoption brought outages, inadequate backups, and rearchitecture. Some users did not know to click a message, showing how unfamiliar browser behavior remained. Hotmail was not highly profitable, but outsourced advertising commitments roughly covered its low costs.

Microsoft approached after Hotmail reached about seven million subscribers. Facing fifteen negotiators, Bhatia declined a \$160 million opening offer without naming a counter and relied on Hotmail's user momentum. Microsoft ultimately paid \$400 million less than two years after launch. Bhatia credits need, timing, and luck alongside execution. His lesson is to minimize behavioral change, remove friction, and earn customers before insisting on immediate monetization.
## Steve Wozniak and Apple

Steve Wozniak's path to Apple began long before the company. With little money, he redesigned computers on paper, competing with himself to remove chips. Fewer parts meant lower cost, deeper understanding, and fewer bugs. While designing calculators at Hewlett-Packard, he built a television terminal, then discovered inexpensive microprocessors through the Homebrew Computer Club.

Combining a keyboard, television, dynamic memory, and a \$20 processor, Wozniak created the Apple I he had wanted since high school. He wrote its Basic by hand and shared schematics at Homebrew. Steve Jobs recognized that enthusiasts wanted assembled boards. A Byte Shop order for 100 machines at \$500 each created a business, after Hewlett-Packard formally declined the design.

The Apple II was decisive. Wozniak used few parts while adding color, sound, graphics, and expansion slots. Jobs wanted two slots, but Wozniak insisted on eight; third-party cards made that openness central to success. Commodore and Atari declined the design before Mike Markkula offered financing. Wozniak initially refused to leave Hewlett-Packard until a friend said he could start Apple and remain an engineer.

His floppy controller illustrates the approach. Having never used a floppy, he bypassed standard circuitry, built a compact controller, accidentally overwrote the demonstration disk in Las Vegas, then recreated it before the show. The story supports his advice to work from first principles, test details, and persist without ideal tools.

Wozniak adds caveats. Exhausting work caused illness, luck accompanied breakthroughs, and close partners still needed honesty. Motivated more by engineering and community than wealth, he later shared stock with early employees. Apple's origin joined his concentrated design skill with Jobs's drive to create a company.
## Joe Kraus and Excite

Joe Kraus learned early that he preferred creating his own work. After quitting a summer job duplicating microfiche, he and a friend built a T-shirt business that earned \$25,000. At Stanford, the political science major persuaded programmer Graham Spencer and four dorm friends to form a company before knowing what it would make.

At a taqueria, their ideas failed until Spencer observed that electronic information was expanding while search tools remained primitive. With \$15,000 from parents, the founders set up Architext in a Palo Alto garage, heated it with an open dryer, and spent eighteen months building indexing technology. A \$300 copier that never worked taught them to conserve capital.

Their route to funding was accidental but cumulative. A line in a Silicon Valley history prompted Kraus to call its author, leading to a \$100,000 InfoWorld contract and venture capitalists. Vinod Khosla bought them a costly drive to test whether the engine scaled. It did, and choosing the Web over HyperCard also redirected the company into web search.

The defining turn came when Excite bid \$3 million for Netscape distribution despite having \$1 million, then lost. Khosla urged them to keep negotiating. When the winner could not deliver, Netscape returned, launching Excite's growth. Kraus concludes that persistence creates access to luck and opportunities generate unforeseeable chains.

Success did not validate every decision. Excite treated search as a portal feeder and used print-style impression advertising, missing paid clicks. Kraus learned to hire slowly, stay cheap, and build fundamentals before racing ahead. Friendship complicated equity decisions, but commitment to one another carried the founders through uncertainty an idea alone might not have survived.
## Dan Bricklin and VisiCalc

Dan Bricklin met Bob Frankston through MIT's Multics project, and their friendship made starting a business natural. At Harvard Business School, Bricklin combined experience in interpreters, word processing, and typesetting with repetitive financial calculations. He imagined a two-dimensional workspace where changing one number recalculated the rest, then tested it in Basic over a weekend on a borrowed Apple II.

Frankston built most of the program in assembly while Bricklin refined interaction. Working from Frankston's attic, they paid for late-night time sharing and borrowed family money. Constraints shaped VisiCalc: an unstable game paddle gave way to arrow keys, cells received names such as A1, and the slash key opened prompting commands. With everything fitting within tens of kilobytes, fuller help was unaffordable.

VisiCalc shipped in October 1979 and persuaded businesses to buy Apple IIs. Adoption was not universal. People recognized value when shown an example from their work; others thought they could reproduce it in Basic or missed how a general tool applied. Users ranged from accountants planning casino layouts to doctors performing calculations.

The author-publisher arrangement with Personal Software became destructive. Contract friction led to litigation as Lotus 1-2-3 arrived, blocking a proposed CompuServe sale and consuming Software Arts. Bricklin says the lawsuit killed the company, though Lotus later bought its assets and prevented bankruptcy.

His lessons are practical and qualified: design against the user's existing alternative, for VisiCalc the back of an envelope; focus on the product; avoid lawsuits when settlement is possible; and do not confuse technical influence with business durability. Bricklin gained less fortune than he might have, but lasting recognition left him without broad regret.
## Mitchell Kapor and Lotus

Mitchell Kapor entered computing without conventional credentials. After work as a radio disc jockey, meditation teacher, and mental health counselor, he bought an Apple II and programmed for local professionals. A statistics tool became VisiPlot and produced about \$100,000 monthly in royalties. Its buyout left Kapor seed capital and an exception allowing an integrated graphing calculator.

The opening appeared when IBM introduced its PC in 1981. VisiCalc's port retained eight-bit limitations while the new hardware offered more memory. Kapor and Jonathan Sachs targeted it directly. Kapor shaped the product vision; Sachs implemented almost all the original assembly code. Fast recalculation and one-button graphs made demonstrations compelling.

Kapor nearly exhausted \$300,000 on side projects before Ben Rosen invested. Lotus 1-2-3 shipped in January 1983, and projected sales of \$3 to \$4 million became \$53 million that year and \$150 million the next. Kapor credits timing and Sachs's complementary skill alongside his judgment.

The harder failures came after launch. Kapor was unprepared to manage beyond roughly twenty-five people, hired poorly, expanded quickly, and allowed an undisciplined product strategy. Lotus created a humane, progressive workplace, but his successor dismantled much of it. Rapid success brought organizational problems product insight had not prepared him to solve.

Kapor's lessons center on values, first principles, and informed financing. He stopped a closing when investors inserted onerous terms, and they backed down. He later regretted supporting litigation over software look and feel. Founders should understand financing, seek aligned mentors, keep working code close to its target, and avoid projects demanding unacceptable compromises.
## Ray Ozzie, Notes, and Groove

Ray Ozzie's startups grew from anticipated user needs rather than technology seeking a market. Experience with the collaborative PLATO system led him to imagine Lotus Notes in 1982, before desktop computers were common communication tools. Unable to fund it, he joined Lotus and led Symphony. Lotus then financed Iris Associates in 1984, and Notes made networked collaboration widely practical.

Groove began in Ozzie's house in 1997 with former colleagues. He wrote one document describing user scenarios and another mapping required technology. The team attacked its greatest uncertainty: masterless synchronization among unreliable computers. After months of whiteboard work and prototypes proved feasibility, Groove committed to a peer-to-peer desktop architecture, contrarian during the Web's ascent.

The product targeted temporary workgroups spanning companies and incompatible systems. Its 2001 release included a 10,000-seat GlaxoSmithKline deal. Rather than proving a repeatable model, that sale encouraged false confidence. Groove still lacked enterprise readiness and entered a market focused on cost and security. Changing habits proved harder than building complex software.

His long horizon required delaying interface and positioning decisions while markets changed. Groove abandoned consumer entertainment and concentrated on productivity, accepting enterprise-management burdens. Ozzie initially self-funded, then took outside money partly to build allies, while warning that venture funding starts a treadmill of expectations.

The enduring lesson is to build trusted relationships across disciplines before they become urgent. Technologists need sales, marketing, and leadership partners. Mission should outrank payday, culture reflects founders' behavior, and founders must separate identity from product. Ambition sustained Notes and Groove, but technical difficulty and an impressive first customer never removed market uncertainty.
## Evan Williams and Blogger

Evan Williams arrived in California after teaching himself technology, dropping out of college, and running a painful first company in Nebraska. In 1998 he and Meg Hourihan began Pyra Labs as a web-based project-management venture, financed largely through Williams's HP contracting. Blogger emerged accidentally. Williams and Paul Bausch had scripts for updating personal sites, while Pyra relied on an internal weblog called Stuff more than its elaborate collaboration product. When Hourihan took a vacation, Williams and Bausch spent a week turning that mechanism into Blogger and launched it without her approval.

The side product attracted influential web users while Pyra's main application lagged. Williams concluded that frequent personal publishing was native to the Web, even if Blogger's technology and business model looked trivial. After raising \$500,000, Pyra focused on Blogger, grew to seven people, and chased growth without revenue. The crash exposed that mistake. Acquisition talks failed, payroll stopped, and in early 2001 everyone left except Williams. A candid server-fund appeal brought roughly \$17,000 for hardware, but he still learned Linux administration and Java while keeping the free service alive.

Small contracts from KnowNow and Dan Bricklin's Trellix bought time. Williams then charged users to remove ads, launched Blogger Pro, rebuilt a team, and secured a Brazilian licensing deal. By 2002 Google approached; four months later it acquired Pyra, though Williams had also considered fresh financing and worried about surrendering control.

His lesson is not blind stubbornness. Blogger survived through luck, reduced costs, user goodwill, and failed deals that later looked fortunate. Williams believed founders sometimes must protect intuition from compromise, attend to colleagues' feelings, and recognize how far a simple product can travel.
## Tim Brady and Yahoo

Tim Brady knew Jerry Yang from Stanford, where they studied electrical engineering, then reconnected in Japan before Brady entered Harvard Business School. In late 1994 Yang showed him the link directory he and David Filo had built while neglecting doctoral work. Traffic was multiplying and the hobby was becoming a business. Brady turned their ideas into a business plan for two classes. When Yahoo needed him before graduation, he submitted it, accepted failures in three other courses allowed by Harvard's rules, and flew west as the first employee.

The four-person team began around Yang and Filo's Stanford trailer. Sequoia invested \$1 million, but the immediate ambition was to keep the service alive and sell advertising to cover costs. Netscape's free directory-button link sent traffic soaring. Yahoo added graphics despite slow modems, sold ads, brought Reuters news online, and hired Tim Koogle after a six-month CEO search. During a May 1995 blackout, the team fueled a diesel generator around the clock and held candlelit meetings under a leaking roof.

As product leader, Brady treated Yahoo as an open connection point rather than AOL's walled garden. Failed searches linked directly to competitors with the query already filled in. Yahoo rented full-text search from successive partners while concentrating on its curated directory, a sound strategy until Google became decisively better. The team also missed Hotmail, then corrected by acquiring Rocketmail.

Brady remembered sixteen-to-eighteen-hour days and unusual trust among early hires. His lesson was to define motivations, limits, and friendship ground rules before pressure arrives. Yahoo benefited from timing and Internet growth, yet correcting errors without ego and prioritizing users over trapping traffic helped it remain on that wave.
## Mike Lazaridis and Research In Motion

Mike Lazaridis began building with Doug Fregin in high school, where access to donated electronics depended on reading each manual first. Their shop work connected mathematics to oscilloscopes, radio, and computer trainers. A teacher warned that an important breakthrough would come from joining computers with wireless technology. At Waterloo, Lazaridis encountered email, ARPANET, networks, and real-time systems years before they became mainstream.

During the 1984 recession, classmates challenged his confidence by asking why he did not start a company. He did within weeks, called Fregin, and left university just short of graduation after winning a \$600,000 General Motors contract. RIM built a rugged local-area network and centrally booted displays for hostile factories, using a broken surplus computer Lazaridis repaired. The founders shared an apartment and leased Honda Civic while grants and contracts paid bills.

The direction appeared in 1987. A conference described Japanese vending machines reporting when they needed refilling, reviving his teacher's prediction. Soon RIM made the Swedish Mobitex system work for Cantel and developed protocols, APIs, and tools. That shifted the company from consulting toward products. Lazaridis later declined a glamorous but tiny Canadarm2 contract because only six boards might be needed, choosing the scalable wireless market instead.

Years of research led to an interactive pager plan written in his basement after settling his newborn son. RIM disguised two-way email in the familiar pager form, then launched BlackBerry into New York's financial market in 1999. Lazaridis's lesson is preparation, not prophecy: vision required faith, economic packaging, timing, and customers. Proximity to Waterloo and investment in co-op students supplied talent, while focus kept side projects from diluting the reliable, secure, power-efficient core.
## Arthur van Hoff and Marimba

Arthur van Hoff left Sun's Java team in 1996 with Sami Shaio, Jonathan Payne, and Kim Polese, although they had no product. Each invested about \$25,000. They rented rooms above a Palo Alto flower shop, hauled \$25 metal desks upstairs, furnished the office for roughly \$1,400, and brainstormed without Internet access. Van Hoff saw the team's ability as the real asset: an initial idea was merely a catalyst for a better second one.

Their first plan, a Java interface builder, collided with a similar startup acquired by Netscape. They abandoned it and addressed a problem they knew from Sun: distributing and automatically updating software. The early subscription model was confused with PointCast-style push technology. Public fascination with the Java veterans, especially Polese, opened executive doors but obscured what Marimba sold.

Kleiner Perkins supplied a \$4 million first round. One founder left the checks in a Kinko's copier, showing that prestigious backing did not confer operational maturity. Marimba grew from four people to forty in its first year and about three hundred by its 1999 IPO. Its market evolved from consumer distribution toward enterprise deployments, where Morgan Stanley and Bear Stearns needed reliable updates across enormous fleets.

Growth exposed management limits. Strong sales and finance executives deadlocked under an inexperienced chief executive until veteran John Olsen replaced Polese. Van Hoff does not equate founding talent with scaling talent. His lesson is to assemble people with an unfair capability, stay flexible about the first plan, and learn to sell the eventual one. He also stresses clean intellectual-property separation from former employers: success makes old ownership claims dangerous.
## Paul Buchheit and Gmail

Paul Buchheit considered web-based email in college, when checking messages meant returning to his dorm room, but his experiment went nowhere. Years later, as Google's twenty-third employee, he finished the first Google Groups generation and received a loose assignment involving email or personalization. Google was identified with search, so the proposal was controversial. Buchheit reused Groups code and built a one-day prototype that searched his mail. Colleagues found even that limited version useful.

For a long time Gmail was mostly Buchheit, then Sanjeev Singh and Jing Lim, before growing to about a dozen people. The team treated annoyances as clues. Search and a one-gigabyte quota reduced the need to delete messages. Conversation view reassembled replies, autocomplete accelerated addressing, and JavaScript created a responsive interface when browsers still associated it with pop-ups. These choices looked obvious only afterward.

Email differed dangerously from web search: delivery had to appear immediately, and lost data could not be crawled again. An early disk failure left everyone's internal mail inaccessible. Buchheit entered the machine room with screwdrivers, transplanted electronics from another drive, and recovered everything. Invitations later controlled growth and abuse while storage strengthened. Buchheit stayed awake roughly seventy hours before Gmail's April 2004 launch, whose date and extraordinary storage offer made some observers suspect a joke.

His method was cheap experimentation under uncertainty. A Friday prototype proved content-targeted advertising possible and catalyzed AdSense, though others built the production system and many experiments failed. Gmail likewise benefited from Google's people and infrastructure. The lesson is not solitary genius, but testing disputed ideas quickly, listening to users, and knowing when established lessons apply or a new problem demands different engineering.
## Steve Perlman and WebTV

Steve Perlman's WebTV grew from years of trying to make television interactive. Apple work produced display and video techniques, while Catapult Entertainment taught him hardware, networking, retail distribution, and the cost of misaligned cofounders. After seeing consumer sites in Netscape, he realized the Web might solve interactive television's content problem. He bought about \$3,000 in parts and, over three days and two nights in 1995, built a prototype that rendered computer pages sharply on television. Bruce Leak immediately proposed a company; Phil Goldman joined them.

Hollywood financier Marvin Davis supplied \$1.5 million, and the team moved from Perlman's dining room into an abandoned BMW dealership. WebTV built nearly everything: custom hardware, a browser fitting two megabytes of memory, remote-control interaction, image processing, proxy servers, and nationwide dial-up access. Sony discussed an exclusive manufacturing deal but stalled, the founders hired more than thirty people, and Perlman mortgaged his house as cash disappeared.

Brentwood Venture Capital committed \$4.5 million even after Sony withdrew. That confidence attracted Paul Allen's matching investment and brought Philips forward. A consultant reopened Sony, producing a terrifying demonstration: its chief technology officer arrived while an untested build was still compiling. It worked perfectly. Before a Tokyo meeting, Leak and a remote engineer added Japanese support overnight, and that demonstration also succeeded. Sony and Philips signed, and WebTV shipped in July 1996.

Perlman cautions that luck mattered, while the first \$329 price plus subscription was a mistake; sales improved at \$99. His lesson is that ambitious execution rests on founders aligned on vision and trust. Technical breadth mattered, but that bond carried WebTV through rejection, scarcity, and uncertainty.
## Mike Ramsay and TiVo

Scottish-born Mike Ramsay came to the United States through Hewlett-Packard in the 1970s and stayed for Silicon Valley's opportunity. He met Jim Barton at HP, followed him to Silicon Graphics, and watched Barton master real-time UNIX and video-on-demand while Ramsay worked with entertainment customers. After both left SGI, recurring lunches produced a broad plan for home servers and networks. Only NEA's Stewart Alsop and Redpoint's Geoff Yang funded the capital-intensive consumer service.

The founders admitted networking was immature and a home server difficult to explain. They chose one application, the digital video recorder, and returned to investors with a changed plan. What sounded like a VCR demanded inexpensive simultaneous recording and playback, accurate program guides, personalized schedules, secure telephone downloads, and remote recovery. Ramsay pressed engineers to test ordinary recording six months before release; it failed, validating his concern and forcing a late repair.

TiVo shipped on the last day of March 1999, celebrated as the Blue Moon holiday. Sixty employees demonstrated units in Bay Area Fry's stores, learning retail firsthand. Consumers struggled to imagine pausing live television until they saw it, while Season Pass and WishList made recording persistent and personal. High prices slowed adoption, yet publicity, an IPO, and partnerships built momentum.

The danger was not merely technical. Replay competed aggressively and crossed broadcasters' line with automatic commercial skipping and program sharing. TiVo avoided those features, endured lawsuit threats, and eventually received investment from media companies that initially called it destructive. Ramsay credits heavy capitalization with surviving the crash. His lesson is to narrow an overbroad vision, hide complexity behind simplicity, test supposedly easy functions early, and innovate without ignoring powerful stakeholders.
## Paul Graham and Viaweb

Paul Graham and Robert Morris first tried putting art galleries online, then noticed galleries did not want it. Because their software generated gallery sites, adding commerce offered a more promising market. In Morris's apartment during summer 1995, they planned desktop software until Graham woke with the idea of running it on a server and controlling it through a browser. Within weeks they had what Graham considered the first web-based application and a working store demo.

Julian Weber invested \$10,000, and Trevor Blackwell joined. Asked to build an image tool, Blackwell disappeared for two weeks and returned having rewritten the system in Smalltalk; Graham hired him but kept the existing architecture. The team feared sales, so early bookstore customers received free software, hand-built sites, and scanned images. Those encounters corrected another assumption: catalog companies resisted the Web, while specialty merchants valued capabilities previously reserved for large firms.

Viaweb emphasized ease and graphic design over transaction processing. Customer growth was smooth, but financing and acquisitions were chaotic. An early \$3 million offer vanished after an investor countered aggressively. Later, an employee's intellectual-property agreement derailed another deal and enabled angels to propose a punishing recapitalization. The founders threatened to leave, secured a better round, and survived. Fred Egan's arrival as chief operating officer was pivotal because he handled investors without directing programmers.

Yahoo acquired Viaweb in 1998 after failed approaches, validating the product but dissolving a team during integration. Graham's caveat is that deals routinely collapse until money arrives. His lesson from Artix and Viaweb: make something people demonstrably want, spend little, learn from users, and let truthful claims rest on a product strong enough to support them.
## Joshua Schachter and del.icio.us

Joshua Schachter's bookmarking service grew from a filing problem, not a startup plan. While running Memepool, he accumulated about 20,000 links in a text file. He marked them with descriptions such as a hash sign followed by “math,” then used grep to retrieve related items. In 2001 he turned that habit into Muxway, a private tool whose public pages unexpectedly attracted roughly 10,000 daily readers. Del.icio.us, launched broadly in late 2003, made it multiplayer.

Schachter kept his Morgan Stanley job while the site spread by word of mouth. He designed code he could understand and improve in fifteen-minute sessions, accepting one useful change a day or a couple per week. By late 2004 del.icio.us had 30,000 users, evidence for a concept earlier startups had failed to commercialize. As his group broke apart and acquisition interest rose, he left in 2005, raised modest funding, and built an eight-person company. Yahoo acquired it that December.

Growth exposed less glamorous work: payroll vendors resisted tiny teams, MySQL tables crashed, and scaling beyond one database proved difficult. A week after the acquisition, a data-center power failure corrupted every machine and caused about forty-eight hours of downtime. Schachter learned to assume failure, shorten recovery, and outsource necessary work that does not differentiate the product.

His product discipline was equally spare. Feature requests often describe a familiar solution rather than the underlying need, so he investigated before building. Earlier elegant but complicated projects taught him that difficult interfaces defeat clever architecture. His central advice was reduction: execute ideas, question conventions selectively, and do as little as possible to solve the real problem.
## Mark Fletcher, ONElist, and Bloglines

Mark Fletcher built ONElist and Bloglines to remove personal annoyances. ONElist began in 1997 because creating a family mailing list required installed software and an always-connected computer. He developed it nights and weekends while employed at Sun, protecting his mortgage and checking ownership issues. Five people sustained it alongside day jobs; after eleven months it had one million users, despite scaling failures, three months with registrations closed, and weekend support backlogs reaching 500 messages.

A \$4 million round let the team quit. Two weeks later, investors sought to replace Fletcher as CEO. He resisted because he identified himself with the company, but later judged the replacement beneficial: the new CEO helped take eGroups to a Yahoo acquisition. The episode taught him the cost of negotiating unfamiliar terms without mentors and of confusing a founder's ego with company needs.

After Yahoo, Fletcher tried an anti-spam company, then shifted to a private tool that checked roughly 100 favorite sites through RSS. Desktop aggregators did not suit multiple computers, so his server background suggested a web service. Bloglines launched in June 2003, received press within weeks, and benefited because reporters used it while blogs surged. Users averaged four daily sessions.

Fletcher self-funded Bloglines with about \$200,000 and reused trusted colleagues, keeping it tiny until Ask Jeeves acquired it in 2005. Looming competition, slower-than-desired growth, and buyer interest made selling reasonable, though timing remained a personal judgment. His lessons are to host rather than own hardware, release a small product, respond visibly to users, seek professional help, and remember that users cannot simply be copied.
## Craig Newmark and craigslist

Craigslist began in early 1995 when Craig Newmark emailed perhaps ten or twelve friends about San Francisco arts and technology events. Recipients called it Craig's List and requested jobs and items for sale. When the carbon-copy list broke, he moved it to a list server and retained the personal name at users' urging. Later, Perl scripts turned message folders into web pages; Pine remained his database until 1999.

The service grew steadily from Newmark's apartment while he did contract work. At one million monthly page views in 1997, he rejected Microsoft's banner-ad approach because ads would slow the site and conflicted with his values. Community feedback shaped a narrow principle: charge businesses that would otherwise pay more for less effective ads, chiefly selected job and apartment listings. Even at billions of page views, less than one percent was charged.

An attempted volunteer nonprofit arrangement deteriorated. In December 1997, major job posters took Newmark to lunch and told him to assume responsibility. He committed full-time and hired across billing, service, and technology. Personnel mistakes followed when he distrusted his instincts and insufficiently heeded lawyers, though settlements limit his account. Jim Buckmaster helped lead the recovery, became CEO, and let Newmark focus on customer service.

Flagging embodied mutual trust by giving users substantial daily control. Newmark repeatedly blocked one evasive apartment broker until the broker apologized and changed behavior, supporting his belief that bad actors are a minority. Craigslist took no conventional investment and declined acquisition offers; eBay's stake came from a former employee. The lesson is specific, not universal: listen continuously, persist, know your managerial limits, and let values govern growth.
## Caterina Fake and Flickr

Caterina Fake started Ludicorp in 2002 with her husband, Stewart Butterfield, and Jason Classon, two days after their honeymoon. Their intended product was Game Neverending, a lightweight online game centered on social interaction, trading, and messaging. Fake brought years of online-community experience; friends-and-family money and cheap post-crash office space supported the team.

Flickr emerged while the game's back end lagged. Team members adapted existing technology into an instant messenger for sharing objects, then added photographs and built the first version in eight weeks. Chat faced a critical-mass problem, but traction increased when photos gained web pages. This accidental direction avoided assumptions of services that treated sharing as a route to selling prints. Flickr instead joined public blogging, social identity, broadband, and camera phones.

By July 2004, six people could no longer support both products. Although Game Neverending had 20,000 prototype testers, Flickr's momentum forced them to suspend it. Fundraising had been grueling for the game; Flickr brought several venture calls weekly and immediate acquisition interest. Ludicorp accepted angel funding and a Canadian government loan, but no venture capital, because it had runway, approached break-even, and struggled to scale organically. Yahoo acquired Flickr in 2005.

Tagging organized personal photos while surfacing public events and creative groups. When the Australian embassy in Jakarta was bombed, three users among only 60,000 uploaded nearby images within a day. Fake stresses that timing and fortune mattered, and recalls periods when only the employee with children was paid. Her lesson is to follow demonstrated use, embrace constraints, and let an emergent product supersede the original mission.
## Brewster Kahle, WAIS, Alexa, and the Internet Archive

Brewster Kahle learned at Thinking Machines to prototype ambitious technology during research and choose surroundings that encourage unconventional thought. There he developed WAIS with Apple, Dow Jones, and KPMG Peat Marwick. Before Gopher and the Web, its open protocol let remote machines answer questions and publish information. When Thinking Machines would not build the central tools, Kahle left to create an Internet-publishing industry, not merely a company.

WAIS began with a contract for Ross Perot's 1992 campaign. The campaign collapsed, but its payment funded much of the first year. Free software encouraged about 10,000 servers, while upgrades and consulting supported the business. Work with leading publishers helped put subscription and advertising services online. Yet cash usually covered only two to four weeks. AOL acquired the roughly thirty-five-person operation in 1995, then dispersed it while using Kahle for strategy, an outcome useful to AOL but unsatisfying to him.

In 1996 Kahle and Bruce Gilliat formed Alexa alongside the nonprofit Internet Archive. Alexa's toolbar recommended related pages from anonymous browsing trails, while collected web material went to an institution intended to outlast companies. The team deleted sensitive trail data. Millions used Alexa, but contextual advertising failed. Amazon bought it and preserved it separately; its frugal practices later helped Amazon reduce hardware and connectivity costs.

The Archive was Kahle's long-term goal: a library giving everyone access to everything. Large visions move slowly, he cautions, so each year needs a concrete accomplishment. His path shows that open systems, demonstrations, compatible partners, and bootstrap discipline can establish a field, even when the business model fails or better competitors take over.
## Charles Geschke and Adobe

At Xerox PARC, Charles Geschke and John Warnock developed Interpress so computers and printers could communicate. PARC demonstrated networked personal computers, graphical editing, and laser printing in 1977, yet Xerox executives reacted cautiously. When management said commercialization would take at least seven years, the pair concluded several technology generations would pass. In 1982, they left secure jobs in their early forties and founded Adobe with venture backing and a \$50,000 loan.

Their plan was to manufacture publishing systems. Digital Equipment and Steve Jobs separately said customers needed only the connecting software. A board adviser urged them to discard the funded plan and follow demand. Adobe licensed PostScript to Apple for the LaserWriter; prepaid royalties and Apple's investment helped Adobe profit within its first year. PostScript's mathematical outlines rendered scalable, high-quality fonts across devices, advancing beyond hand-tuned bitmaps.

The LaserWriter nearly died when Apple sales managers objected that a printer cost more than a Macintosh. Jobs proceeded after memory prices fell. Sales later weakened until Apple, Aldus, and Adobe promoted PageMaker and the printer as “desktop publishing,” creating a new market. Adobe diversified through Illustrator, inspired when Warnock tired of hand-coding his designer wife's artwork, and Photoshop, acquired before scanners and digital cameras were broadly affordable.

Adobe survived Apple and Microsoft's 1989 font alliance by shipping Adobe Type Manager quickly and cheaply on both platforms. Publishing professionals overwhelmingly supported Adobe after the announcement. Geschke cautions that successful products and channels eventually decay. His lessons are to listen beyond literal requests, anticipate markets, diversify before necessity, and build a company where passionate people can sustain work and life.
## Ann Winblad and Open Systems

Ann Winblad studied mathematics, business, and computer science. After thirteen months at the Federal Reserve Bank, rapid promotion convinced her she could recover if a venture failed. In 1976 she left with three colleagues to start Open Systems, though two retained sabbatical safety nets. Incorporation exhausted her savings, so she borrowed \$500 from her high-school-aged brother.

With no startup funding, the founders consulted by day and developed their product separately. A Minnesota vocational-school contract paid them to learn estimation, management, and scheduling. They then reopened Winblad's accounting textbook and built software for smaller computers, moving early from minicomputers toward commodity Intel processors and avoiding the collapse of many proprietary platforms.

They learned sales experimentally. At a CADO reseller meeting, Winblad stood on a crate before sixty or seventy older men and offered unlimited module rights for \$10,000 that day. When attendees lacked corporate checkbooks, she requested personal checks with delayed deposits. She left with roughly twelve checks and phoned from the airport carrying \$120,000 in her handbag. Some buyers may simply have wanted the young company to succeed.

Operations brought education. A fire destroyed Winblad's memorabilia but spared the computers, prompting a move from their apartment office. A solid-oak trade-show booth needed emergency neon repair after a worker fell inside its tower, while software was shrink-wrapped on a used pizza machine that smelled of burned cheese.

Winblad says inexperience was tiring rather than crushing because the market allowed mistakes. Her later investor's lesson is that most failures are self-inflicted leadership wounds, not competitive defeats. Founders must evolve into leaders responsible for employees, customers, shareholders, mission, and values, while finding leverage toward an ambitious strategy.
## David Heinemeier Hansson and 37signals

David Heinemeier Hansson joined 37signals as a contract programmer while finishing his degree. The consultancy's client work was disorganized, so the team adapted blogging into a project log. Within a month, they used Basecamp to build Basecamp. Colleagues wanted it too, revealing a market between email and heavyweight tools such as Microsoft Project.

Consulting revenue funded development, but Hansson had ten hours weekly and designers at most a third of their time. Constraints became strategy. Basecamp offered a weblog, milestones, files, and to-do lists, omitting billing and time tracking. Its generality opened uses from weddings to student collaboration. A free plan, low monthly upgrades, an existing blog audience, and word of mouth replaced advertising.

Three days before launch, the bank refused annual billing because the company might fail before delivering a year. Rebuilding for monthly payments delayed release a month, but enabled higher annualized prices with less customer risk. Other assumptions cost more: a one-firm, one-client model took eighteen months to repair, and Central Time defaults made Australian milestones arrive a day late.

To remain the sole programmer, Hansson moved from PHP to Ruby and extracted Basecamp code into Rails. Basecamp launched at 4,000 lines; Rails had 1,000. He delayed Rails to provide documentation, then released it as open source. Its focus on the common eighty percent reflected Basecamp's discipline.

37signals rejected venture funding and tried to scale users without scaling staff, though Hansson acknowledged this does not fit every company and did not rule out an acquisition. His lesson is not mere minimalism: constraints should trigger rescoping, product judgment, rapid shipping, and visible improvements after launch rather than attempts to satisfy every request.
## Philip Greenspun and ArsDigita

ArsDigita grew from software Philip Greenspun wrote for photo.net, the photography community that evolved from his travelogue, Travels with Samantha. He released the ArsDigita Community System as open source, then discovered that large companies would pay for customization even with thousands of programmers. Greenspun and friends formed a company, working from a photographer's rented house after MIT banned dogs. They won repeat business by listening, delivering on time, and giving small programming teams responsibility for projects and profits.

Levi Strauss funded broadly reusable infrastructure, while Greenspun's book, free tutorials, lectures, and seminars supplied education-driven marketing. By 2000 ArsDigita had roughly \$20 million in revenue, several million in profit, and rapid growth. Yet recruiting business executives and pursuing an IPO pushed it toward venture capital. Greylock and General Atlantic invested \$38 million and installed managers who separated sales, programming, project management, and client service. Greenspun believed this erased accountability. After being marginalized, he used his majority shareholder rights to change the board and restore himself as CEO, prompting costly litigation and an eventual buyout.

The company then burned through about \$40 million and pursued an ambitious Java rewrite. Management discouraged customers from using the proven system while its replacement arrived more than a year late, ran dramatically slower, and remained difficult to adopt. ArsDigita collapsed in 2002. Greenspun's account is openly partisan, and he concedes that he grew too quickly, managed harshly, and could have treated people more gently. His central lessons are still concrete: grow only as fast as culture can be transmitted, test software on real users, preserve clear profit responsibility, and deliver the value a customer actually bought.
## Joel Spolsky and Fog Creek Software

Joel Spolsky and Michael Pryor founded Fog Creek in 2000 without a defining product. Inspired partly by ArsDigita, they planned to finance scalable software with consulting while creating a programmer-centered workplace in New York. Joel on Software, written during Spolsky's summer off, supplied their first three clients. Then the dot-com collapse erased that market almost invisibly: sales cycles lengthened until the founders recognized no new work was coming.

Their small size saved them. An internal bug tracker was closest to sale, so they shipped it as FogBugz while two other ideas, CityDesk and the unbuilt Tintin, disappointed or vanished. FogBugz soon brought in roughly \$5,000 to \$10,000 monthly, enough to cover a lean office and basic expenses after two consultants were laid off. Spolsky invested about \$50,000 and sometimes took no salary. Each stronger release produced not a temporary sales hump but a lasting step upward. Counterintuitively, raising the price also increased unit sales by making the product appear substantial rather than shareware.

Fog Creek preserved private offices, excellent equipment, generous leave, and programmer management because the founders believed concentration and respect improved recruiting and output. They rejected outside investment to retain those choices. Spolsky admits they wasted scarce development time on affiliates, coupons, and imagined marketing partnerships; none matched the return from improving the product and listening directly to customers. The popular blog gave Fog Creek unusual publicity, but he warns that this route is not readily reproducible. His practical advice is narrower: commit full-time, recruit at least one real cofounder, simplify aggressively, and build an organization capable of repeatedly turning useful ideas into working software.
## Stephen Kaufer and TripAdvisor

TripAdvisor began after Stephen Kaufer and his wife struggled to find candid vacation information. Booking pages abounded, but useful warnings hid in chat rooms and personal sites. In 2000 Kaufer joined business-oriented Langley Steinert and two former colleagues to build a travel search engine. Crawling produced too much noise, so people manually read, summarized, and classified articles. The database was small but unusually relevant; later user reviews became fresher and more detailed.

The original model failed. TripAdvisor.com was a demonstration for portals that would license its content, yet prospects wanted payment instead. A Lycos deal earned only hundreds per quarter, and after September 11 the eight-person company faced extinction. Meanwhile the demo attracted about 5,000 daily visitors. Generic banners paid almost nothing, but a reader viewing a hotel was declaring purchase intent. Deep-linked Expedia ads led to a free test and a \$10,000 monthly order. Relevant links achieved click rates near 10 percent, moving TripAdvisor from no revenue to break-even in four months.

Kaufer credits small teams, restrained spending, complementary founders, and flexibility more than fidelity to the initial vision. He also refused a potentially lifesaving licensing deal that would have handed a customer the core database after two years. User reviews introduced caveats: TripAdvisor screened submissions for guidelines and fraud but did not verify every claim, while hotels could publish responses. His lessons are to test cheaply, accept small failures, protect essential assets, and hire exceptional people slowly, since stronger contributors can produce multiples of ordinary output.
## James Hong and HOT or NOT

HOT or NOT started as a joke among James Hong, Jim Young, and Hong's brother after Young called a woman a perfect ten. Hong imagined a service where people submitted photographs and strangers rated them. With Hong unemployed and Young exhausted by his doctorate, they built it within days. Hong's retired father became absorbed in the prototype. After Hong emailed forty friends, the site received about 40,000 first-day hits and spread unaided.

Popularity created an immediate crisis. Hosted images implied roughly \$150,000 in annual bandwidth while Hong had student debt. In an In-N-Out drive-through, he realized users could host pictures on Yahoo GeoCities; hours later the database pointed there. They hid a free 400-megahertz computer under Young's desk, protected its switch with a pushpin case, and persuaded Rackspace to provide machines for publicity. Hong slept eight hours during the first eight days as press overwhelmed the service.

Pornographic submissions threatened advertising, so the founders developed public moderation around a promise that the site would be fun, clean, and real. Rejected contact details revealed that users wanted introductions, inspiring the Meet Me feature. Charging six dollars monthly turned an accidental anti-spam response into a dating business earning about half a million dollars annually at first. An Ofoto affiliate arrangement even converted image hosting from expense into revenue. Hong declined an early multimillion-dollar Lycos offer because he expected a corporate owner to damage the experience. He stresses that the path was improvised, not visionary: young founders benefit from low obligations, lean spending, broad early skills, rapid problem solving, and willingness to keep moving when no plan survives contact with users.
## James Currier and Tickle

James Currier's idea for Tickle, originally Emode, emerged from a Harvard Business School personality test. Eleven days after filling paper circles, students received plain reports and discussed them for weeks. Currier concluded that the Internet could create media about ordinary people, rather than republish radio or magazines. Tests could spread cheaply because people compared results. He organized psychologists, writers, and engineers around online self-assessment.

The serious tests on anxiety, parenting, and relationships attracted almost nobody. The turning point was deliberately frivolous: a fifteen-question quiz identifying which dog breed resembled the user. Eight days later, one million people were trying to enter the site, forcing the team to unplug its server, drive it to an Internet provider, and connect it to a faster line. Traffic unlocked venture interest after forty-three pitches, even though Currier found investors more receptive to an inflated \$50 million forecast than his candid \$18 million projection. Women.com offered \$32 million once term sheets supplied external credibility.

The harder problems involved people and strain. Currier hired an engineering leader who required a marshal nearby when dismissed, and approved a maternity policy proposed by an HR chief who soon used it while founders went unpaid. He remembers waking at four with chest pain from fear, sleeping four hours nightly, and nearly losing his future wife. A trusted replacement engineering leader created breathing room. Currier says startups require obsession, cross-disciplinary communication, and teammates who are kind, articulate, smart, and capable. Tickle's acquisition by Monster worked because Monster preserved its team and mission, but his enthusiasm carries a caveat: entrepreneurship can consume relationships and a life.
## Blake Ross and Firefox

Blake Ross joined Mozilla in 2000 at fourteen, fixed bugs, and interned at Netscape. After AOL's acquisition, demands for revenue turned Netscape's browser into a vehicle for advertisements and portal traffic. Pop-up protection was blocked because Netscape.com used pop-ups. Ross, David Hyatt, Joe Hewitt, and allies began Phoenix after hours, sometimes at Denny's, to make a fast, simple browser. They restricted control because neither corporate departments nor developer consensus reliably served ordinary consumers.

Trademark conflicts changed Phoenix to Firebird and finally Firefox. The first milestone, quietly placed on an FTP server and mentioned by mozillaZine, matched Mozilla's download volume. That response split the community: developers resisted attention to nontechnical users, while others welcomed a mainstream product. After Netscape separated Mozilla into an independent organization, direct distribution became necessary. Firefox 1.0 launched in 2004 with grassroots marketing. Spread Firefox gave students and other noncoders practical roles, drawing more than 250,000 participants, while 10,000 donors funded facing advertisements in the New York Times.

Ross defined success through users, not defeating Microsoft. Firefox had unusual freedom because it was free, supported by donations and seed money, and could fail without ruining anyone. That caveat distinguishes it from conventional startups. Still, it disproved claims that browsers were finished or users would not download software. People valued simplicity, tabs, and a product worth recommending. Ross learned to consult eventual users, use technical advocates to reach broader press, and avoid hype until something works. In his next startup, attention created pressure; time, trustworthy advice, and careful decisions became scarcer than capital.
## Mena Trott and Six Apart

Mena Trott began Dollarshort in 2001 as a creative outlet and a way to connect. After she and her husband Ben lost their jobs, dissatisfaction with blogging tools led them to build Movable Type in their apartment. They expected donationware and gifts, not a company. Yet about 2,000 people awaited the October launch, and users made the project a full-time obligation. Donations and keys for appearing in a recently updated list brought break-even within two months.

Consulting briefly seemed promising, but unpaid work and unwanted customization pushed them toward hosted service TypePad. Working back to back in a spare bedroom for eighteen months blurred marriage and work; they sometimes spent no more than six hours apart. Investor Joi Ito approached as a user, and Barak Berkowitz challenged their ambition. A trip to Japan and fear that Blogger would gain backing changed their minds. Neoteny invested under a million dollars; Berkowitz supplied operational guidance and eventually became CEO.

The Trotts guarded money so intensely that they delayed hiring, offices, and a refrigerator, then exhausted themselves building TypePad alone and replacing hardware. Mena later saw a middle course between waste and harmful austerity. Six Apart also mishandled Movable Type licensing by restricting personal users while trying to charge businesses profiting from the software; the backlash showed how payment changes community expectations. Trott nevertheless rejects apologizing for building a sustainable company. Her account keeps the cost visible: pressure once left her crying in the shower, and public criticism curtailed her blogging. The durable lessons are to build something personally useful, charge ethically, delegate before health collapses, and accept guidance without surrendering the product knowledge that created demand.
## Bob Davis and Lycos

Lycos began with Michael Mauldin's federally funded Carnegie Mellon search project. Mauldin preferred research, so CMGI acquired most of the venture while he and the university retained stakes. Bob Davis, an unhappy mainframe-memory sales executive, volunteered as CEO before the company existed. In June 1995 he led technology with no employees, customers, or products. He assembled a team and debated whether Lycos was a technology licensor, media company, or both; the hybrid soon became an advertising-supported consumer brand.

Lycos positioned itself as an approachable guide for newcomers. Its first advertiser, AT&T, signed a euphoric \$5,000 order, revealing that Lycos had no ad-serving system. Engineers built one in about ten days. Licensing with AT&T, CompuServe, and Prodigy added cash and visibility, while Bertelsmann helped create native-language European sites. Public relations, partnerships, employee evangelism, and a family-sedan identity grew the audience from tens of thousands toward over 100 million monthly users.

The company had only about \$1.25 million in practical working capital and owed Carnegie Mellon half of its first \$1.5 million in revenue. It traded promotional credit for discounted hardware and became profitable unusually early. Rapid growth continually broke offices, data centers, accounting systems, and organizational assumptions. A proposed 1999 merger with USA Networks collapsed amid shareholder opposition, leaving Davis overwhelmed but giving Lycos enormous publicity as it overtook Yahoo. A year later Terra bought it for \$5.4 billion. Davis cautions that no business follows its original blueprint: daily adversity, strong hiring, simultaneous priorities, profitability, and persistent self-promotion mattered more than a single turning point or uniquely superior search technology.
## Ron Gruner, Alliant, and Shareholder.com

Ron Gruner spent thirteen years at Data General, progressing from design to management in its competitive culture. In 1982 he, Craig Mundie, and Rich McAndrew founded Alliant to build parallel computers that ran existing programs faster. They spent months researching, then raised about \$30 million including from Kleiner Perkins. After three years, custom chips and a Fortran compiler proved the architecture. Revenue climbed from roughly \$5 million to \$30 million in a year, and Alliant went public in 1986.

Success proved fragile. High-performance machines were judged largely by speed per dollar, so a late product generation was punishing. Workstations displaced centralized systems while defense demand shrank with the Cold War. Gruner favored gradual transition; the board preferred a riskier architectural change. He left after ten years, accepting responsibility for an unhealthy company; Alliant failed the next year. His next venture required recurring revenue, low capital needs, customer focus, organic growth, and founder control.

Shareholder.com began in 1992 with \$276,000 from friends. Gruner adapted telephone personal ads into toll-free investor-information lines, replacing mailed reports. Advance quarterly subscriptions and outsourced infrastructure avoided capital expense. Profitable in 1994, it embraced the Web and benefited from disclosure rules broadening investor access. Its scariest episode came when a pharmaceutical client printed one million reports with the wrong number. A private investigator found the pager owner, who transferred it for two family subscriptions, preserving the mailing and strengthening the relationship. Gruner concedes extreme bootstrapping may have slowed growth, but values its control. His lesson is persistence: a damaging incident can create loyalty if handled honestly and completely.

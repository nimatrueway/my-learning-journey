# Founders at Work: Stories of Startups' Early Days

Jessica Livingston

## Foreword

Paul Graham compares startups to sprinters: their fastest, most productive period often comes immediately after the start, when only a few founders are present and the largest ideas take shape. Apple at the moment when every employee was either Steve Jobs or Steve Wozniak captures the intensity of that stage. Yet it looks almost nothing like the conventional image of business, with suits, conference tables, presentations, and thick reports.

Graham argues that this disconnect reflects a broader principle. When performance is weak, people often spend more energy on appearing impressive, and that effort can make real work harder. He recalls a car magazine making a production sports car faster by stripping away the decorative equipment added to make it look fast. Much of corporate professionalism, in his view, works like that unnecessary equipment. Formal clothes do not improve thought, and executives may have their best ideas at home on a quiet Sunday morning rather than in an office designed to signal productivity.

His own startup exposed the contradiction. Before outsiders visited, the team cleaned the office, dressed better, and tried to ensure that people were visible during normal hours. The actual programming happened amid clutter, in bad clothes, often at two in the morning. Graham even remembers programming in a towel. Despite producing results, the founders felt like impostors because their process looked unprofessional to visitors and investors. They had, in his analogy, built a Formula One car while worrying that it did not resemble an ordinary sedan.

Few people witness this reality because the dramatic growth usually occurs when a startup has only three or four people. By contrast, tens of thousands see the established practices of major corporations, so ceremony becomes mistaken for business itself. The interviews in this book are meant to correct that imbalance by opening a view into startups' first year and showing what concentrated productivity actually looks like.

Graham does not claim that large organizations can copy everything. Their scale brings more politics, coordination, and limits on individual decisions. Still, early startups offer a useful direction of travel. Instead of founders disguising themselves as corporations, established companies might learn to remove some performance theater and become more like startups. The result would look unusual by traditional standards, but could work considerably better.
## Introduction

Jessica Livingston began with a simple problem: the formative period of a startup can seem almost magical, yet usually only its founders witness it. She therefore asked successful founders to reconstruct those beginnings in their own words. Across their accounts, she looked for common qualities and found a picture that differs sharply from the mythology surrounding famous companies.

The founders were often unsure that they had discovered anything important. Some companies began almost accidentally, and many founders hesitated before committing to a business. Their confidence centered less on eventual greatness than on making something good or repairing something broken. Livingston identifies determination as their most consistent exceptional trait. Startup life brought uncertainty, isolation, slow stretches, and plans that repeatedly failed, but these founders kept building.

They also endured rejection from investors, journalists, and established companies. Innovation is admired in the abstract but frequently dismissed in concrete form because it does not fit familiar categories. Web-based email, now ordinary, once seemed commercially unpromising. The caveat is that perseverance alone is insufficient: because almost nothing proceeds according to plan, founders must also adapt rather than merely defend their first idea.

The interviews repeatedly show companies changing direction. PayPal began with encryption software and handheld devices, Excite with database search, and Flickr emerged from an online game. Startup formation is therefore closer to trial and error than to the growth of a perfect original seed. What helped founders choose among experiments was empathy for users. However often the product changed, they kept asking whether they were making something people would actually want.

Money mattered and successful founders gained financial freedom, but Livingston found pride in craftsmanship and a desire to affect the world to be stronger recurring motives. Many went on to equally ambitious projects rather than retiring from creation. Their behavior also reveals how different young startups are from established companies. Early productivity can look disorganized and unbusinesslike precisely because it is stripped of much of the ceremony associated with corporate work.

Livingston's aim is not to turn these histories into a guaranteed formula. Instead, she wants to preserve a shared fund of experience, including recurring problems and lessons founders wished they had known earlier. Retrospective success can make exceptional people seem like a separate species and conceal their original uncertainty. Seeing the rejections, pivots, accidents, and ordinary starting conditions may make entrepreneurship less daunting. The central invitation is not to imitate every decision, but to recognize that celebrated companies were built by people who once stood at an unclear beginning and learned by continuing.

That record may also help established organizations. If young startups appear unbusinesslike while producing unusually valuable work, conventional business could benefit from paying closer attention to what they do and discarding some habits that merely look productive.

## Max Levchin and PayPal

Max Levchin reached Silicon Valley in the summer of 1998 after finishing college in Champaign-Urbana, where he had already started three companies and chosen a startup over graduate school. Squatting on a friend's floor and unsure what to build, he wandered into a nearly empty Stanford lecture given by Peter Thiel; only six people came because of the heat. Levchin introduced himself afterward, they met for breakfast, and over several weeks Thiel, then running a hedge fund, told him to take the better of his two ideas, promised a couple hundred thousand dollars, and eventually offered to be CEO himself. On January 1, 1999, they agreed: Thiel as CEO, Levchin as CTO.

The founding idea had nothing to do with payments. In college Levchin had bought and reverse-engineered the security tokens that generate one-time passwords, then written a Palm Pilot emulator so system administrators could throw away their pocketful of devices. Thousands of downloads convinced him to sell handheld cryptography, on the assumption that every corporate employee would soon carry a Palm Pilot. That migration, he jokes, never came, like the early Christians still waiting for the second coming. So the company iterated: crypto libraries nobody bought, enterprise software nobody needed, a consumer wallet with nothing to store, then cryptographically secure IOUs beamed between Palm Pilots by infrared. The gimmick was impractical but futuristic enough to raise money. At Buck's restaurant in Woodside, the venture round itself arrived as a \$4.5 million transfer beamed from a preloaded Palm Pilot. Levchin and two programmers coded for five days without sleep to make the demo real rather than faked; it worked at nine in the morning for a ten o'clock showing, and he then fell asleep face-down beside his omelet.

The real business came from an unsexy website demo that let people run the same transactions in a browser. eBay sellers kept asking to place the PayPal logo in their auctions, and the team told them to go away before recognizing that these users were begging to be served. Levchin killed the handheld product at 12,000 users while the website passed 1.2 to 1.5 million, calling it an emotional but obvious decision. Growth also forced a merger with the rival X.com, which turned bitter over whether to run on Windows or Unix; Levchin, a lifelong Unix programmer, even built a load tester to show the Windows prototype had one percent of the scalability. The clash ended with the X.com chief executive leaving and Thiel returning as CEO, at which point Levchin took ownership of fraud.

Fraud nearly destroyed the company. Advisers had warned him about chargebacks he could not even define, and by mid-2000 losses exceeded \$10 million a month. With an intern named Bob whom he persuaded to drop out of Stanford, Levchin built statistical tools and a visualization system called IGOR, one of a set given four-letter Russian names, that traced networks of accounts and ranked cases by expected rather than absolute loss. Combined with human investigators managed by Sarah Imbach from a cheaper Omaha office, this brought fraud under control and became a secret advantage; PayPal kept IGOR in a locked room and never patented it. Levchin describes PayPal as a security company pretending to be a financial services company, one that won through naïveté where incumbents like Citibank feared to bleed publicly and rivals like eMoneyMail simply quit at 25 percent fraud.

Levchin credits luck and his cofounder far more than foresight. The plan changed about six times, viral free-money invitations drove eBay adoption, and he and Thiel took turns talking each other out of despair. He had told Peter he might quit at twenty-five employees; the company reached a thousand. eBay bought PayPal for \$1.5 billion in 2002, then dismissed the rival Billpoint team PayPal had beaten. His lesson is that a good entrepreneur is not wedded to one product but is simply unable to work for anyone else and willing to abandon a cherished idea the moment users point elsewhere.

## Sabeer Bhatia and Hotmail

Sabeer Bhatia met Jack Smith at Apple, where both built PowerBooks, and followed their manager to the startup FirePower Systems to design PowerPC chips meant to outrun Intel. When Intel caught up on price and performance and the manager left, Bhatia drifted toward the unfolding Internet. Watching Stanford classmates raise a million dollars for what he saw as a mere directory, Yahoo, convinced him the Web was permanent, and he sketched JavaSoft, a personal database stored on a server and viewed through a browser. Jack read the plan and signed on the next day. Because Bhatia was single, he told Jack to quit first and paid him half his own salary so Jack could support his family.

The stronger idea came from their own frustration. FirePower's firewall blocked the personal email accounts they used to reach each other, Bhatia's at Stanford and Jack's at AOL, forcing them to trade files on floppy disks and paper. If a browser could reach any website, they reasoned, it could carry email from anywhere. Fearing that a venture capitalist might simply hand the concept to Netscape, they pitched JavaSoft as a screen: only when an investor poked holes in that plan, rather than dismissing them for being young hardware engineers, would they reveal web-based email. Draper Fisher Jurvetson passed the test. Bhatia asked for \$3 million, then half a million, and Tim Draper offered \$300,000 for about 15 percent. Buried in the terms was a right of first refusal that Bhatia, poorly advised, did not understand; it let DFJ hold down his valuation later by quietly warning other investors away, and once left him accepting harsher bridge terms while Draper sat at the Atlanta Olympics and only two weeks of payroll remained.

Funded on Valentine's Day 1996, Hotmail launched on the Fourth of July and reached 100,000 subscribers within three months, spreading by word of mouth amplified by a small PR firm. Jack's idea to append a signup invitation to every outgoing message became a textbook case of viral marketing, though Bhatia bristles that Draper later claimed credit for the tagline and even for web-based email itself. Hourly pager alerts showing signups climb from hundreds to thousands energized the company. Hotmail was never very profitable, but its costs stayed near a million dollars a month, and outsourcing ad sales for a guaranteed million-dollar monthly minimum was enough to break even. Rapid growth still broke servers, and with weak backups reliability took time to master. Consumers baffled them: one salesman's mother could see an email but did not know to click it, and some users reached Hotmail only by searching the word on Yahoo first.

A painful conflict came from Rocketmail, whose user directory Hotmail had licensed rather than build. Seeing the flood of registrations Hotmail sent, Rocketmail, also funded by DFJ, decided to launch its own email service; Hotmail scrapped the partnership, treated it as competition, and learned to share less with its own investor. By mid-1997 Hotmail had about seven million subscribers, more than Microsoft managed for its 2.5 million MSN users. Microsoft flew them to Redmond on October 13, 1997, walked them past Bill Gates, and sat them across a giant table from roughly fifteen negotiators who opened at \$160 million. Coached not to speak, Bhatia thanked them and refused to name a number even when the CFO pushed. Betting that an unassailable subscriber lead could not be replicated by Microsoft's sixteen thousand engineers, he held out until the price reached \$400 million on New Year's Eve, roughly twenty months after founding.

Bhatia sometimes wonders whether licensing Hotmail, as Google later licensed search, might have made it larger, but concludes that email cost far more to run and monetized worse than search. His durable lessons are to own the customer even before monetizing, since a large user base can always be tapped later, and to demand only a small but important change in behavior. Hotmail succeeded because signing up resembled visiting any other website, and every message from an @hotmail.com address advertised the brand for free.

## Steve Wozniak and Apple

Long before Apple, Steve Wozniak taught himself to design computers on paper, competing with himself to remove chips because he could never afford to build one; fewer parts meant lower cost, deeper understanding, and fewer bugs. By day he designed calculators at Hewlett-Packard, a company he loved and expected to stay at for life, and by night in his Cupertino apartment he built a video terminal from a \$60 keyboard and a home television. When the Homebrew Computer Club introduced him to cheap microprocessors, he realized they were just the minicomputers he had been redesigning since high school. Choosing a \$20 MOS 6502 and the new dynamic RAM, he combined them with his terminal to build the Apple I, hand-wrote its Basic without ever having taken a compiler course, and passed out photocopied schematics at Homebrew so others could build their own.

Steve Jobs saw a business where Wozniak saw a hobby. Club members wanted assembled boards, so Jobs proposed selling printed circuit boards, then landed a Byte Shop order for 100 built machines at \$500 each, a \$50,000 deal worth twice Wozniak's annual salary. They funded it on credit, paying suppliers only after the store paid cash. A third partner, Ron Wayne, typed their partnership contract and drew the first logo but, terrified of being liable for the parts debt, sold his 10 percent back for a few hundred dollars even after the Apple II existed. Wozniak first offered the design to HP, whose legal department polled every division and formally declined.

The Apple II made the company. Wozniak halved the chip count of the Apple I while adding color, invented late one exhausted night at Atari from a single cheap chip, along with sound, graphics, and eight expansion slots. Jobs argued that customers needed only two slots, for a printer and a modem; Wozniak, who had watched real users fill machines with cards, refused, telling Jobs to buy a different computer if he wanted two. The openness proved decisive, spawning an entire industry of add-in cards. Commodore's Chuck Peddle, the very man who had sold Wozniak his first 6502, talked his boss out of the machine; Atari was consumed by its home Pong; and venture capitalist Don Valentine, unimpressed by Wozniak's claim that a million ham radio operators proved the market, at least pointed them to Mike Markkula. Markkula invested \$250,000, most of it as a loan, and brought in Mike Scott as a forceful first president, later ousted for rash layoffs.

Markkula insisted Wozniak leave HP, and Wozniak nearly refused, having no wish to run a company. Only when his friend Allen Baum told him he could start Apple and stay an engineer did the block lift. He remains, by his own description, at the bottom of the org chart to this day.

His floppy-disk design shows his method. Having never seen a floppy, he bypassed roughly twenty of Shugart's chips, built a compact state machine, and handed Jobs a bargaining lever for a cheaper drive. Racing to demonstrate it at a Las Vegas show, he and Randy Wigginton worked until six in the morning, whereupon Wozniak accidentally overwrote the good disk while backing it up, slept a few hours, and recreated everything from his handwritten listings before the show, where the working floppy stunned the crowd. He counts three genuine eureka moments, getting color to work, writing Breakout in Basic, and reading data back off a disk, and in each he did not know beforehand whether it was even possible.

Wozniak is careful about the costs and the luck. Four sleepless nights building Atari's Breakout gave him and Jobs mononucleosis, and Jobs quietly kept most of that payment, a fact Wozniak only discovered twelve years later. Motivated by engineering and community rather than wealth, he gave large stock grants to five overlooked early employees and sold shares to about forty coworkers so each could buy a house, echoing how HP had once cut everyone's pay rather than lay anyone off. His advice is to keep the highest ethics, seek excellence until the whole design lives in your head, and never let the lack of a perfect tool stop you.

## Joe Kraus and Excite

Joe Kraus learned early that he never wanted an office job he hated. After his parents "bought" him a summer position duplicating microfiche beside three seventy-year-old women, he quit in three weeks to bag groceries, then the next summer ran a T-shirt business selling to private-school bookstores that earned \$25,000. A political science major at Stanford, he recruited the smartest person he knew, freshman-dorm neighbor Graham Spencer, arguing that Apple and Microsoft would always want him but the freedom to gamble would not last. Six dorm friends agreed to start a company before choosing a product; at a Redwood City taqueria every pitch flopped until Spencer noted that information was going electronic while search tools still dated from the 1950s. Their slogan captured the mood: they were unencumbered by reality.

With \$3,000 from each of five sets of parents, they set up Architext in a Palo Alto garage, heated by a dryer with its door taped open and furnished with VT100 terminals and Herman Miller chairs quietly rolled out of Oracle by a founder who worked there part-time. Kraus, nicknamed Phone Boy, cold-called names from the Wall Street Journal. One accidental chain proved everything: a gossipy Silicon Valley history led him to lunch with its author, then to a \$100,000 InfoWorld contract to index archives on the Web, then to a board meeting, then to venture capitalists. Most investors loved the demo until they asked how it made money; Vinod Khosla instead asked whether the technology could scale, and when Kraus admitted they could not afford a big enough disk, Khosla phoned his assistant to buy them a \$9,000 ten-gigabyte drive. Choosing the Web over HyperCard for the interface turned Architext into web search almost by chance, and a \$3 million round with Kleiner Perkins and IVP followed.

Money forced a painful reckoning among friends. The six had split equity evenly, but Khosla suggested they reconsider. In a quiet, awkward couch-side conversation, Kraus and Spencer told the team the shares would be redistributed unequally. Nobody begrudged Spencer more, since he was the man among boys technically, but valuing Kraus, the non-programmer, was hard, and only their friendship, stronger than greed, carried them through a discussion that would have fractured a group bound only by money.

Kraus never felt secure. Even as the fourth-largest site in the world, Excite felt like fooling people, a sausage that looked tasty until you saw the factory. In late 1995 Microsoft offered about \$70 million; Excite asked for \$100 million, and when the negotiator told Gates it would cost a year, \$25 million, and twenty-five people to build search in-house, Microsoft did neither, while its CTO Nathan Myhrvold insisted search was not a business. The company's real break came from distribution. Netscape's browser carried two search buttons then given free to Infoseek and Yahoo; when it opened them to bids among Excite, Infoseek, and MCI, Excite bid \$3 million it did not have, lost, and refused to accept the loss, showing up unannounced until MCI failed to deliver its service on time and Netscape handed the slot back. That deal, Kraus says, launched the company, and taught him that great hires and great deals, like a marketing VP he flew to New York to win over, come only to people who never hear the first no.

Kraus stresses that opportunity creates opportunity. Acquiring the editorial engine Magellan led AOL to notice Excite, which led to buying WebCrawler, which led to a twenty-to-thirty-million-dollar Intuit deal that saved the public company when it was running out of cash, a chain no one could have planned. Yet success did not validate every choice. Excite treated search as a traffic director toward a portal and clung to print-style cost-per-impression advertising, missing the cost-per-click model that later made search itself the business. His carried-over lessons are to hire slowly, stay cheap, and build the fundamentals before letting momentum outrun them.

## Dan Bricklin and VisiCalc

Dan Bricklin met Bob Frankston as an MIT freshman on the Multics operating-system project, where his first task was finishing Frankston's bachelor's thesis. Both came from families of entrepreneurs, so starting a business felt ordinary, and their friendship, not a negotiated split, held the venture together; they divided work fifty-fifty and trusted each other with the family money at stake. At Harvard Business School, running numbers by hand across case after case, Bricklin combined his experience with programming-language interpreters, computerized typesetting, and word processing into a single idea: a two-dimensional layout of words and numbers where changing one value recalculated everything, like word wrap for arithmetic. Over a 1978 vacation weekend he wrote a prototype in Basic on a machine belonging to publisher Dan Fylstra, whom he had met at business school and who agreed to sell the product through his company, Personal Software.

The design was shaped by relentless constraint and a refusal to think in computer terms. An unstable Apple II game paddle pushed him to arrow keys; a recalculation beep was scrapped when it consumed three-quarters of the processor. Bricklin used decimal arithmetic so the tool behaved like a calculator, made the slash key open commands because it could never begin a number, and named cells by map coordinates like A1 so the parser always knew a letter meant a variable. His accounting professor warned that he was competing against the back of an envelope, so everything had to feel effortless. Frankston wrote most of the assembly code in his Arlington attic, paying for cheap late-night time-sharing, and squeezed the operating system, program, and data into 32K on a machine that held at most 48K, leaving no room for on-screen help; Bricklin's father, a printer, typeset the reference card from which many users learned instead.

VisiCalc shipped in October 1979 and became the reason businesses bought Apple IIs, though it sold only about a thousand copies a month at first. Adoption depended on imagination: people who could map a general tool onto their own work, from accountants laying out casino floors to doctors doing surgical calculations, got excited, while others shrugged that they could just do it in Basic. Recognition finally landed when the Wall Street Journal wrote of yellow ledger pads and VisiCalc spreadsheets all over Washington, and when EDS executives, with infinite mainframe power available, admitted pricing multimillion-dollar deals on VisiCalc. Yet the product lacked commas, dollar signs, variable column widths, and long labels, the exact features buyers began demanding, and precisely the ones Lotus 1-2-3 supplied.

The company's structure was its undoing. The author-publisher arrangement with Personal Software, later renamed VisiCorp, bred contract friction that erupted into a lawsuit in September 1983, just as 1-2-3 arrived. Days before Software Arts was to be bought by H&R Block's CompuServe for roughly \$50 million in cash plus stock, with both boards approving, the suit struck and killed the deal, because the business could not be sold while in litigation. Bricklin is blunt: the lawsuit did not merely distract, it killed the company. Running into Mitch Kapor on an airplane one Monday led, by that Friday, to Lotus buying Software Arts' assets and paying off its loans, rescuing Bricklin from a bankruptcy he had come within minutes of declaring.

Bricklin's lessons are practical and hard-won. Design against the customer's real alternative, which for VisiCalc was the back of an envelope; stay out of lawsuits, since face-to-face settlement almost always beats a sport of kings that the boards involved should never have allowed. He also learned that timing and greed matter, quoting the market adage that bulls and bears both make money but pigs get slaughtered, and that the impersonal "they" who seem to run business are always really an individual, down to banks that quietly slipped personal guarantees into loan papers he had explicitly refused. He gained lasting recognition rather than a great fortune, and, a quarter century on, finds that enough.

## Mitchell Kapor and Lotus

Mitchell Kapor came to software without conventional credentials, having worked as a progressive-rock disc jockey, a transcendental meditation teacher, and a mental-health counselor before an Apple II obsession in 1978 turned him into a self-taught programmer writing Basic for local professionals. As a favor to MIT finance student Eric Rosenfeld, he spent a weekend building a statistics routine, which grew into Tiny Troll and then, at the urging of VisiCalc's publisher, into the companion products VisiPlot and VisiTrend. Those royalties reached about \$100,000 a month against almost no expenses, giving him real options. Working alongside Software Arts, he had also helped devise the clumsy way data moved from VisiCalc into VisiPlot, and he repeatedly proposed combining the programs; neither the authors nor the publisher, who saw him as a credential-less pest, took him seriously.

Kapor turned that dismissal into leverage. Knowing the publisher wanted to end his insupportably high royalty stream, he let them buy him out for \$1.2 million, netting \$600,000 that he split into two piles: an \$89,000 Cambridge house and \$300,000 of seed money he could stretch for years on \$40,000 a year. The hinge of the deal was a non-compete, and Kapor, betting the publisher underestimated him, persuaded them to carve out an exception for an integrated graphing calculator, the very product that would become Lotus 1-2-3. He had already hired Jonathan Sachs, who had implemented spreadsheets several times before.

The galvanizing event was IBM's PC in August 1981, which legitimized personal computers for business and, crucially, offered a 16-bit processor addressing 640K where the Apple II held only 64K. The VisiCalc and Microsoft spreadsheets shipped for the IBM PC still ran in 64K of tweaked 8-bit code, leaving the machine's real capacity unused. Kapor saw the gap and had Sachs, an artist at optimizing scarce resources, write 1-2-3 in assembly with a faster natural-order recalculation, one-button graphing, a friendlier interface, and a macro language. Kapor supplied the product vision and Sachs the implementation, a pairing he calls one plus one equals three. He credits the opening squarely to the distracted VisiCalc camp: had they been doing their job, the opportunity would not have existed. Ben Rosen and L.J. Sevin funded him, the latter over a nervous, expensive French dinner where Sevin finally asked how much the company was worth and Kapor, with nothing but Sachs and an unfinished spreadsheet, dropped his fork.

Lotus 1-2-3 shipped in January 1983 and buried the business plan's \$3-to-\$4-million forecast, reaching \$53 million that year and \$150 million the next. Kapor was unprepared for the magnitude. His mistakes came after launch: he led well up to about twenty-five people but could not build middle management, hired badly, expanded without discipline, pursued a scattered product strategy, and chose a poor successor. He grades himself a C. He did build a strikingly progressive workplace, with a diversity committee including gay and lesbian employees in 1984, sponsorship of an AIDS walk, values tied to manager bonuses, and every manager required to take customer support calls, but his successor dismantled most of it.

Kapor's lessons center on values, financing, and first principles. He once stood up and walked out of a second-round closing when investors piled on onerous last-minute terms and tried to make him personally liable for a technicality; they backed down, and he insists such behavior, however standard, is simply wrong, the reason venture capital earns the "vulture capital" name. He regrets voting, out of company loyalty, for the look-and-feel lawsuits that tried to stop independently developed lookalike products. His advice is to keep working code running close to the target machine, understand financing before signing, find mentors whose values you share, and refuse projects that demand compromises you cannot live with.

## Ray Ozzie, Notes, and Groove

Ray Ozzie built both his companies from anticipated user needs rather than technology chasing a market. His engineer's confidence that software can accomplish almost anything made the real question what was worth accomplishing, so he kept a technology roadmap and a market roadmap in mind and aimed several years ahead at a future hole. Exposure to the large interactive PLATO system in college let him glimpse a world where everyone had networked computers, and in 1982 he wrote the specification for what became Lotus Notes, imagining desktop machines as communication tools before networks were common. Unable to fund it, he was hired in 1983 by Mitch Kapor and Jonathan Sachs at Lotus, did a little work on 1-2-3, and led the suite product Symphony on the condition that Kapor help him launch Notes afterward. The day Symphony shipped, Kapor made good, but because Lotus held plenty of cash he had the company fund Ozzie directly; at twenty-seven, Ozzie formed Iris Associates in December 1984 as an unusual corporate R&D partnership. Notes shipped in 1989 and Lotus acquired Iris in 1994.

Groove began in Ozzie's house in the fall of 1997 with his brother Jack and former Iris engineers, soon moving to an office in Beverly, Massachusetts. Before starting, he wrote two founding documents, an outside-in scenario paper describing the user problem and a bottom-up map of the technologies required. The team's first code was a primitive version of the hardest thing they faced: masterless synchronization among unreliable PCs that get rebooted, restored from backup, and lost. Academic versions existed, but the commercial desktop was harsh, so for three or four months they proved feasibility on whiteboards and prototypes before hiring fifteen to twenty people. An early make-or-break choice was C++ over Java, which they rejected after concluding Sun would never ship a stable client runtime they could count on across random PCs. The peer-to-peer desktop architecture was a deliberate contrarian bet during the Web's ascent, meant to complement rather than replace it for mobile, secure, cross-firewall work.

Groove targeted temporary virtual workgroups spanning companies that could not dictate each other's software. It launched in beta in October 2000, three years to the month after founding, and shipped commercially in April 2001 with a 10,000-seat GlaxoSmithKline deal. In hindsight that marquee sale hurt more than it helped, deluding the team into thinking enterprises would adopt Groove quickly when it was not yet enterprise-ready and IT was focused only on cutting cost and tightening security. Changing habits, Ozzie learned, is often harder than building the technology. His ten-year horizon forced him to late-bind interface, positioning, and marketing decisions, and to shift the company from an early media and entertainment focus toward pure productivity, accepting the heavy enterprise-management burden that came with it.

Ozzie believed Microsoft was his prime competitor at both companies, since Microsoft cares about anything broadly horizontal, and enterprise customers kept asking why Microsoft would not simply crush Groove tomorrow. He took Microsoft's investment knowing it was market tracking, a way to watch which customers found the technology attractive after Notes had once grown unnoticed under Redmond's radar, and reasoned that if Microsoft is your competitor it is best to stay stealthy and embrace them when the timing helps; the backing reassured wary accounts. Microsoft ultimately acquired Groove in 2005 and made Ozzie its chief technical officer, then its chief software architect, succeeding Bill Gates.

His enduring lesson is to build trusted relationships across disciplines before they become urgent, since technologists need marketing, sales, and leadership partners and it takes about a year to know whom to trust. Companies take the shape of their founders' behavior; a tech founder is rarely the right long-term leader and must know when to step back to chief technologist. Above all, separate your identity from the product you are building, share custody of it with team and investors, and never tell people you are doing it for the money, because mission, not payday, is what makes people believe and what makes the company last.

## Evan Williams and Blogger

Evan Williams was self-taught and constitutionally unemployable, having dropped out of college and run an earlier Nebraska company into the ground over three years before moving to California for a brief job at O'Reilly and then contracting for HP and Intel. In late 1998 he started Pyra Labs with Meg Hourihan to build a web-based project and information management tool, a forerunner of things like Basecamp. Lacking startup connections, they funded the first year themselves; an HP contract quietly paid for three people while they built their real product. Their first employee, Paul Bausch, joined in May 1999.

Blogger was a side effect. Williams and Bausch had turned their own homepages into weblogs with simple scripts, and the revelation was not publishing to the web, which was already easy, but doing it in seconds, which transformed how often Williams wrote. They built an internal blog called Stuff that became the center of the company, then extended it to post to Pyra's public site. In March 1999 Williams registered the Blogger domain, and that July, while Meg was on vacation, he and Paul launched Blogger as a product, justifying it as a focused piece of the bigger vision. Meg was rightly furious. Blogger caught on far beyond the thousand users Williams hoped for, appealing to web geeks who knew what FTP was and spreading as the alpha "cool kids" of the early blog community linked to it.

The dilemma was that Blogger had buzz but no business, while the "real" Pyra app had a plausible model. In April and May of 2000, around the crash, they raised half a million dollars from O'Reilly, Advance.net, advisor Jerry Michalski, Meg's parents, and AOL's John Borthwick, grew to seven people, and finally committed to Blogger. Williams had concluded that blogging was a native form of the web, about freshness, frequency, hyperlinks, personal voice, and the democratization of media, forces he considered inevitable. But they never charged, betting on eyeballs and buzz, and by fall were running out of money as the extent of the crash sank in.

Williams refused to pivot to enterprise even after a Cisco pilot, believing it was pointless and that they could not both sell to companies and serve consumers well. A near-merger with Nick Denton's Moreover, a lowball of roughly \$1 million in private stock, was rejected by Moreover's board, which Williams later counted as luck; a New York startup's \$50,000 bridge also led nowhere. In December 2000 and January 2001 he laid everyone off, including himself. Meg and the rest left, relationships soured, and he was badmouthed across their shared community as the guy who fired his friends, a period compounded by a legal fight that cost him nearly as much as he paid himself. A candid Server Fund Drive on the site, run through PayPal, plus a server from the publisher CMP, raised around \$17,000 for hardware, and with no payroll his burn dropped from \$50,000 a month to a few thousand.

As the sole employee he taught himself Linux administration and Java to keep the growing service alive, fixing bugs on the live site. Dan Bricklin, moved by Williams's public blog post about the collapse, had his company Trellix license Blogger for about \$40,000 to help, and a KnowNow deal added \$35,000. Through 2002 Williams rebuilt with a new team, launching Blogger Pro, a \$12-a-year option to remove ads, a Brazil licensing deal with Jason Shellen, and the Blogger API. In October 2002 Google came knocking, largely through O'Reilly and Sergey Brin's interest in blogs, and Blogger became Google's first acquisition; Williams turned down a \$1 million investment term sheet from Joi Ito's Neoteny and, after struggling with the loss of control, closed with Google four months later.

Williams's lessons are hard-won: resist compromise and trust a strong gut, since original ideas rarely command agreement; luck often looks bad at first, as his dead deals proved; pay closer attention to how people feel; and never underestimate how far a genuinely simple idea can go.

## Tim Brady and Yahoo

Tim Brady met Jerry Yang as a Stanford electrical engineering undergraduate, reconnected with him in Japan while working for Motorola, and was finishing his MBA at Harvard Business School when Yang called at the start of his second year. Yang and David Filo had started their site as a list of the technical papers they needed for their PhD theses, and when the wider electrical-engineering graduate community began emailing requests, the two added categories all day, everything from EE resources to Yang's beloved sumo wrestling. With their thesis advisor conveniently on sabbatical, they spent eight or more hours a day for eight months building the directory. Traffic multiplied roughly tenfold between October 1994 and January 1995, a Newsweek article appeared, and suitors like the LA Times, AOL, and Microsoft started calling, turning a hobby into a business.

Yang asked Brady to write a business plan, modeled on the known advertising approach of sites like HotWired, and Brady turned it in as the final paper for two of his five classes, exploiting Harvard's pass/fail rule that let him fail up to three. In late March 1995 the four-person team, Yang, Filo, Filo's friend Donald Lobo, and Brady, launched with a booth at a San Jose consumer-electronics show, working out of the Stanford trailer and Yang's apartment before Sequoia's \$1 million let them rent Mountain View offices. The near-term goal was survival: at a \$20 CPM, advertising across their pages would cover costs, so they sold ads and did book deals while searching six months for a real CEO, eventually landing Tim Koogle, who soon hired Jeff Mallett as operating chief.

Several turning points defined the early company. Netscape's browser directory button linked to Yahoo for free and sent traffic through the roof; when that button later became a paid search button, Excite bought it for about \$5 million, and Yahoo declined to overpay for brand it could not yet justify. Brady pushed graphics onto the all-text site despite slow modems, brought Reuters online in the first six months, and later put "Rabin Assassinated" on the front page as Yahoo's first real news. Bill Gates's famous internet memo ended with "My favorite site: Yahoo. Cool. Cool. Cool.", flattering and frightening at once given Microsoft's habit of building what it might otherwise partner on.

Brady, who ran product under Mallett, treated Yahoo as an open connection point rather than AOL's walled garden; Filo even had failed searches link straight to competitors like Excite with the query prefilled, on the principle that serving the user mattered more than trapping them. Yahoo deliberately stayed a media company rather than a technology company, renting full-text search from Open Text, then AltaVista, then Inktomi as falloff behind its curated directory, a strategy that was flawless until Google grew good enough to become the kingmaker they had assumed impossible. Yahoo also missed Hotmail after Brady and Yang dined with Sabeer Bhatia and Jack Smith and failed to see how fast web email would spread, a mistake they corrected by acquiring the number two, Rocketmail. Stanford, whose servers Yahoo was overwhelming, told the pair to leave, and Marc Andreessen briefly hosted the site at Netscape. Yahoo went public in April 1996, driven by a hot IPO window, the need for stock currency to acquire companies, and the desire to raise money.

Brady's memories are of sixteen-to-eighteen-hour days among unusually trusting young colleagues, a May 1995 blackout when they refueled a diesel generator around the clock and held candlelit meetings under a leaking roof, and Jeff Mallett dismissing his exhausted PR kit as "C+ work," a lesson that appearing big required acting big. His advice is to know yourself before you start: define your breaking points, your motivations, and, because he was going into business with a close friend, to set the friendship's ground rules in advance so pressure never has to.

## Mike Lazaridis and Research In Motion

Mike Lazaridis met Doug Fregin in grade school and began building with him in a high school blessed with a state-of-the-art electronics shop donated by a local industrialist. The teacher let students open the crated equipment on one condition: read the manual first, so Lazaridis worked through dense texts on oscilloscopes and signal generators until he had opened every box. He and Fregin learned computer fundamentals on Digital Equipment trainers, wiring gates, registers, and clocks by hand, and converted television tuners for the ham-radio band under a teacher who also ran the local amateur radio club. That teacher gave him the seed of everything: don't get too lost in computers, because the person who marries wireless and computing will make the real difference.

At the University of Waterloo in the early 1980s, Lazaridis arrived just in time to skip punch cards for terminals in the mainframe "Red Room," used email to submit assignments, worked on the ARPANET, and joined network and real-time research including the Watlan local-area project and an interpretive language called STOIC that anticipated the virtual-machine ideas later seen in Java. When the 1984 recession wrecked Waterloo's famous job placement and classmates complained, they dared him: if you believe in this so much, start a company. Within weeks he did. He called Fregin, then at Windsor, who came up within two weeks, and Lazaridis took a leave of absence a month short of graduation; the university president who gently tried to dissuade him would, twenty years later, sit on RIM's board.

The early years were lean, Lazaridis and Fregin sharing a leased Honda Civic whose one luxury was a five-speed transmission, and living in the same apartment while small Canadian government grants helped them survive. Consulting work for the National Film Board and Kodak eventually earned an Emmy and a technical Oscar, but the breakthrough contract was a \$600,000 project for General Motors. Reading an RFP that had gone unfilled for two years, they recognized it demanded a local-area network built from scratch, rugged enough to survive arc welders and 4,800-volt systems, with displays that booted diskless from a central computer, work they did on one of the first IBM PCs fitted with a then-luxurious ten-megabyte hard drive.

The turn toward wireless came at a 1987 conference describing a Japanese system that let Coca-Cola vending machines signal when they needed refilling, saving needless truck trips. Remembering his teacher's prophecy, Lazaridis pursued wireless data, and Cantel, now Rogers, hired RIM to make sense of a raw Swedish system called Mobitex whose documentation was barely translated. His partner Michael Barnstijn, who read Dutch, deciphered enough that they got it working within hours and won the contract, then wrote much of the earliest wireless protocol software, APIs, and development tools. Along the way Lazaridis declined a childhood dream, a SPAR Aerospace contract for Canadarm2, once he learned it meant only six units for a space station not yet through Congress; years later BlackBerries would ironically carry email between NASA and the International Space Station.

The BlackBerry itself was drafted in a basement. The night his son was born, Lazaridis put the baby to bed, went downstairs, and in three hours wrote the plan for what he called an interactive pager, defining five improvements to wireless networks that would make push email reliable and power-efficient. That document guided the company for years and kept it from chasing fads that hurt battery life. Knowing the market was not ready, RIM disguised the device as a familiar pager, a two-way email terminal backed by a server, and launched in 1999 into New York's financial markets, where affluent, time-pressed users prized real-time information. The name came from the firm Lexicon after six months of study, the most expensive word Lazaridis ever bought.

RIM, which went public in 1997, never left Canada. Lazaridis anchored it beside Waterloo for talent, put building signs on the back facing the university rather than the road, and hired first- and second-year co-op students years before they could work full-time, making RIM the country's largest co-op employer. His conviction was that exposure to seemingly irrelevant future technology, not narrowly "relevant" teaching, is exactly what prepares people for the decades ahead.

## Arthur van Hoff and Marimba

Arthur van Hoff left Sun's Java team in 1996 with fellow developers Sami Shaio and Jonathan Payne and Java's product manager Kim Polese, and the striking thing is they left with no idea what to build. They rented rooms above a flower shop on California Avenue in Palo Alto, hauled up secondhand metal desks that cost \$25 apiece and weighed a ton, put in \$25,000 each, and furnished the whole office, fax and printer included, for about \$1,400, working off a whiteboard with no Internet for the first weeks; their law firm, Gunderson Dettmer, deferred its fees until funding arrived, as Silicon Valley custom allowed. Their bet was on people, not a plan: van Hoff believed that when a core team leaves together, ideas are easy, and that the first idea is merely a catalyst you argue past through denial, panic, and regret until the important second idea arrives. When he and Polese told Scott McNealy they were leaving, he refused to wish them luck lest everyone follow, and warned them not to mess with him.

Their first product, a Java user-interface builder, collapsed when a small startup showed them nearly the same thing and was acquired by Netscape the next week, becoming the IFC and eventually Swing; with little invested, they moved on quickly. Meanwhile the press was infatuated, running a full-page Wired photo of a team that was deliberately saying nothing because it had nothing to say. They then settled on software distribution, a subscription model with automatic updates that anticipated how Windows and antivirus tools now update themselves, but PointCast had just popularized "push," and Marimba spent years filed under a label it had to keep disavowing.

Publicity cut both ways. The mystique opened any executive's door, yet the company constantly had to reeducate a market that never knew what it actually sold, and the coverage fixated on Kim Polese as a rare female technology CEO. Van Hoff recalls people knowing her name but not the product, and a Fortune reporter who spent two days being shown the technology only to publish an exposé built around extreme close-ups of Polese. He came to see the hype as a net negative. Polese was an able startup CEO, but as Marimba grew from zero to forty people in a year and past three hundred by its 1999 IPO, an inexperienced chief executive between a strong sales VP and a strong CFO could not break ties; her successor John Olsen, who had run big companies, made those calls easily, illustrating that founders skilled at starting are not necessarily skilled at scaling.

Money stories capture the culture. The \$4 million first round from Kleiner Perkins arrived as physical checks that Sami took to Kinko's to photocopy and nearly left in the machine, and van Hoff's early joke about buying an espresso machine was shouted down as a misuse of startup capital. Years later, as post-IPO morale sagged and veterans quit, he finally ordered a \$15,000 automatic Italian espresso machine on a personal credit card; the CFO was horrified, then a month later admitted it was the best money they had ever spent, a lesson that environment and feeling valued matter, not just business expenses. Marimba's real revenue came from an unglamorous enterprise pivot, distributing software reliably to a hundred thousand endpoints for firms like Morgan Stanley and Bear Stearns.

The hardest part was legal. A meritless patent suit dragged on and consumed attention, and after the IPO the founders faced the trap that any sale as the stock fell could trigger a personal shareholder lawsuit, so they watched the price drop from \$75 to almost nothing without selling, because losing such a suit could cost a house rather than just company money. The four split their founding shares equally and, thanks to good early VC terms, kept meaningful stock through the IPO. Van Hoff's advice is that talent attracts talent, that a first business plan is just proof you can use a word processor and should be pitched to friends and parents before VCs, that joining a funded startup is a low-risk way to learn, that founders should trust their own intuition over experienced advice more often, and that anyone leaving to start something competitive should take nothing, write nothing down, and simply talk it over a beer and leave, because success is exactly what invites a lawsuit.

## Paul Buchheit and Gmail

Paul Buchheit, Google's twenty-third employee, had first tried building web-based email in 1996 as a college student annoyed that checking messages meant walking back to his dorm; he even called that abandoned experiment Gmail. He joined Google from Intel almost casually, emailing his résumé twice after the first attempt bounced off a downed mail server, expecting only that the work would be more fun than his old job and privately convinced AltaVista would eventually destroy Google. After finishing the first generation of Google Groups, he was handed a vague charter to build "some type of email or personalization product," a controversial idea at a company that still thought of itself as pure search. Reusing Groups code, he built a first version in a single day that searched only his own mail, and when the Googlers he shared it with found even that useful, the project grew.

For a long time Gmail was mostly Buchheit alone, then joined by Sanjeev Singh and later Jing Lim, reaching about a dozen people by launch. The team designed from the user's irritations outward. A one-gigabyte quota, against a prevailing standard of two to four megabytes, removed the most common reason people deleted mail; search made large archives navigable; conversation view reassembled the replies people had been manually foldering back together, partly inspired by wanting to treat email like chat; and autocomplete of addresses, a first for web mail, made sending fast. Doing the entire interface in JavaScript was contentious internally, since JavaScript was then associated with pop-up ads and browser crashes, but it worked better than anyone expected and users loved it.

Email proved a fundamentally different systems problem from search, because delivery had to be instant and no data could ever be lost, whereas a lost web page can simply be recrawled. An early prototype's lag, where incoming mail took time to be indexed, had to go. When a single machine holding everyone's internal mail lost its hard disk, Buchheit walked into the machine room with screwdrivers, to alarmed looks, and transplanted the electronics from another drive so nothing was lost; through everything, Gmail never lost data despite running on cheap commodity PCs. The code name was Caribou, borrowed from a Dilbert cartoon after the too-obvious "Gmail" was set aside. The invitation-only rollout controlled the rate of adoption to protect reliability and to keep spammers from grabbing millions of accounts. Buchheit had been awake about seventy hours stitching together the login and other systems when Gmail launched on April 1, 2004, a date and a storage offer so extravagant that many assumed it was a hoax.

Gmail was not Buchheit's only mark on Google. AdSense, the content-targeted advertising program, began as a throwaway prototype he wrote in less than a day one evening, over the objection of colleagues who considered the idea unworkable; it proved possible, and others turned it into a real product. He also coined "Don't be evil" at an early-2000 values meeting, intending both a memorable phrase resistant to removal and a jab at competitors who blended paid results into search listings without telling users; when the facilitator kept pushing it down the list, Buchheit and Amit Patel kept forcing it back up until it stuck and Patel began writing it on whiteboards across the building.

Buchheit credits Google's culture and its founders for making the project possible, praising Larry Page and Sergey Brin as genuinely smart and unusually open to crazy ideas, the first people who met his unconventional proposals with agreement rather than lectures. His temperament suited the work: he liked uncertainty as a form of adventure, noting that most people pretend life is risk-free even as they take risks daily, and he kept startup hours from noon until three in the morning, always meaning to make just one more improvement. His advice to anyone with a big idea trapped in a rigid company is blunt, that rather than trying to change an entire culture from below, they should go somewhere, whether Google, a startup, or elsewhere, that will actually let them build it.

## Steve Perlman and WebTV

Steve Perlman had chased interactive television for years before WebTV, doing early work at Apple in 1989 on a system with movable video, pause, and rewind that could never ship because no content existed and no hard disk was big enough to hold live video, the chicken-and-egg problem that would haunt him. He worked half-time at General Magic on interactive-TV ideas, then cofounded Catapult Entertainment, which built a modem letting existing Sega and Nintendo games be played online by reverse-engineering titles like NBA Jam and Mortal Kombat and shipping to store shelves, custom silicon and all, in six months of round-the-clock work. Catapult taught him consumer hardware, networks, and retail, and a painful lesson when a cofounder pulled a different direction: founding a company is like a marriage, and misaligned partners just lock horns.

A self-taught hobbyist with a liberal-arts degree, Perlman saw Netscape 1.0 and sites like Campbell's Soup and realized their audience might not own computers. If he could put those pages on a television, using image-processing tricks he had learned at Atari and Coleco to defeat interlace flicker and sharpen the picture, he could break the content deadlock. He bought about \$3,000 of parts at Fry's and, over three days and two nights in 1995, built a prototype that rendered web pages crisply on a TV. He called Bruce Leak at midnight to come see it; Leak said they had to form a company, and they named it WebTV on the spot. Phil Goldman, who had written MultiFinder and much of the General Magic OS, joined as the third cofounder.

Hollywood financier Marvin Davis, who had profited handsomely flipping his Catapult shares, put in \$1.5 million, and the team moved from Perlman's dining room into a vacant BMW dealership renting at ninety cents a square foot, where Pacific Bell at first refused to believe a car dealership needed a T1 line. WebTV built almost everything itself: a browser written from scratch to fit 2 megabytes of RAM on a 112 MHz MIPS box when even Microsoft's Mosaic-derived Explorer could not, custom silicon, a gate array doing the flicker-killing video, proxy servers that resized oversized JPEGs, and a nationwide dial-up network. Investors balked, insisting people would never want to interact with their TV and wary of hardware's inventory and capital costs in a year when every other internet bet was pure software. When Sony could not push an exclusive manufacturing deal through its bureaucracy, Davis got nervous and withheld the second tranche; with more than thirty employees and cash nearly gone, Perlman mortgaged his house and told no one.

The company nearly died repeatedly. A semiconductor investor tried at the last minute to lock WebTV into buying all its chips from them, wasting two months, and Perlman came to feel some VCs were circling to buy the pieces cheap. Brentwood's Jeff Brody was about to sign \$4.5 million when a certified letter arrived from Sony withdrawing entirely; Brody said he still believed and funded them anyway, which brought Paul Allen in to match for a \$9 million round. A consultant, Spencer Tall, who knew Sony's CEO Idei-san, then reopened the door: Sony's CTO was dispatched by private jet on two and a half hours' notice, arriving as an untested, freshly compiled build finished, and by pure luck it ran perfectly. Before the follow-up meeting in Tokyo, Leak and a remote engineer living in a rice paddy added Japanese support overnight, and that demo won Sony back, though by then Philips had also signed and no one got an exclusive. A roughly \$35 million Series C followed, with Microsoft, Seagate, Citicorp, and others, and WebTV shipped in July 1996, one year almost to the day after Davis's first check.

Perlman is candid about mistakes and luck. Pricing the box at \$329 and charging a subscription was an error; at \$99 it sold well, though only 56,000 subscribers existed when Microsoft bought WebTV for over \$500 million. He left after Microsoft reneged on supporting RealNetworks and Java and its politics made focus impossible, but WebTV, later MSNTV, turned profitable in eighteen months and grossed over \$1.3 billion in eight years. His deepest lesson is that a startup is only its people: the worst thing that can happen is a founding team that does not get along, especially in front of the troops, and WebTV survived rejection and scarcity because he, Leak, and Goldman shared one vision and never had to watch their backs.

## Mike Ramsay and TiVo

Scottish-born Mike Ramsay came to the United States with Hewlett-Packard in the mid-1970s, fleeing a Britain of strikes and runaway inflation for what felt like Disneyland for technologists. He met Jim Barton during a second stint at HP, followed a talented crowd to Silicon Graphics, and watched Barton become a world-class engineer who made UNIX work in real time and built the first video-on-demand system with Time Warner in Orlando, an experience that soured Barton on cable and satellite monopolies. After both left SGI, recurring lunches turned into a plan to bring computing into home entertainment through a wired home server and network. Most venture firms threw them out, because it was a consumer play, a service business, and capital-intensive, three strikes at once; only Stewart Alsop of NEA and Geoff Yang of Redpoint, drawn to the people and the vision rather than the spreadsheet, funded them.

Once inside the technology, they realized home networking was immature and a home server impossible to explain to consumers, so they picked the single most compelling application their imagined server would run, the digital video recorder, and went back to the investors to say they had changed their minds. The reflex reaction, "that just sounds like a VCR," enraged Ramsay, because a hard disk makes it fundamentally different: its fast random-access head can create the illusion of recording and playing back at once, enabling pausing live television. Solving that cheaply became the Time Warp patent, built around a device they called the media switch, one of the company's most important patents and among its first filings.

Two other problems were equally novel. Driving the machine from program-guide data had never been done, so TiVo licensed Tribune Media Services listings, still assembled by armies of people reading newspapers, and massaged them into something accurate enough that a viewer asking for The Sopranos would tolerate no error, delivering roughly sixty-five thousand ZIP-code-and-provider combinations over a built-in modem. The system had to be secure, so no one could see what a subscriber watched, and recoverable, so a bad software release could not brick millions of boxes; TiVo built a back-end server farm from nothing and a "phone home" safe mode, likened to recovering a lost Mars probe. Ramsay, worried a cheap consumer box would not attract top engineers, found the opposite: hiding hard technology behind a remote control was exactly the challenge great people wanted. He also insisted, over objections, on testing ordinary recording six months before release; it fell apart, validating his fear and forcing a late scramble.

TiVo shipped on the last day of March 1999, a date that happened to fall on a blue moon and became a permanent company holiday, with the whole company donning blue jackets at the Milpitas manufacturer. Sixty employees fanned out to Bay Area Fry's stores to give demos and learn retail firsthand. Pause-live-TV was the hook nobody believed until they saw it, and Season Pass and WishList let people request a show or a director once and receive it forever, smart enough to skip repeats and adjust to schedule changes. The consumer press loved the simplicity while technology reviewers like Walt Mossberg found it not techie enough, and a single bad review could tear the company apart. About 18,000 units had sold by the oversubscribed September 1999 IPO, despite a \$500-to-\$600 price.

Money and enemies defined the rest. TiVo raised close to half a billion dollars over time, including Paul Allen's Vulcan and a \$200 million AOL investment, and Ramsay credits a strong ex-banker CFO, Dave Courtney, and being well-capitalized for surviving the downturn that killed rival Replay. That rivalry, complete with dueling-remote photo shoots, taught the company to compete, and Replay ultimately did TiVo a favor by crossing Hollywood's invisible line with automatic commercial-skipping and internet program-sharing, drawing the lawsuits and casting TiVo as the good guy. When DIRECTV sold both TiVo and Microsoft's DVR on equal terms, TiVo outsold the far richer Microsoft until Microsoft gave up. The networks first threatened weekly lawsuits and called TiVo evil, then, astonishingly, invested; roughly \$50 million from Disney, Viacom, Discovery, NBC, Showtime, HBO, and Time Warner helped carry TiVo through the bursting bubble, turning a company hunted by media giants into a partner they embraced.

## Paul Graham and Viaweb

Paul Graham and his best friend Robert Morris had first built Artix to put art galleries online, only to discover galleries did not want to be online, teaching them to make something people actually wanted. Since their gallery software was only a shopping cart away from generating online stores, they pivoted in the summer of 1995, writing a prototype in Robert's Cambridge apartment in a couple of days. Days in, Graham woke one morning with an idea so dramatic it sat him up in bed like the letter L: run the software on the server and control it through the browser, treating the browser like an xterm. It worked, which thrilled them mainly because it spared them from writing Windows desktop software, and it produced what Graham believed was the first web-based application, the source of the name Viaweb.

Their friend Julian, who had also worked on Artix, put in \$10,000, and they recruited Trevor Blackwell, whom Robert called the smartest grad student he knew. Asked to write a small image tool as a test, Trevor vanished for two weeks and returned having rewritten the entire system in Smalltalk; Graham told him he was hired but to go write the image software, because they were not rewriting everything. Being hackers who found customers terrifying, they wrote thousands more lines of code before selling, and to launch they signed a few technical bookstores by doing whatever it took, giving away the software, building sites, and scanning images themselves. They discovered their imagined customers, catalog companies, wished the Web would go away, while specialty merchants selling antique chess pieces loved gaining what big catalog firms had.

Viaweb's secret weapon was the realization that e-commerce was really about graphic design, not transaction processing, since a site had to convince someone to buy before there was any transaction; they did not even process credit cards for about two years. Their biggest competitor, iCat, was great at raising money and seeming corporate but weak technically; when iCat launched a web version, Graham and Trevor at a trade show swapped a URL argument for the system password file's path and the server displayed it, with accounts that had no passwords at all. To seem like a real company for that first acquisition visit, they borrowed computers as props, including Robert's from downstairs, prompting him to storm in demanding to know where his machine had gone.

The software and customer story rose smoothly, but the business story was one disaster after another. A giant company offered \$3 million days after launch, and their metals-trader investor, trusted as a great negotiator, countered by offering a \$1 million option to buy the company later for \$20 million; the buyers walked out and would not return, teaching Graham that it is never a deal until the money is in the bank. Robert then quietly left for a summer research job at DEC in California, forcing Graham to spin it to investors, who were pressuring the founders to accept a business-guy CEO. They lucked into Fred Egan as chief operating officer, who was content to handle investors and let them write software; on his first day he answered the fearsome investor's call with "Hi Alan, are you buying or selling?"

The worst stretch came during their last funding round, when due diligence revealed a programmer had signed away ideas relating to his graduate sponsor's business, casting an intellectual-property cloud that killed an acquisition and made raising money nearly impossible. The angels played chicken, proposing a cramdown at a zero pre-money valuation that would wipe out the founders in exchange for options; Graham was literally on his way to a lawyer to quit when Fred stopped him, and calling the bluff produced a reasonable round at roughly a \$12 million valuation. Only months later Yahoo, whom they had openly wished for and reached through an Ali Partovi connection, bought Viaweb in 1998, upstanding enough to require no vesting, though integration later dissolved the tight-knit team across the company. Graham's honest sales trick was simply to tell people, truthfully, that theirs was the best software, and his enduring lessons were to raise as little money as possible, spend cheaply, learn from users, and above all make something people want.

## Joshua Schachter and del.icio.us

Del.icio.us began not as a startup but as a solution to Joshua Schachter's own filing problem. Running Memepool, a link site he built around 1998 that was essentially a blog before the word existed, he accumulated a text file of some 20,000 links he could no longer search. He began annotating each URL with a hash mark and a word, the first being "math," so he could grep out related items, and in 2001 he turned that habit into Muxway, a single-player tool whose public pages unexpectedly drew about 10,000 daily readers. Earlier variations like Reversible failed, but in late 2003 he built a multiplayer version, del.icio.us, released it broadly that December, and by November 2004 word of mouth had carried it to 30,000 users.

Throughout he kept his Morgan Stanley job doing data mining and proprietary trading algorithms, structuring del.icio.us code so no chunk exceeded a screen and he could accomplish one useful thing in a fifteen-minute session. He is convinced that discipline mattered: earlier bookmarking startups like Backflip had failed, and he views being overcapitalized as a path to failure because investors pressure you to spend and the problem never gets thought through. He also doubts he could have raised money at all without an existing user base and press to point to, since a pitch to "build a bookmarking service" would never have gotten off the ground. When his Morgan Stanley group began breaking apart in early 2005 and acquisition offers kept rising, he left, raised about \$1 million from Union Square Ventures and Amazon after meeting Jeff Bezos at Foo Camp, and built an eight-person company in which everyone, including himself, held vesting shares.

Turning a hobby into a company exposed unglamorous work. Payroll providers refused teams under five people, so Union Square introduced Albert Wenger, whose operations sense and product work extended to the first Firefox toolbar. Schachter's rule was to execute flawlessly on the undifferentiated necessities, keeping the site up and the office connected, but to outsource all of it because doing it yourself adds no value, while reserving real attention for the core problem of scaling. That core proved genuinely hard: MySQL tables corrupted routinely, and he learned that auto-assigned database IDs collide the moment you add a second machine, one of a long list of pitfalls he would design around differently today. A week after the acquisition a data-center power failure corrupted every machine and knocked the site down for roughly forty-eight hours because the boxes were not configured to recover cleanly.

Yahoo acquired del.icio.us in December 2005 for a figure rumored near \$30 million. Schachter warns that feature requests usually describe a familiar solution rather than the underlying need, so he dug for the root cause before building anything, and that elegant but complicated earlier projects failed because a hard interface defeats clever architecture. His guiding lesson is reduction: do as little as possible, take things away until they fit together rather than adding features, because a smaller system is easier to understand, analyze, and use. He also trusts founders who execute over those who only supply ideas, since you cannot know whether an idea is any good until you have built it and paid the cost of doing so.

## Mark Fletcher, ONElist, and Bloglines

Mark Fletcher built both of his companies to scratch his own itch. ONElist began in 1997 because starting a mailing list for his parents then required installed software and an always-connected computer, absurdly hard for an ordinary person. A senior engineer at Sun, which he had joined when it acquired the startup Diba, he built ONElist nights and weekends for a full year while keeping his salary, being risk-averse with a mortgage and little savings, and checked with a lawyer that Sun could not claim the intellectual property since the project was noncompetitive and never touched at work. Five people ran it in their spare time, surviving the first year on \$55,000.

The growth was punishing: about a percent and a half a day, reaching a million users at eleven months in 1998 with no monitoring systems in place. Fletcher endured constant downtime, once curling into a fetal position over 500 weekend support emails before a Monday at Sun, and was woken on his birthday to learn the database machine at Digital Nation in Virginia had died; scaling problems forced him to close new registrations for three months, something a startup is never supposed to do. In December 1999 he raised \$4 million from CMGI and Bertelsmann Ventures having never seen a term sheet, with no mentor and flying blind, and two weeks later David Wetherell's CMGI, which had invested citing the management team, moved to replace him as CEO. He fought it because he could not separate his ego from the company, then conceded; the new CEO carried the renamed eGroups to a Yahoo acquisition in June 2000 with twenty million users, and Fletcher, who had not touched the code in a year, left cleanly.

For Bloglines he deliberately did the opposite. After time off left him bored and an anti-spam venture called Trustic soured because everybody hates a spam filter, a side project took over: he had a bookmark list of about a hundred sites he checked daily, discovered RSS, and, unimpressed by desktop aggregators and needing something usable across several computers, built a server-based reader for himself. He threw it online in June 2003, embarrassed because nobody yet knew what a blog was, and it drew immediate, wildly disproportionate press, partly because reporters themselves were becoming Bloglines users as the blog trend took off by pure serendipity. Reusing the Trustic corporate shell and his ONElist lawyer, he self-funded about \$200,000 and brought back former ONElist colleagues for stock, remaining the only full-time person until September 2004.

Fletcher's convictions run through both companies: with consumer internet services you should not worry about a business model, because any technology or concept can be copied but users cannot, and the way to win them is to release early and often and iterate on the fifty to a hundred feature emails arriving daily, which also builds loyalty. His stickiest metric astonished him, an average user returning four times a day for twelve page views. He credits an investment banker, who ran an auction for two or three percent, with the clean Ask Jeeves acquisition in February 2005, after which he stayed fourteen months to transfer the knowledge in his head and protect his reputation. His biggest operational regret was buying machines at Bloglines instead of repeating the virtual hosting he had used at ONElist, since acquirers never value the hardware. Above all, he advises solving a problem you actually have, shipping before it is perfect, and doing a startup young, because it multiplies every high and low a hundredfold.

## Craig Newmark and craigslist

Craigslist began in early 1995 while Craig Newmark was at Charles Schwab evangelizing the Internet, when he started emailing ten or twelve friends about San Francisco arts and technology events like the Anon Salon, using Pine and a CC list. Recipients christened it Craig's List and asked for more categories, jobs and items for sale, and when the CC list broke in mid-1995 he moved to a listserv; told to keep the personal name because it signaled something quirky and personal, he did. That set the pattern for everything since: people suggest, he figures out what many are asking for, does it, and listens again, so the site is almost entirely built from user requests. He wrote Perl to turn his email folders into web pages, giving instant publishing, and kept using Pine as his database until switching to MySQL in late 1999; craigslist now runs on well over a hundred small, cheap Linux servers.

By the end of 1997 the site drew a million page views a month, and when Microsoft Sidewalk's people approached him about running banner ads, he declined because ads would slow the site and were mostly dumb, and because he already had enough money as a contractor. That decision crystallized what he calls his moral compass. A volunteer effort to run craigslist as a nonprofit failed through 1998, and it was only after two big job posters took him to lunch and told him to get real that he emerged from denial, went full-time in 1999, and hired a handful of people for billing, customer service, and technology. The turn came when Jim Buckmaster joined as lead programmer in early 2000 and was made CEO, freeing Newmark, who recognized he was a poor manager and interviewer, to become "customer service rep and founder," doing forty hours a week of support and occasional figurehead speaking.

Newmark never took investment, funding craigslist only with his own time, and the company stayed tiny, around twenty employees, by charging for less than one percent of listings, chiefly job ads and later New York apartment-broker listings, a policy the community itself proposed on the principle of charging those who would otherwise pay more for less effective ads. The site polices itself through flagging, an idea from his customer service team that effectively hands day-to-day control to users and expresses a culture of trust; even the sleaziest brokers, blocked repeatedly for bait-and-switch, eventually reformed and asked to be charged to raise quality. His hardest moments came from misjudging whom to trust, producing personnel disputes and legal settlements he cannot discuss. eBay acquired a 25 percent stake in 2004, not from the company but from a former employee selling his granted equity, and craigslist remained independent, spreading to well over a hundred cities. His advice is simply to trust your instincts and your moral compass.

## Caterina Fake and Flickr

Caterina Fake and Stewart Butterfield, who married after a long-distance courtship that began with Stewart's suggestion they start a company together, launched Ludicorp with Jason Classon two days after their honeymoon in the summer of 2002. Their product was Game Neverending, a lightweight web-based massively multiplayer game built around social interaction, instant messaging, and trading rather than sword-and-sorcery, inspired partly by Neopets, whose in-game markets had addicted Fake. She brought years of online-community experience from the WELL, Electric Minds, and Netscape communities, and they soon added Eric Costello, a renowned DHTML developer working remotely from New York. Working from cheaply sublet space during the post-crash bust, when both talented developers and offices were unusually available, they operated under the belief that constraints inspire creativity.

Flickr began as a lark. When the game's back-end development fell behind the front end, the restless team built an instant-messenger application in which people could form communities and drag-and-drop photos onto each other's desktops, adding photo sharing as a feature. They built the first version in eight weeks, conceiving it in December and presenting it at the O'Reilly Emerging Technology Conference in February 2004. As a mere feature it suffered a critical-mass problem, valuable only if your friends were already on it, and it grew slowly until they added the ability to put photographs on a web page. Fake credits their naïveté: because they never researched the market, they avoided copying incumbents like Ofoto, Shutterfly, and Snapfish, which treated photo sharing as a loss leader funneling users toward prints. Flickr instead rode a convergence of blogging, social networking, and camera phones, and borrowed from blog culture the radical default that a photo is public.

By July 2004 the six-person team had to put Game Neverending on hold despite its 20,000 signed-up testers and their own attachment to it, because Flickr's momentum could not be argued with. The reversal in financing was total: venture capitalists who had not understood the game were now calling three or four times a week, yet the founders declined venture capital, taking only a small angel round from investors including Esther Dyson and Reid Hoffman plus an interest-free Canadian government loan they won by simply resubmitting a rejected application. Near break-even and already straining to scale, they saw no need to make the big bet that venture money implied, especially as acquirers had circled almost from the moment Flickr was still just an IM client.

Tagging transformed the product, letting users organize their own photos while browsing everyone else's public ones by keyword, so a global Tokyo tag surfaced strangers' images and breaking events emerged spontaneously; when the Australian embassy in Jakarta was bombed, three of Flickr's then-60,000 users uploaded tagged photos within a day. Groups like Squared Circle turned photography into collaborative creativity. Yahoo acquired Flickr in March 2005. Fake also speaks candidly about institutionalized sexism, recalling a venture capitalist who told an associate that Stewart should not bring his wife to meetings, prompting Stewart to insist her contributions equaled his; women, she argues, must arrive twice as prepared, and she has worked through groups like the Forum for Women Entrepreneurs to give them a fairer shot. Her marriage worked as a partnership because their personalities were complementary, his improvisational and hers directed toward a goal.

## Brewster Kahle, WAIS, Alexa, and the Internet Archive

Brewster Kahle learned his founding philosophy at Thinking Machines, where he helped commercialize the MIT Connection Machine work, absorbing the lesson to learn on someone else's nickel during a research phase before spending his own money. The company, funded with \$8 million from wealthy believers rather than venture capital, could take a leisurely first year and drew figures like Richard Feynman, Marvin Minsky, and Stephen Wolfram; it also taught him to choose surroundings that provoke unconventional thinking, being set in an 1800s Victorian mansion on a hundred wooded acres where a stuck engineer could take a long walk. There Kahle prototyped WAIS in his spare time, an open-protocol system for asking remote machines questions that predated Gopher and the Web and was, he believes, the first Internet publishing system, built as a project with Apple, Dow Jones, and KPMG Peat Marwick.

When Thinking Machines declined to build the central tools the vision needed, Kahle left with Harry Morris to start not just a company but an industry, carefully basing WAIS on public-domain software to avoid an intellectual-property fight. Heeding his mentor Bill Dunn's advice to "go someplace where people don't think you're crazy," he chose California over recession-era Boston, bootstrapped with no savings, and funded the first year on a contract building the Perot campaign's information system. WAIS pioneered giving away client and server software while selling upgrades and services, an early instance of the internet-as-distribution model that spread to roughly 10,000 servers, and spun up what he calls the first web studio, working with best-in-class customers like the Wall Street Journal, Encyclopedia Britannica, and the U.S. Congress. His goal was always to get others to copy him so publishing would move onto an open Net, and he considered it won when AOL embraced internet protocols in 1994 and Microsoft backed the Web in August 1995; AOL bought the roughly thirty-five-person WAIS in 1995.

The AOL acquisition was painful because his little company was dispersed, teaching him how hard acquisitions are for entrepreneurs. He next built two organizations at once: Alexa Internet, a for-profit named for the Library of Alexandria, and the nonprofit Internet Archive, designed to outlast any company by receiving everything Alexa collected. Cofounding Alexa with the business-minded Bruce Gilliat, whom he likens to a spouse in the difficulty and value of the match, he raised \$1 million and then a single private investor's backing. Alexa's toolbar showed related links for any page through web-scale collaborative filtering, tracking where people went, an idea rooted in a Thinking Machines conversation with Carl Feynman; crucially, Alexa deleted much of that data as a matter of ethics, a discipline he warns later imitators abandoned.

Alexa gathered tens of millions of users as Netscape and then Microsoft bundled it, but its contextual-advertising business never worked, so when Amazon came looking for data-mining technology Kahle steered the conversation toward running Alexa as an independent company. Having learned from AOL, he insisted on autonomy, and Jeff Bezos agreed, keeping Alexa as a separate think tank with monthly all-day meetings; Alexa promptly repaid its own acquisition by teaching Amazon to stop overspending on hardware and to cut connection costs by ninety percent. He stayed three years before moving full-time to the Internet Archive to build the great library he had wanted since the 1970s. His guiding beliefs, drawn from Marvin Minsky, are to pick a project big and hard enough to work on for decades and to show a pointable accomplishment every year, since everything takes far longer than anyone expects.

## Charles Geschke and Adobe

At Xerox PARC, Chuck Geschke and John Warnock built Interpress so any computer could talk to any printer, but when Xerox management said commercializing it would take at least seven years, two or three technology generations, the pair grew frustrated enough to leave. Warnock's thesis advisor Dave Evans sat on the board of Hambrecht & Quist and introduced them to Bill Hambrecht, who backed their plan to build internal publishing systems for the Fortune 500 and lent them \$50,000 personally so they could quit and lease a Vax. In their early forties with families, Geschke felt little fear because a computer-science PhD guarantees work; the only real risk was the stigma of failure. They named the company Adobe after the creek behind Geschke's house and incorporated in December 1982.

The plan almost immediately proved wrong in a productive way. Gordon Bell of Digital Equipment and then Steve Jobs each visited, and each said he did not need Adobe's computers or printers, only the software that connected them, which their own teams were failing to build. Their board chair Q.T. Wiles told them to throw out the business plan, since customers were revealing what the business should be, and Adobe pivoted to licensing PostScript. They signed Apple in December 1983 to build the LaserWriter, and Jobs prepaid royalties and bought just under twenty percent of the company, quintupling the original investors' money and making Adobe profitable within its first twelve months. PostScript's breakthrough was describing type as pure mathematical outlines with algorithms deciding which bits to render, replacing the impossible labor of hand-tuning bitmaps for every size, face, and device.

The LaserWriter nearly died when Apple's salespeople balked at a printer costing more than the computer, since it contained a more powerful computer than the Macintosh and needed pricey RAM, but a timely dip in RAM prices and Jobs's resolve pushed it out in 1985. Sales sagged until a young Apple marketer, John Scull, united Apple, Aldus, and Adobe behind a campaign called "desktop publishing," which, paired with Aldus PageMaker, turned the industry digital within a decade. Geschke, whose father and grandfather were letterpress photo engravers, understood the toxic analog world being replaced. He and Warnock resolved never to be a one-product, single-channel company, building Adobe Illustrator, prompted by Warnock hand-coding PostScript for his graphic-designer wife, and investing in Photoshop from the Knoll brothers two or three years before the market was ready, on the hunter's logic of shooting where the duck is going to be.

The defining crisis came at the September 1989 Seybold Conference, where Microsoft and Apple allied against Adobe: Bill Gates announced Windows would use Apple's TrueType and a PostScript clone so Apple could stop paying Adobe royalties. Adobe, unable to strike a licensing deal despite having working prototypes, responded by rushing Adobe Type Manager to market on both platforms for around \$99, selling hundreds of thousands of units while Apple and Microsoft took three years to ship, by which point Apple returned and redid the PostScript deal. When conference organizers polled 1,500 publishing professionals on whether they wanted Apple and Microsoft to put Adobe out of business, only a couple dozen hands rose, vindicating the founders' gospel of treating customers as people whose success you are responsible for. Geschke's lessons run deeper than any product: keep moving on even from successes, as Xerox failed to do with the copier, invest today in where the market will be, and pursue the engineer's real thrill, which is impact rather than money.

## Ann Winblad and Open Systems

Ann Winblad grew up the oldest of six in a middle-class Minnesota family, always hustling for money, and exploited a five-college cooperative in St. Paul to double major in mathematics and business while taking every computer science class available. Finding accounting trivial after set theory, she felt superhuman, joined the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis under 1970s affirmative-action hiring, earned a master's at night to become only the second woman in the entire Federal Reserve system to hold one, and, after a quick promotion, concluded the work was uninspiring and the risk of leaving negligible. In 1976 she started Open Systems with three Federal Reserve colleagues, two of whom kept a year's sabbatical as a safety net; incorporation exhausted her savings, leaving her \$500 short, which she borrowed from her high-school-aged brother.

With no funding, the founders consulted by day and built the real product separately at night, a discipline Winblad still preaches: find money to cover the business rather than compromising the business to raise money. A contract to build a student accounting system for a Minnesota vocational school taught them estimation, project management, and how to be paid while learning on the job. They then reopened her college accounting textbook and wrote accounting software from scratch, first for minicomputers and then for the CADO machine built on Intel's early commodity 8080A processor, deliberately OEM-ing a fast language interpreter rather than relying on Microsoft's weak early Basic, a choice that let them skip the wave of application vendors and platforms that soon died. Their genuine computing background, unusual among the era's hobbyist founders, kept them on a steady growth path without ever having to restart the company.

Much of the education was improvised and harrowing. Pitching CADO's sixty-odd older resellers from atop a crate because the podium dwarfed her, Winblad ran a "blue light special" and flew home with roughly a dozen personal checks for \$10,000 each, some \$120,000 in her handbag, after the CEO George Ryan told her that to run with the big dogs she had to learn to lift her leg. An apartment-office fire spared the computers and software while burning her cheerleading letters and yearbooks, a signal it was time to take real office space. At their first Comdex, a solid oak booth shipped from Minnesota became "booth hell" when a crane operator fell inside the neon tower and medics wanted to saw it in half; they pulled him out by his feet and rebuilt the shattered signage overnight. To reach retail stores, they shrink-wrapped software on a machine bought from a defunct pizza company, filling the office with the smell of burned cheese.

Winblad credits an ensemble of equals who vetted every idea against one another, a dynamic she now looks for as a venture capitalist. Open Systems sold for more than \$15 million about six years in, and she later cofounded Hummer Winblad, the first venture firm focused solely on software. Her hardest-won lesson distinguishes the inventor from the business leader: a founder must stop fending for their own salary and net worth and fend instead for shareholders, customers, employees, and mission, because most companies die not from competition but from self-inflicted wounds by a flawed leadership team. Her prescriptive advice is compact: think like a big dog, then find the leverage to get there.

## David Heinemeier Hansson and 37signals

Jason Fried founded 37signals as a web design shop in 1999, and David Heinemeier Hansson joined from Copenhagen as a contract programmer while finishing his bachelor's degree, writing code while the others handled design. As the consultancy grew, its client work became disorganized enough to look unprofessional, so the team took the blogging idea, drawn from Hansson's personal blog Loud Thinking and the company's Signal vs. Noise, and applied it to project management. They built the project blog in about a month, then began using Basecamp to build Basecamp, making it self-contained almost immediately. Showing it to respected colleagues revealed a shared problem: the gap between messy email threads and heavyweight critical-path tools like Microsoft Project, which suited a 200-person construction site but not three people shipping a web application.

37signals funded the work from consultancy revenue rather than outside money, giving Basecamp only about ten hours of Hansson's week, a quarter of a programmer, with designers contributing at most a third of their time. Those constraints became the strategy, captured in the mantra of making less software; what impressed early viewers was everything Basecamp deliberately did not do. Hansson argues Microsoft could not have replicated it by assigning twenty people for six months, because abundant resources tempt teams to overbuild, and even the same people with unlimited time and money could never make Basecamp again. They picked a few simple elements, a project weblog, milestone tracking, and file and to-do sharing, and refined rather than expanded them; ironically, by never adding features specific to creative-services firms, they opened Basecamp to weddings, home improvement, and student collaboration.

They spent nothing on advertising. A single project was free for life, the upgrade curve climbed gently from \$9 to a top plan of \$99 a month, and monthly billing kept customer risk low. Because Signal vs. Noise already had a large following, they built the audience first and the product second, blogging previews before launch and going viral from there. The mistakes were instructive: three days before launch the bank refused annual billing, unwilling to be liable if the young company folded mid-contract, which forced a monthly model that delayed release by a month yet raised effective prices while lowering customer risk. A one-firm, one-client assumption buried deep in the database took roughly a year and a half to repair, and a Central Time default quietly made Australian milestones arrive a day late.

Hansson remained the sole programmer and systems administrator for well over a year, and to sustain that he moved from PHP to Ruby for its productivity and extracted Ruby on Rails piece by piece from Basecamp, pulling out whatever looked generic into a growing toolbox. Basecamp launched in February 2005 at only 4,000 lines of code, and he held Rails back about two months to document it properly, annoyed by the poor documentation of the open source he had relied on, before releasing it and later winning Hacker of the Year at OSCON 2005. Rails aims for the common eighty percent rather than the situation-specific last twenty. His broader lessons run past minimalism: treat the urge to do too much as a clue to rescope, always ship a visible major update within thirty days of launch to reinforce buyers, innovate on behalf of customers instead of letting them drive, and be willing to tell a customer that Basecamp is not for them. He rejected the flood of venture calls on the theory that too much money destroys focus, and preferred scaling users without scaling staff, treating the distributed team's seven-hour gap as interruption-free alone time and low-bandwidth instant messaging as a feature.

## Philip Greenspun and ArsDigita

Philip Greenspun had built multiuser Internet applications since the early 1980s and, as soon as the Web appeared, concluded it was how all applications would eventually be built. When MIT professors dismissed Internet applications as having no future, he drove to Alaska for a summer, writing a weekly chapter that became the travelogue Travels with Samantha, named for his laptop because he was between dogs. Reader questions were mostly about photography, so he built a question-and-answer forum that took on a life of its own and grew into the photo.net community, for which he wrote an ever-larger toolkit. Noticing that other publishers' sites were broken and buggy, he released his code as the open source ArsDigita Community System, pitching it as SAP for building online communities. When big companies with thousands of their own programmers began offering \$100,000 for two weeks of customization, he and some friends formed ArsDigita in 1997; Oracle forced them from loose 1099 individuals into a real corporation, and a dog ban at MIT's Tech Square, against his \$1,300 monthly stipend, pushed him to rent the photographer Elsa Dorfman's house as an office.

ArsDigita amazed customers with three things most programmers ignored: actually listening to what the customer wanted, caring about the end-user experience, and meeting deadlines. Greenspun pushed profit-and-loss responsibility down to teams of two or three who sat with clients as engineers rather than order-taking coders, shared bonuses, and were held accountable, because he wanted programmers to become true professionals with independent reputations instead of interchangeable, offshore-able puzzle-solvers. Levi Strauss, wanting a web front end that measured customers for custom khakis, funded broadly reusable infrastructure, which was ideal because non-technical firms did not insist on owning the code. Marketing was almost entirely educational: the 1998 book Database Backed Web Sites, free online tutorials, and one-day seminars that drew 400 people, a couple of whom would become customers. Because releases were tested on real running sites like photo.net, they could ship every two or three months while Microsoft-style rivals developed software as if the Web did not exist. By 2000 the company had roughly \$20 million in revenue, three to four million in profit, and was growing 500 percent a year.

The fatal difficulty was hiring businesspeople, since first-rate managers rationally preferred the resources of a GE or Microsoft, and underwriters admitted they would only take venture-branded companies public without diligence. Chasing an IPO and recruits dazzled by big VC names, Greenspun let his MBAs choose Greylock and General Atlantic over the humbler Summit and took \$38 million. The senior partners delegated junior directors with no profit-and-loss, engineering, or startup experience, installed a smooth-talking CEO and an innumerate CFO, and dismantled the team-accountability model into functional silos where no one below the CEO owned profit. Marginalized and effectively fired, Greenspun used his majority-shareholder rights to call a meeting, change the bylaws, and retake the board; the VCs then had their pet CEO write a \$1 million company check to their own lawyers and sued him in Delaware Chancery Court, a minority-shareholder case that could not be won, before simply buying him out. Left with about \$40 million in cash, management fell into classic second-system syndrome, telling customers to wait for a J2EE rewrite that arrived more than a year and a half late, ran up to a thousand times slower, and never solved the upgrade problem it had promised. ArsDigita burned through the cash, handed its assets to Red Hat, and dissolved in early 2002. Greenspun's account is openly partisan, and he concedes he grew too fast and could have been kinder, but his lessons stay concrete: grow only as fast as culture can be transmitted, preserve clear profit responsibility, test software on real users, and always deliver the value the customer actually paid for.

## Joel Spolsky and Fog Creek Software

Joel Spolsky and Michael Pryor, friends from Juno Online Services, founded Fog Creek in 2000 with no specific product, reasoning that if barely competent kids were getting \$100 million valuations, merely semi-competent people should be able to do at least as well. The direct inspiration was Philip Greenspun's ArsDigita, whose model of using consulting cash flow to fund a product they admired but wanted to improve on two points: they would license their software rather than open-source it, so revenue could grow like a hockey curve instead of linearly with headcount, and they would drop ArsDigita's reflexive fear of everything Microsoft. The consulting arbitrage was stark, paying a bright MIT grad roughly \$60 an hour while billing \$200 to \$250, and their intended edge was spending a few percent more on a humane environment, Aeron chairs, private offices with doors, first-class flights, and never being away from home on a weekend, to win the best programmers whom the crammed New York shops treated as interchangeable cogs. Joel on Software, written during a summer off in a beach house, supplied their first three clients.

Then the consulting market vanished in November 2000, though almost no one recognized it until April, because the sales cycle simply kept lengthening by about a day per day rather than anyone actually saying no. Firms like ArsDigita, Razorfish, and Scient kept paying idle consultants and hemorrhaged to death, while Fog Creek, still tiny, survived by wrapping up an internal bug tracker as FogBugz and finding that people bought it. Two other planned products, CityDesk and the never-written Tintin, disappointed or vanished, but FogBugz soon brought in \$5,000 to \$10,000 a month, enough to live on after two hired consultants were laid off. Spolsky put in about \$50,000 of his own money and often took no salary, working from his grandmother's Manhattan brownstone. Each stronger release produced not a temporary hump but a permanent step up in sales, because a better product wins more of the evaluations, and counterintuitively raising the price twice increased unit sales by making the product look substantial rather than like shareware.

Their biggest recurring mistake was chasing marketing gimmicks, affiliate programs, coupons, and a fruitless search for a partner company to sell their software, when nothing ever beat simply improving the product and talking directly to customers; he likens the schemes to General Motors inventing rebates because it forgot how to design cars people want to buy. They refused all outside investment after watching VCs push out ArsDigita's founders, prizing the freedom to keep private offices and to say no to distracting pet projects a board would force on them. Spolsky's mantra, formed at Microsoft where he saw disciplines like bug tracking, written specifications, and usability testing that most companies simply lacked, is to listen to your customers, not your competitors; he cheerfully ignores the roughly seven FogBugz clones that copied the product but missed the blog and the culture that made it work. He now wants to build a factory that reliably turns ideas into shipped software, a capability first proven when four summer interns built Fog Creek Copilot in a single summer. His blunt advice to would-be founders is to quit the day job, recruit at least one real cofounder, and treasure the elegant hack of realizing what everyone thinks you need but you can actually do without.

## Stephen Kaufer and TripAdvisor

TripAdvisor grew from Stephen Kaufer's own frustration when he and his wife Caroline could not find honest information about vacation destinations; booking sites were everywhere, but the real scoop hid in chat rooms and someone's home page. After a year on ice, in early 2000 he assembled trusted former colleagues plus a business-side cofounder, Langley Steinert, raised a first round, and worked rent-free above a pizza place in space owned by his wife's declining software company. The plan was a travel-focused search engine that surfaced unbiased opinion rather than prices. Automatic crawling from hubs like Yahoo Travel produced too much noise, so they instead hired people to read every travel article they could find, spending about half an hour on each to summarize and tag it by genuine topic rather than keywords. The result was a small but extremely relevant database in which every article on the Maui page really was about Maui; later, user reviews would dwarf those articles with fresher, more colorful, and more trusted content.

TripAdvisor.com was meant only as a demo, because the business model was licensing the database to portals like Expedia, Yahoo, and AOL under a "Powered by TripAdvisor" badge. But after eighteen months only Lycos had signed, and its quarterly check would not even cover the weekly employee lunch, since everyone else wanted to be paid to feature the content rather than pay for it. September 11 froze the pipeline, and the eight-person company, slimmed from eleven with salary cuts, faced running out of money in the first half of 2002. Meanwhile the demo was quietly drawing about 5,000 visitors a day. A copied Expedia banner produced only a hundred near-worthless clicks, but Kaufer recalled a prospect's earlier question about query-based ads and realized that a person reading a review of the Eliot Hotel was declaring an intent to book it. He deep-linked such readers straight to Expedia's booking page, ran a free trial month, and before it ended Expedia offered \$10,000 for December, then \$20,000, then uncapped orders.

Because the links were so relevant, roughly ten percent of viewers clicked, against an industry norm of a quarter to half a percent, and TripAdvisor went from no revenue to break-even in about four months, needing only \$75,000 a month, before adding Travelocity, Hotels.com, and Orbitz. Kaufer's caution about spending, favoring small teams over big ones and refusing lavish offices, was deliberate, and it was tested when a major company offered \$30,000 to \$50,000 a month to license the database, enough to cover half the burn rate at a moment when the alternative looked like death. But the terms let the licensee walk away after two years with a copy of the database and the tools to maintain it, effectively handing a future competitor the crown jewels, so Kaufer chose to risk going out of business instead; his advice for such moments is to keep pushing rather than saying yes or no too fast. He credits Langley's business-development role as indispensable to an engineer-led team.

The user reviews that became TripAdvisor's core carry important caveats: staff screen every submission against family-friendly, non-hateful guidelines and either reject it or allow it unedited, but make no attempt to verify factual accuracy, relying on conduit protections comparable to a phone carrier's, offering hotels a response form, and using human and algorithmic methods to catch owners who post fake praise, sometimes from the very email address they had just used to complain. TripAdvisor became the world's largest travel community and was acquired by Barry Diller's IAC in 2004 for around \$200 million, which let it keep running independently. Kaufer's lessons are to choose the founding team carefully and settle roles early, to hold the vision loosely because plans always change, to do whatever an early customer wants in order to win the reference account, to take cheap reversible risks and expect frequent small failures, and above all to be fanatical about hiring, since a great hire may cost twenty percent more than a good one but deliver two or three times the throughput.

## James Hong and HOT or NOT

HOT or NOT started as a joke among James Hong, Jim Young, and Hong's brother after Young called a woman a perfect ten. Hong imagined a service where people submitted photographs and strangers rated them. With Hong unemployed and Young exhausted by his doctorate, they built it within days. Hong's retired father became absorbed in the prototype. After Hong emailed forty friends, the site received about 40,000 first-day hits and spread unaided.

HOT or NOT began as a joke while James Hong, his friend Jim Young, and Hong's brother were drinking and Young called a girl he met a perfect ten. Hong, then building the web-services directory XMethods with his brother, imagined a consumer service where people posted photos and strangers rated them one to ten, originally as a widget floating random faces across an office worker's screen. With Young burned out on his PhD and Hong unemployed after business school, they coded it in spare time between one Monday and Thursday with no urgency. The first outside viewer was Hong's retired sixty-year-old father, who became instantly addicted, hinting the idea had legs. They launched with their own pictures, and after Hong emailed forty friends asking them to be nice, the site drew about 40,000 hits on the first day and spread on its own, as he confirmed by evangelizing a stranger in the Tellme parking lot and days later hearing the name given back to him.

Popularity was an immediate emergency rather than a plan. A Salon.com interview the next morning threatened to slam a server his brother needed for XMethods, and because they hosted the images themselves, Hong calculated that bandwidth alone would cost at least \$150,000 a year while he carried \$60,000 in grad-school debt, having turned down a \$170,000 consulting job. At 12:30 a.m. in an In-N-Out drive-through he remembered Dave Winer's dismissive remark that Yahoo GeoCities let you FTP files, and realized they need not host pictures at all, only point the database at user-uploaded URLs and take twenty-five new photos a day. They moved every image to Yahoo by 3 a.m., then hid a free 400-megahertz Celeron, obtained by opening an eTrade account, under Young's Berkeley desk, guarding its power switch with a pushpin case and a wall of books. Hong slept only eight hours across the first eight days, until he was shaking and forced to rest.

Survival kept improvising a business. Riding the heavy press, Hong persuaded Rackspace's head of business development to supply machines by promising to make the site their scaling poster child in his interviews. Advertisers balked at pornographic submissions, so the founders coined the motto that the site would be fun, clean, and real, built community moderation that killed frequently flagged pictures, and, after the DoubleClick founder warned they must move to a guilty-until-proven-innocent model, recruited public moderators by essay once Hong's parents could no longer stomach the images. The anti-porn "Meet" feature, which required both people to be active, only later slapped them in the face as a product; charging \$6 a month as an impulse price turned it into roughly half a million dollars a year, the accident that finally gave the MBA-holding, formerly venture-backed Hong the one model that worked, while an Ofoto affiliate deal even converted image hosting from a cost into a small revenue source.

They took no outside money, remaining just Hong, Young, and Hong's brother as equal partners, so Hong's CEO title was nominal. A Howard Stern mention triggered a cease-and-desist from a site called Am I Hot, forcing a rebrand to hotornot.com and an eventual cheap buyout of that rival's assets, and they declined an early Lycos offer of \$3 to \$5 million because they feared a corporate owner would turn it lame and kill it. HOT or NOT stayed independent and had counted about thirteen billion votes by 2006. Hong's advice is to start young before mortgages and dependents create liabilities, to accept that there is no right path or magic formula, to spend even raised money as if it were your own and stay cheap and lean, and to expect entrepreneurship to be painful and emotionally unstable; the hardest decision, he insists, is made years earlier, in simply choosing to forgo the safe path and become an entrepreneur at all.

## James Currier and Tickle

James Currier was a former venture capitalist with a passion for digital media and social science who, watching companies like Broadcast.com and Spinner.com sell for hundreds of millions merely by putting radio, magazines, and other old media online, was sure the Internet would grow its own native, user-generated form. The insight crystallized at Harvard Business School when a Myers-Briggs test, filled in with a number-two pencil, returned plain black-and-white results eleven days later and set his classmates talking for two weeks, longer than anyone ever discussed a movie. Reasoning that people are their own favorite subject and that every prior medium only told us about strangers we would never meet, he saw self-assessment tests, many trapped in the academic ivory tower, as viral, data-rich, and nearly free to distribute, a foundation for a company that would even do some good if it failed. He organized psychologists, writers, and engineers around online testing and named it Emode.

He found the vision almost impossible to convey, partly because few people can picture what does not yet exist and everyone was mesmerized by the era's media darlings. Starting a company, he says, is collecting and organizing the human energy in the ether, and everyone around you demands the next confidence point, a name, a business plan, employees, an office, business cards, a website, traffic, before they will commit. Each spark point pulls in more people, and his arrived when venture term sheets suddenly made PR firms, bankers, and acquirers materialize; Women.com even offered \$32 million once those term sheets supplied outside credibility.

The serious tests on anxiety, parenting, and relationships drew almost nobody, so, recalling the advertising wisdom that babies and puppies make people remember, Currier added an unscientific fifteen-question quiz for which dog breed you resemble; eight days later a million people were trying to enter, the server crashed every ten minutes, and the team unplugged the machine, drove it to an ISP in Lynn, Massachusetts, and hooked it to a T3. Traffic finally unlocked venture money after forty-three pitches, though he learned that investors found his honest \$18 million projection uninteresting and his inflated \$50 million one pretty interesting, a lesson in always appearing a step ahead so others need not absorb your risk.

The hardest trials were human: an unstable VP of engineering had to be fired with a marshal on standby, and an HR chief he empowered wrote herself five months of full-pay maternity leave while the founders went unsalaried. He woke on October 3, 1999 at four in the morning, chest burning with fear that he had squandered his friends' and mentors' money, slept four hours a night on NyQuil, and nearly failed to marry his girlfriend until a trusted new engineering VP gave him room to feel again. His defensibility, he believes, was cultural: iVillage copied Tickle for about a year before fading, because it is hard to make psychologists, writers, and engineers truly understand one another, which takes empathy, a communicate-don't-blame culture, and teammates who are at once nice, articulate, smart, and capable. Tickle was acquired by Monster in 2004 for about \$100 million, which worked because Monster left the team and mission intact, but Currier's central caveat is the one HBS never taught him: entrepreneurship is pain, and it will consume your presence in every softer part of life.

## Blake Ross and Firefox

Blake Ross began contributing to the open source Mozilla project in 2000 and interned at Netscape at fourteen. After AOL bought Netscape for about \$4 billion and, post-crash, demanded a return, the browser degenerated into a vehicle to drive traffic to Netscape.com, cluttered with search buttons and advertisements, and pop-up blocking that was ready to ship was forbidden because Netscape.com itself served pop-ups. Firefox was therefore a reaction to Netscape rather than to Internet Explorer, whose team Microsoft had disbanded in 2001, leaving the market wide open. Ross, David Hyatt, Joe Hewitt, and a few allies started an after-hours project, sometimes coding at Denny's, called Phoenix for the bird reborn from its own ashes. They forked the Mozilla code and deliberately closed off access, convinced that nothing consumer-focused could emerge from a thousand Netscape people chasing revenue and a thousand open source purists who shunned big business all trying to reach consensus.

Trademark complaints pushed Phoenix to Firebird, which collided with an existing open source database, and then through a string of Fire-anything names to Firefox, the Chinese name for the red panda. The team was globally distributed, with contributors from New Zealand, France, and Slovakia, and Hyatt eventually left to build Apple's competing Safari. The first milestone, quietly dropped on an FTP site and noted by the community site mozillaZine, drew as many downloads as an official Mozilla release, splitting the base between hardcore developers who thought catering to mom and dad heretical and those relieved Mozilla was finally going mainstream. Ross's persistent tension was convincing five hundred flesh-and-blood developers that their pet feature might not serve five hundred million silent imaginary users. After Netscape spun Mozilla off as an independent foundation with no distributor, the organization had to court ordinary users directly or collapse.

Firefox 1.0 launched in 2004 on grassroots marketing, and Ross discovered that marketing was simply making a product good enough that people spread it themselves and handing them the tools to do so; he now cannot stand professional marketers crafting the message. Spread Firefox, which he and Asa Dotzler started, gave students, Toastmasters, and other noncoders a role and drew more than 250,000 sign-ups, while 10,000 donors funded two facing full-page New York Times advertisements with no marketing budget. His press strategy was to win prominent bloggers first, which pulled in PC World and CNET and finally the mainstream outlets, who made "everyone is talking about Firefox" a self-fulfilling prophecy. Firefox's freedom came from being free software backed by donations, AOL seed money, and later a Google deal, so it could fail without ruining anyone; Ross defined success by users, not by beating Microsoft, whom he faults for being driven by competition rather than by users.

He was surprised how easily Firefox took off despite years of analysts insisting browsers were dead and the market monopolized, an experience that left him skeptical of industry insiders who predict without building. He barely told his own parents and friends he worked on Firefox until a magazine article revealed it, preferring the freedom to fail silently and return to school, which he half did during a leisurely six-month break, knowing Microsoft was not coming back soon. His carried-forward lessons are to stay in constant contact with eventual users rather than locking yourself in a room to code, to build honest buzz through technical advocates before chasing the mainstream, and to under-promise; his new startup Parakey with Joe Hewitt inverted the freedom of Firefox, since fame now brings pressure, unsolicited investor attention, difficulty telling genuine advisors from sharks, and the discovery that time, not capital, is the scarcest resource.

## Mena Trott and Six Apart

Mena Trott started a personal blog called Dollarshort in April 2001 as a creative outlet and, having no real friends after years spent joined at the hip with her husband Ben since high school, a way to connect with people online. When their employer folded and they were laid off, they built their own blogging tool, Movable Type, in their Richmond apartment and released it in October 2001 expecting only donations and items off their Amazon Wish List. About 2,000 people had signed up on her blog to be notified, and having paying customers from day one is what forced them into being a company; they never set out to solve anyone's problem but her own and a few bloggers'. They never actively begged for money, keeping one modest donation page and rewarding roughly \$20 gifts with recently-updated keys, and reached break-even within two months.

Around July 2002 they briefly tried consulting on Movable Type customizations, hated it, and once even paid an unpaid invoice out of their own pocket, so they chose the harder path of going straight to consumers with a hosted service, TypePad. Working back to back at desks in a spare bedroom for eighteen months, they went a two-year stretch never more than six hours apart; the strain blurred marriage and work until an office and, later, a rule against discussing work at home restored some separation. Investor Joi Ito approached not as a suitor but as a user asking technical questions and donating, which made them feel obliged to respond, and at the Supernova conference his colleague Barak Berkowitz bluntly told them their goal of 3,000 users was a niche lifestyle business he would not fund. That challenge, plus a free trip to Japan and the fear that Blogger would take the money and leave them a footnote, changed their minds.

Neoteny invested under a million dollars, an anomaly for a company with no structure, no real accounting, and only an LLC they had chosen off a California incorporation checklist precisely because it said it could not take money, not realizing they could simply convert to a C corp later. Terrified of dilution, they treated the money as their own, delayed hiring and an office until August, and balked at a \$150 refrigerator; Mena later concluded there is a better middle road between Bubble excess and harmful austerity. They nearly killed themselves building the first TypePad alone and repeatedly replacing hardware they had skimped on. Barak proved invaluable, working six months without salary on nothing but a promise to be made CEO, helping with insurance, wiring, and strategy while letting them learn from mistakes like renting an office they outgrew in eight months.

Their biggest public misstep was Movable Type licensing: reasonably wanting businesses profiting from the free software to pay, they set blog and author limits but applied them too broadly to personal users, and the community, long treating the Trotts as industry darlings, flipped out, teaching Mena that the moment you charge, people who believe Internet things should be free recast you as evil. She refuses to apologize for wanting to be a paid, ethical company, comparing the backlash to the "Ev sold out" reaction when Blogger went to Google. The cost was real: before TypePad launched, overwhelmed by paying customers and unable to see any light at the end of the tunnel, she broke down crying in the shower, and public dissection eventually curtailed her blogging. Yet she frames it with gratitude, saying the Japan trip alone would have justified everything, and credits the couple's mutual competitiveness, dating back to raising her own high-school grades to match her valedictorian husband, as the single engine behind Six Apart.

## Bob Davis and Lycos

Lycos grew from a 1994 federal research grant that let Carnegie Mellon computer scientist Michael "Fuzzy" Mauldin build a search engine in a closeted office. Mauldin wanted to remain a research scientist, so through CMU's tech-transfer office the technology reached Dan Nova of CMGI, whose small \$35 million @Ventures fund, run by the prescient Dave Wetherell, bought 80 percent while Mauldin and the university kept 10 percent apiece. Bob Davis, an unhappy VP of sales for a company that sold memory for IBM mainframes, heard about the deal from his friend Nova and volunteered on the spot to be CEO. For about a week in June 1995 he ran a company that did not yet exist, with no employees, customers, or products, only the technology; the first month was about building a team and deciding whether Lycos was a technology licensor, a media company, or a hybrid, a confusion he briefly labeled Technomedia before abandoning licensing to become a pure advertising-supported consumer brand.

Their CMU agreement forced a meaningful Pittsburgh presence even though headquarters were in Boston, a split Davis likened to oceans between the offices; the Pittsburgh operation peaked near 300 employees and hired its first engineers, including Fuzzy's student assistant, straight out of the university. Because Lycos, Yahoo, Infoseek, and Excite sold broadly similar products, Davis differentiated on brand rather than technology, positioning Lycos as the Internet on training wheels, a safe family sedan, a Ford Taurus rather than a Maserati. He counted three customers, employees, advertisers, and users, and always ranked users first. The first paying advertiser, AT&T, placed a euphoric \$5,000 insertion order that instantly exposed the fact that Lycos had no way to serve a banner ad; engineers scrambled for about a week and a half to build one.

Lycos ran on roughly \$1.25 million of real working capital, having also owed Carnegie Mellon half of its first \$1.5 million in revenue, so it traded on-site "Powered by Sun" and later "Powered by Digital Equipment" credit for discounted hardware and struck licensing deals with AT&T, CompuServe, and Prodigy plus a Bertelsmann joint venture that seeded native-language Lycos Europe. Davis was a relentless PR evangelist who called publicity the cheapest advertising and pushed employees to spread the word until an eighteen-month snowball became unstoppable; the company also focused on earnings and turned profitable within about a year and a half, a rarity for the era. It went public in the fastest IPO in NASDAQ history and became the first to offer image search, which competitors copied within two months.

Explosive 200-to-300-percent annual growth meant that everything, accounting systems, data centers, and facilities, was perpetually crumbling and had to be outrun, an experience Davis found painful but satisfying to scale. His lowest point came in 1999 when the announced merger with Barry Diller's USA Networks, the first attempt to fuse offline and online assets, collapsed under shareholder dissent; the flood of publicity, however, coincided with Lycos overtaking Yahoo in April 1999 as the Web's most popular destination. A year later Terra Networks bought Lycos for \$5.4 billion, about a 300,000 percent return on its venture capital. Davis insists no company follows its original blueprint, quotes Machiavelli that a leader is judged by the strength of his generals, and credits the Lycos motto "Let up, you lose," daily perseverance through setbacks rather than any single turning point or uniquely superior technology.

## Ron Gruner, Alliant, and Shareholder.com

Ron Gruner joined Data General in 1969 as its 43rd employee and watched it grow past 15,000, moving from computer design to management in an almost Darwinian culture captured in The Soul of a New Machine, where he headed the losing Fountainhead team. In 1982 he, Craig Mundie, and Rich McAndrew left to found Alliant Computer Systems, aiming to use parallel processing to run existing Fortran programs four to ten times faster than Digital's top VAX for about fifty percent more money. Knowing money is raised on a sense of exclusivity and urgency, they took a couple hundred thousand dollars from angel investors, spent four to six months on an over-engineered business plan researched at the MIT library and with Illinois professor David Kuck, then pitched Kleiner Perkins, whose John Doerr and Tom Perkins liked the analogy to their Tandem win. A roughly \$4.7 million first round grew, with Hambrecht & Quist and Venrock, to about \$30 million across four rounds.

The machine took nearly three years and most of that \$30 million. Gruner filtered hires through a three-stage process he called chemistry, mechanics, and religion, revealing the secret parallel-processing project only after a candidate signed on and warning them up front that they must work every other Saturday for two years; of about forty people, attrition over those two years was one or two. The critical milestones were the Fujitsu gate arrays, a \$3 million bet, coming back working almost completely, and then the Fortran compiler successfully recompiling off-the-shelf VAX programs to run faster in parallel. Alliant announced in the summer of 1985, raced the Cray-compatible Convex, went public in December 1986, and reached a market value near \$475 million; revenue jumped from about \$5 million to \$30 million in a year.

Then it hit the wall. High-performance computers are the ultimate commodity, bought on megaflops per dollar like a tank of gas, so being late on one generation was punishing; simultaneously workstations from Sun and Apollo pulled customers like Bell Labs away from centralized machines, and the end of the Cold War dried up defense demand. The board split between Gruner's patient multi-year transition and a riskier bet on a new architecture, colliding with the venture dogma that the worst outcome is the living-dead company that grows steadily but never returns big. After ten years he was effectively fired for refusing the strategy he thought wrong; Alliant went bankrupt a year later, and he accepts real responsibility for leaving it unhealthy.

Reinventing himself in his early forties as hardware design died out, Gruner set deliberate criteria for his next company: recurring revenue, so he would never again scramble to close eighty percent of a quarter in its final two weeks; the freedom to spend ninety-eight percent of his time on customers rather than investors and lawyers; a bootstrap small enough to be a big fish in a small pond, managed quarter to quarter rather than to a five-year plan; and sole, controlling ownership. Recalling how at Alliant he had personally returned every anxious shareholder's call, even to a mom and pop in Topeka, and hearing that a friend had built telephone voicemail personal ads for the Boston Phoenix, he founded Shareholder.com in 1992 to replace stale mailed quarterly reports with toll-free Shareholder Direct hotlines. His IBM pitch swapped a \$1.5 million printing bill for a roughly \$250,000 service billed as a \$4,000 quarterly subscription plus per-minute fees, and by outsourcing the telephony to West Interactive he needed no capital; \$276,000 from eight to ten friends carried the company to profitability in the summer of 1994.

Determined not to repeat Alliant's slowness, he jumped on the Web in 1995, building Campbell's Soup's first website, and later rode SEC comfort letters, Regulation FD, and Sarbanes-Oxley as they forced companies to communicate with all investors. His scariest moment came when a pharmaceutical client printed over a million annual reports carrying the wrong 800 number weeks before a legally fixed deadline; an intractable paging company forced Gruner to hire a private investigator for \$200 to find the pager's owner in Dallas, who cheerfully surrendered the number for two years of pager subscriptions, turning near-catastrophe into a loyal client and more referrals. Against roughly \$85 million of venture money flooding the niche, Shareholder.com stayed on its \$276,000, restructured its entire pricing in a single afternoon that its lean structure allowed, and bought two failed competitors for pennies on the dollar before NASDAQ acquired it in January 2006. He concedes that extreme frugality left him myopic and that a modest raise might have grown the company faster, but he keeps his central lesson, echoing Michael Dell's one word, persistence, and the conviction that a disaster handled honestly can bind a customer closer than smooth service ever would.

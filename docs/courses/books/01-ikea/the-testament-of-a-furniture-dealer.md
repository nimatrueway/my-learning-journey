# The Testament of a Furniture Dealer

## The product range – our identity

Kamprad presents the product range as IKEA’s public identity and the practical expression of its purpose. In his philosophy, the company should furnish the whole home, indoors and outdoors, with useful objects that ordinary households can afford. Breadth matters because customers should be able to solve many related needs in one place. Yet breadth must be disciplined. Too many marginal products would absorb attention, complicate operations, and weaken the low-price position. The essential products in each category therefore deserve most of the effort.

The basic range should also be recognisable. Kamprad wants it to embody a simple, direct, durable, and informal way of living, expressed through cheerful colour and a youthful outlook rather than through fashion for its own sake. In Scandinavia this identity should feel characteristically IKEA; elsewhere it may read as Swedish. A small traditional range may coexist with it, but should not blur the central profile. This is less a neutral theory of taste than a founder’s prescription for keeping a growing company coherent.

His idea of the total home is expansive but not limitless. Tools, utensils, decoration, and components for do-it-yourself projects can belong when they support domestic life, and a small number of products may serve public interiors. The criterion is whether an addition strengthens the furnishing mission without forcing the range, and its costs, to sprawl. Selection is therefore part of design: saying no to a plausible item protects the price and identity of the products that remain.

Function and technical quality are equally central, but Kamprad rejects quality detached from use. A dining table needs a surface that tolerates hard wear, while a protected bookcase shelf may not justify the same expensive finish. Spending more on the shelf would raise the customer’s price without adding meaningful benefit. Product standards should therefore be sensible and long-lasting, while engineering effort should follow the stresses of real life. Disposable goods do not fit the concept, but neither does prestige construction whose cost exceeds the value customers receive.

Low price completes the identity. Kamprad’s intended customer is the broad public with limited money, so affordability cannot be an occasional promotion. Each product area should contain offers striking enough to establish trust in the whole range, and IKEA should maintain a clear value advantage over competitors. That demand reaches beyond designers and buyers to warehouse, office, and sales staff, because every operating choice eventually affects the price. Low price has meaning only when function and adequate quality survive the cost reduction.

The strength of this approach is its tight connection between mission, design, and operations. It forces creativity: a product succeeds not merely because it looks good, but because it performs at a price many people can pay. The tension is that a centrally defined identity can narrow cultural variety or treat affordability as more important than other customer priorities. Kamprad nevertheless treats service to the many as the non-negotiable test. Product range, quality, and cost are not separate policies in his account; together they explain what IKEA is meant to be.
## The ΙΚΕΑ spirit – a strong and living reality

Kamprad describes the IKEA spirit as a living work culture rather than a slogan. He remembers a smaller company in which people were close enough to speak directly, help wherever needed, improvise with scarce resources, and share setbacks as well as successes. Expansion makes that intimacy harder to sustain. Formal personnel systems, collective structures, and sheer numbers can turn individuals into records and make work feel anonymous. His concern is that growth may preserve the company’s name while thinning out the habits that made it distinctive.

The spirit he wants to preserve combines enthusiasm, renewal, cost-consciousness, humility, simplicity, responsibility, and practical mutual aid. It appears in ordinary acts rather than corporate ceremony: someone notices a problem outside a narrow job description, lends a hand, or completes an unglamorous duty without seeking attention. Kamprad calls such dependable people the pillars of the organisation. They exist in warehouses, offices, purchasing, and sales, and they understand the success of the whole as part of their own work.

Not everyone in a large organisation will feel this commitment. Some will reasonably regard employment chiefly as a livelihood, and Kamprad admits that leaders share responsibility when routine work loses warmth or meaning. He argues that a job occupies too much of life to be endured without interest. In his prescriptive view, managers must therefore do more than allocate tasks. They must develop people, model commitment, and help each person understand why a contribution matters.

Teamwork does not eliminate leadership. Kamprad compares a work group to a football team: consultation should inform a decision, but once the person responsible has decided, the group should act together rather than prolong the argument. That approach values participation while insisting on clarity and pace. Leaders must also recognise the quiet contributors who keep systems working without building personal territory around their responsibilities.

Culture, in this account, cannot simply be inherited. It must be cultivated and adapted as circumstances change. Kamprad warns that development is not automatically progress; leaders have to judge whether new systems strengthen the purpose or merely add distance. The useful side of his philosophy is its respect for initiative, solidarity, and visible example. A company can become more humane when people see colleagues rather than functions and are trusted to help beyond formal boundaries.

Example is the transmission mechanism. A written value acquires meaning when senior people answer directly, share inconvenient work, watch costs in their own choices, and thank those whose contribution is easily overlooked. Conversely, a leader who speaks about community while relying on hierarchy and privilege teaches the opposite lesson. Kamprad’s culture therefore asks authority to be unusually visible in daily conduct.

The tension lies in the intensity of the prescription. Expecting enthusiasm as a moral duty can undervalue fair limits, varied motivations, and legitimate disagreement. A demand to stop debate after a decision may create unity, but it can also silence information that leaders need. Kamprad’s model works best when mutual obligation truly runs both ways: co-workers bring energy and responsibility, while leaders provide respect, context, recognition, and room to speak. Under those conditions, the IKEA spirit is not nostalgia for a small firm. It is his proposed discipline for keeping personal commitment alive inside a much larger one.
## Profit gives us resources

Kamprad treats profit as a means rather than the final purpose. IKEA’s stated aim, a better everyday life for many people, requires capital to design products, purchase efficiently, open stores, and endure over time. In his philosophy, an independent company should generate those resources through committed work instead of expecting subsidies. Public support also comes from economic surplus or taxation, so it does not remove the underlying need to create resources.

The challenge is to earn profit without abandoning low prices. Charging too much would betray the intended customer and weaken IKEA’s value position. Charging too little would prevent reinvestment and make the mission unsustainable. Kamprad sees this tension as productive: it pushes designers toward economical construction, buyers toward better terms, and the whole organisation toward persistent cost reduction. Profit and affordability are thus meant to reinforce one another through efficiency.

This argument usefully rejects the idea that a social purpose can survive without financial durability. Reserves give a company freedom to invest and withstand setbacks. The risk is that profit can gradually become its own justification, while low cost can transfer pressure to workers, suppliers, or product quality. Kamprad’s claim is therefore credible only when the purpose continues to govern how profit is earned and used. In his prescription, the proper result is neither the highest possible margin nor price cutting at any cost, but enough surplus to keep serving customers well over the long term.
## Reaching good results with small means

Kamprad makes constrained resources a test of skill. A costly solution can often reach a target because money absorbs the difficulty. The more demanding achievement is to meet the real need with very little. His central example contrasts an expensive desk, which almost any designer could produce, with a functional desk affordable to an ordinary household. The second requires sharper judgment about materials, manufacturing, transport, and the features that genuinely matter.

For this reason, he insists that every proposed solution be evaluated together with its cost. A product considered without a price is incomplete, just as a supposedly free public service hides the bill ultimately paid by taxpayers. Cost does not by itself determine worth, but it reveals whether resources are proportionate to the benefit. In Kamprad’s philosophy, extravagant answers often signal that the hard design work has not been done.

Waste also hides in routine behaviour. People preserve documents no one will use, spend time proving they were right, defer decisions to another meeting to avoid responsibility, or choose a needlessly expensive channel for a simple message. Each act looks minor, yet together they consume money and, more importantly, attention. Resource discipline therefore applies to minutes, meetings, paperwork, and investments as much as to raw material.

The same lens changes how a proposal is discussed. Instead of admiring an idea first and discovering its price later, teams should place need, result, and total cost beside one another from the beginning. This makes hidden assumptions contestable and gives less glamorous alternatives a fair hearing. It also distributes cost awareness: economy is not a final correction performed by finance, but a design condition shared by everyone who spends time or money.

The constructive side of this principle is that scarcity can stimulate invention and keep the organisation focused on customer value. It challenges status spending and makes tradeoffs visible. The tension is that thrift can become false economy. An apparently cheap choice may create maintenance, safety, environmental, or human costs later. Kamprad’s prescription is strongest when “small means” refers to eliminating waste, not denying a task what it truly requires. Good results remain the standard; low expenditure is evidence of ingenuity only when function and long-term value are preserved.
## Simplicity is a virtue

Kamprad accepts that a large company needs rules, plans, and coordination. His warning is that each additional layer makes the whole harder to understand and obey. Fear of error, inherited procedures, and reluctance to decide produce more reports, committees, studies, and approvals. These devices may look responsible while actually delaying action. In his prescriptive philosophy, bureaucracy grows when people replace judgment with process and use collective machinery to avoid personal accountability.

Planning illustrates the distinction. Long-term direction and shared guidelines are necessary, but exhaustive planning can consume the time required to perform the work. It narrows freedom to respond when facts change and may give uncertain assumptions an appearance of precision. Kamprad therefore asks people to plan enough to coordinate action, then rely on common sense and direct observation. Simplicity is not the absence of thought. It is the discipline of making thought usable.

The later dictionary clarifies what he means. Simplicity is not nostalgia, untidy offices, primitive tools, or resistance to computers. Technology is simple when it improves efficiency and complicated when it adds effort without value. A short conversation may be better than an elaborate internal message, while a modest one-person investigation may answer a question as well as a large project group. Yet even a straightforward task must be explained: people need to understand why they are doing it if they are to do it well.

Simplicity also governs behaviour and status. Kamprad links plain routines, direct relationships, inexpensive travel, and avoidance of luxury symbols to credibility as a low-cost company. Managers should not ask others to economise while insulating themselves with expensive cars, hotels, titles, uniforms, or privileges. Humility toward customers, suppliers, and colleagues makes direct cooperation easier and signals that authority rests on contribution rather than display. Personal preferences outside work, however, should remain personal; corporate frugality does not justify policing how colleagues dress, travel, or enjoy their own money.

The appeal of this philosophy is practical. Shorter instructions are easier to follow, fewer handoffs reduce delay, and visible leadership can replace layers of control. Simplicity can release time for customers and products. Its danger is that “common sense” differs across roles and can conceal complexity that specialists manage for good reasons. Compliance, safety, accessibility, and fair employment may require procedures whose value is not immediately visible. A leader can also label objections complicated merely because they are inconvenient.

Kamprad’s principle is therefore most useful as a demand for intelligibility, not a blanket demand for fewer rules. A process should be no more elaborate than its purpose requires, and everyone affected should understand that purpose. Simplicity earns its place when it improves action, lowers real cost, and respects people. When it merely shifts hidden work or risk onto others, it has ceased to be the virtue he intends.
## Doing it a different way

Kamprad frames unconventional action as disciplined questioning, not novelty for display. IKEA itself is his first example. Experts might have doubted that the small town of Älmhult could support a major home-furnishings business, yet the company grew there by acting before convention granted permission. The recurring question is why a familiar practice exists and whether its purpose could be met more effectively another way.

This habit operates at every scale. Buyers sought table legs from a window factory and cushions from a shirt factory because useful production capabilities did not always sit inside the expected industry. IKEA designed its own furniture when retailers normally sold manufacturers’ designs, built large stores outside town centres, used door makers for tables, and transferred final assembly to customers. Each departure addressed cost, capacity, or access. Difference is valuable only when it improves the result.

Kamprad extends the principle to stores themselves. He hopes no two will be identical, because each new location should learn from the last and test improvements of its own. The newest store will contain mistakes, but experimentation can still make it the best so far. A standard concept provides boundaries while local variation keeps the organisation alert. In his philosophy, progress depends on preserving this tension between repeatable identity and restless adaptation.

The strength of asking why is that it exposes habits which survive after their rationale has disappeared. It can open overlooked suppliers, methods, and business models. The danger is romanticising disruption. An unconventional choice may be inferior, and repeatedly varying systems can raise training, safety, and maintenance costs. Kamprad explicitly distinguishes reasoned challenge from protest for its own sake. Existing solutions should be reused when they work, and experiments should remain controlled within the concept.

Thus “doing it a different way” is not permission for recklessness or eccentricity. It is his prescription to treat convention as a hypothesis rather than a command. The practical sequence is to understand the goal, question the inherited method, test a better alternative, and learn from the errors that action reveals. Difference earns approval through greater efficiency, lower cost, or better service, not through originality alone.
## Concentration – important to our success

Kamprad argues that limited resources gain strength through focus. A general who divides forces everywhere becomes weak everywhere, and even a gifted athlete cannot master every discipline at once. IKEA likewise cannot offer every style, promote every product, or enter every market simultaneously. In his philosophy, choosing what not to do is essential to creating noticeable results with modest means.

The product range should concentrate on IKEA’s own profile rather than attempt to satisfy every taste. Promotion should feature selected offers instead of giving equal weight to the entire catalogue. International expansion should build one market deliberately rather than scatter capital and attention. During a market launch, marketing may temporarily receive priority while other desirable work waits. Kamprad calls the companion skill “lista,” the Småland practice of making do with the absolute minimum until resources can be redirected.

Focus brings speed and coherence, but the chapter acknowledges a sharp risk. Neglected areas do not become unimportant merely because another priority is urgent. Kamprad even notes that security systems may receive less attention during a concentrated push, leaving honesty and loyalty to carry more weight. That may describe entrepreneurial reality, but it is not a sufficient safeguard where failures could harm people or the business.

The durable lesson is to concentrate discretionary effort while maintaining essential controls. Clear priorities prevent a small organisation from dissolving into dozens of weak initiatives, and temporary improvisation can bridge genuine gaps. Yet leaders must name what is being deferred, understand the exposure, and restore attention in time. Concentration is powerful in Kamprad’s prescription because it channels resources toward a decisive result; it becomes dangerous when urgency is allowed to erase basic obligations.
## Taking responsibility – a privilege

Kamprad sees progress as the work of people who would rather make a decision than hide behind someone else’s. Such people appear at every level: in warehouses, buying, sales, and offices, regardless of education, rank, or wealth. Their defining quality is willingness to act for the whole. In his prescriptive philosophy, an organisation with too few responsibility-takers compensates with bureaucracy, meetings, and group discussions that distribute authority so widely that no one owns the outcome.

Freedom and duty therefore belong together. Individuals should receive room to use initiative, while accepting responsibility for consequences and for the interests of colleagues. An expanding company cannot specify every boundary in advance, especially when new roles are continually emerging. People must be willing both to accept work that falls between descriptions and to delegate meaningful authority to others. Kamprad favours promoting promising people before they possess conventional proof of experience, because responsibility itself is how capacity develops.

Decision-making inevitably produces mistakes. His memorable formulation is that “Only while sleeping one makes no mistakes.” The point is not that every error deserves approval, but that action under uncertainty cannot be made perfectly safe. Waiting for complete certainty loses opportunities and feeds the analyses, committees, and delays that he associates with bureaucracy. Active people should be allowed to make correctable mistakes, acknowledge them, and put them right without spending their energy defending the past.

The later dictionary adds necessary limits. Permission to err is not permission to deliver careless work, rush without information, or abandon a half-finished project. A person should gather enough evidence for a responsible choice, act before certainty becomes impossible, and then follow the result closely. When an experiment fails, explanation matters only insofar as it supports correction and learning. Responsibility means repairing damage, adjusting the method, and carrying the task through.

Kamprad also rejects the assumption that one decision is uniquely right in advance. Several paths may work, and committed execution can determine which becomes successful. This belief favours positive, forward-looking colleagues over those who mainly document why they were not at fault. As an example, he says that when another business copies an IKEA model, litigation is less useful than designing a better successor. Energy goes toward solving the competitive problem rather than proving grievance.

For him, the finest outcome does not require another person to lose; responsibility should create value rather than merely establish who prevailed.

There is real force in this approach. Local authority can shorten feedback loops, develop younger colleagues, and make work more meaningful. Treating mistakes as information reduces defensive behaviour, while explicit ownership prevents consultation from becoming paralysis. The aspiration to create victories without losers also directs initiative toward improvement rather than punishment.

The tension is that positivity can itself become a way to suppress criticism. A colleague who identifies legal, safety, or ethical risk is not necessarily mediocre or afraid. Some decisions need broad consultation because their consequences fall on people who do not hold the authority. Likewise, repairing an error does not erase every harm, and unequal power may determine who is allowed to fail without penalty. Kamprad’s principle works best when leaders distinguish obstruction from informed dissent and apply the privilege of mistakes fairly.

Taking responsibility, then, is both a right and an obligation in his account. The organisation must trust people with real choices, and people must use that trust to decide, follow up, correct, and learn. Initiative without accountability becomes recklessness; accountability without freedom becomes bureaucracy. Kamprad’s desired culture depends on holding the two together.
## Most things still remain to be done. A glorious future!

Kamprad warns that the feeling of completion can sedate both people and organisations. Retirement becomes decline when someone believes all useful contribution is behind them, and a company stagnates when it treats current success as the destination. In his philosophy, satisfaction may be enjoyed briefly, but vitality comes from remaining on the way. Happiness lies less in arrival than in the continuing movement toward a worthwhile goal.

That outlook turns improvement into a daily question: how can today’s work be done better tomorrow? Discovery should remain pleasurable, and apparent impossibility should invite investigation rather than immediate surrender. Kamprad is deliberately suspicious of experience because it can harden into an argument against experiments that have never been tried. Much of the present once looked impossible to people whose evidence came only from the past.

Yet he does not discard experience altogether. Personal successes and failures shape useful judgment, and previous failure does not necessarily disprove an idea. The later dictionary sharpens the balance: perseverance means continuing work that still promises a result, not carrying every project forever. Before beginning, people should ask whether they can sustain the effort. A one-time purchasing trip, unused language training, or machinery unsupported by local technicians consumes resources without building capability. Periodic review should separate difficult progress from effort that has lost its purpose.

Humility is the companion to ambition. Kamprad defines it as respect, consideration, kindness, generosity, and willingness to listen and admit weakness. Success can threaten those qualities by making a company intoxicated with its own achievement. Willpower without humility turns into conflict, while humility without will can become passivity. Together they allow people to pursue demanding goals, hear criticism, apologise for errors, and improve without surrendering resolve.

Time gives the philosophy urgency. Kamprad asks readers to notice how much can happen in ten minutes and how completely that interval disappears once spent. Time is not merely payroll cost; it is a piece of a person’s life. Meetings, reports, indecision, and meaningless activity therefore carry a human cost that cannot be recovered. Dividing life mentally into small units is his way of making that loss visible and encouraging deliberate use of attention.

The optimistic side of this chapter is its refusal of complacency. It keeps curiosity alive after success and joins personal development to organisational renewal. Its danger is endless striving. If completion is always suspect, rest may look like weakness and achievements may never feel sufficient. Deleting “impossible” from the vocabulary can inspire invention, but physical, ethical, and economic limits remain real. A healthy version of Kamprad’s prescription needs room for recovery, evidence, and the decision to stop.

His glorious future is therefore not a forecast but a chosen posture. The work remains unfinished because needs change, methods improve, and human beings can continue learning. Positive determination matters most when disciplined by humility, honest review, and respect for finite time. Kamprad closes by inviting colleagues to pursue the possible together, stubbornly but not blindly, convinced that the next improvement is still available to be found.
## A Little ΙΚΕΑ Dictionary

Kamprad’s dictionary explains how familiar words acquired a particular meaning inside IKEA. Shared language can coordinate a dispersed company, but he recognises that it can also confuse newcomers, mislead outsiders, or accumulate meanings never intended. The entries are therefore less objective definitions than an attempt to keep a founder’s philosophy intelligible as the organisation changes.

One cluster describes character. Humbleness means respect for competitors, customers, suppliers, colleagues, and the difficulty of the task. It requires listening, sharing information, accepting criticism, admitting mistakes, and apologising, while still speaking plainly when something matters. A manager who tolerates bad work without discussing it is not humble. Willpower supplies the other half: colleagues agree on objectives, decide, and persist without confusing determination with getting their own way. Togetherness and enthusiasm turn these traits into community. Kamprad believes appreciation, interesting work, belonging, and readiness to help can motivate more deeply than material rewards alone, though that belief should not be used to discount fair pay or personal boundaries.

A second cluster connects simplicity, common sense, and cost-consciousness. Simplicity means efficient explanations, short instructions, appropriate technology, direct conversation, and methods people can understand. It does not mean shabby work or rejection of computers. Common sense tests both old convention and fashionable novelty against practical ratios: customer numbers, storage needs, space, and full cost. Cost-consciousness then links those judgments to the low-price mission through manufacturable products, careful purchasing, modest stores and travel, attention to small expenses, and disciplined use of time. Cheapness is not the sole test; a badly prepared meeting for hundreds of people wastes more than it saves.

These ideas support service to the many people. Kamprad asks IKEA to represent households with thin wallets even when short-term profit points elsewhere. That can mean removing an attractive design that costs too much to produce or refusing a lucrative arrangement that excludes ordinary local customers. Understanding people requires contact with everyday life, not detached tourism. Public transport and direct observation are offered as ways to learn how homes, money, and routines actually work.

Making do, derived from the Småland idea of “lista,” is the practical craft of stretching resources. A warehouse might lower a shelf so an existing hand-operated pallet truck remains useful, or use a padlock until a permanent repair is available. Such temporary solutions address today’s problem without unnecessary investment. They are valuable when ingenuity preserves the result, but dangerous if temporary fixes quietly become permanent substitutes for safety or quality.

Experience and doing things differently govern learning. Experience informs judgment, yet becomes harmful when invoked to prove that an untried idea is impossible. IKEA’s own departures included designing furniture, locating large stores outside town, sourcing tables from door manufacturers, and letting customers assemble purchases. Difference must improve efficiency or cost rather than serve eccentricity. Controlled tests are encouraged because fear of mistakes creates missed opportunities and bureaucracy. The privilege of error carries a duty to gather reasonable information, finish work properly, and correct what goes wrong.

“Never say never” adds perseverance, the Småland quality Kamprad calls “bli vé.” Teams should begin projects they can sustain and continue through slow progress, while reviewing objectives closely enough to stop efforts that promise no return. This is determination with an exit test, not blind endurance. Accepting and delegating responsibility follows naturally. Growing organisations cannot map every role, so leaders should trust people with potential, including younger colleagues without long records, and those people should step into gaps and own consequences.

Status and bureaucracy describe threats to that agency. Status should come from example and contribution, not executive dining rooms, first-class flights, luxurious company cars, unused equipment, grand furniture, or fancy titles that contradict a low-cost identity. Bureaucracy appears in unread reports, oversized meetings, too many direct reports, distant decisions, and rules created from fear. Kamprad favours small empowered teams and local authority, while the critical limit is that necessary safeguards must not be dismissed merely because they require documentation.

Honesty makes decentralisation possible. Product communication must include shortcomings as well as benefits, and internal speech should be open without becoming cruel. Constructive criticism begins with what worked, then addresses what needs improvement. Facing up to reality similarly pulls managers away from desks and theories toward stores, customers, operational detail, and follow-up. Decisions gain credibility when leaders observe their effects and recognise those who implement them.

Finally, Kamprad warns that “the IKEA Way” can become an empty veto. The phrase has content only when a person explains which value is at stake: waste, cost, humility, service to the many, complacency, or an idea lacking a price. Used thoughtfully, this vocabulary connects mission to daily choices. Used dogmatically, it can enforce conformity and protect authority from challenge. The dictionary’s lasting value is therefore not its special terminology alone, but its demand that cultural claims be translated into reasons, observable conduct, and consequences for the people IKEA intends to serve.

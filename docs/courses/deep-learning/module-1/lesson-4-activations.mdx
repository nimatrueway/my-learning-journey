## The hook

Here's a trap that has humbled many an engineer: stack 100 layers with **no activation functions**, and your giant network is mathematically identical to… one single layer. A straight line in a trench coat.

Why? And what exactly do ReLU and friends fix?

## Why the kink matters

Composition of linear functions is linear: `f(g(x))` where both are lines… is a line. All the depth collapses.

One nonlinear squiggle between layers breaks the collapse. Now each layer genuinely *adds* expressive power, and depth means something.

**Practical defaults (memorize this, it's 95% of real life):**

- Hidden layers → **ReLU** (or its cousins GELU/SiLU in transformers)
- Output for "probability of yes" → **sigmoid**
- Output for "pick one of N classes" → softmax (Module 3)
- Output for "predict a number" → no activation

<details>
<summary>🤓 The actual math (optional — skip freely)</summary>

The collapse proof is two lines. Two linear layers:

$$W_2(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = (W_2 W_1)x + (W_2 b_1 + b_2)$$

That's just $W' x + b'$ — one linear layer. By induction, any depth collapses.

The saturation problem: sigmoid's derivative is $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, which maxes out at 0.25 and is nearly **0** for large $|z|$. During backprop, gradients multiply through layers — multiply enough near-zeros and the early layers learn nothing ("vanishing gradients"). ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 for $z>0$, which is why it took over the world.

</details>

## Recap 📌

- No activation ⇒ any depth collapses into a single linear layer
- The **nonlinearity** (kink/curve) is what makes depth actually useful
- Default: ReLU in hidden layers; pick outputs by what you're predicting

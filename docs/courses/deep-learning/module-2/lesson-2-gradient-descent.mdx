# 2.2 — Gradient descent in 10,000 dimensions ⏱️ ~12 min

<Meme quote="When something is important enough, you do it even if the odds are not in your favor." author="Elon Musk — the ball, probably, rolling into dimension 10,000" />

## The hook

Lesson 1.2's ball rolled down a 1-D curve — one knob. Real networks have millions of knobs. What does "downhill" even *mean* in a million dimensions?

Answer: exactly the same thing. And there's a classic failure you can only see with 2+ knobs — let's trigger it.

## The ravine 🎮

Two knobs now: w₁ (shallow direction) and w₂ (steep direction). The ellipses are the loss landscape seen from above — a stretched bowl. Green dot = the minimum.

<Descent2D />

**Things to try:**

1. **lr = 0.3**, train → watch it **zigzag**: it bounces across the steep direction while slowly creeping along the shallow one. This is the classic ravine problem.
2. **lr = 0.5** → the zigzag amplifies and 💥. The steep direction couldn't survive steps that the shallow direction *needed*.
3. **lr = 0.05** → stable but takes forever crawling along the valley floor.

One learning rate has to serve every direction at once — and directions disagree. This exact tension is why fancier optimizers exist (**Adam**, next lessons, adapts the step size per knob).

<Meme top="steep direction: slow down!!" visual="↕️😵‍💫↔️" bottom="shallow direction: SPEED UP" />

## Scaling to a million knobs

Nothing changes. The gradient is just a list of slopes — one per knob. Each knob steps downhill along *its own* slope, all simultaneously. A million-knob network takes a million-component step. The picture in your head can stay 2-D; the math doesn't care.

<details>
<summary>🤓 The actual math (optional — skip freely)</summary>

The landscape here is $L(w_1, w_2) = 0.15 w_1^2 + 2 w_2^2$. Its gradient — the vector of slopes:

$$\nabla L = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}\right] = [0.3\,w_1,\; 4\,w_2]$$

The update is the same rule from lesson 1.2, applied to the whole vector: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla L$.

Why the zigzag: the steep direction's slope (coefficient 4) is ~13× the shallow one's (0.3). A step size that's calm for $w_1$ *overshoots* $w_2$, which flips its sign each step → bounce, bounce, bounce. Stability requires roughly $\eta < 2/4 = 0.5$ for the steep direction — try lr just under and over 0.5 and watch the theory come true.

</details>

## Recap 📌

- Gradient = a slope per knob; all knobs step downhill simultaneously
- Ravines cause **zigzagging**: one learning rate can't please steep and shallow directions
- This is why adaptive optimizers (Adam) exist — per-knob step sizes

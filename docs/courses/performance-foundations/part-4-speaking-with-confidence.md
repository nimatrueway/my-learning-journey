# Part IV: Speaking With Confidence at Work

Your work is solid. Then a meeting starts, attention turns inward, and suddenly
you are live-commentating your own heartbeat like it is a production incident.

<Meme quote="If you have an important point to make, don't try to be subtle or clever. Use a pile driver." author="Winston Churchill" />

The gap between the objective quality of your work and an internal sense of low
worth can be widened by cognitive distortions and self-focused attention. When
you expect to appear weak or awkward, you may monitor your heart rate, voice,
hesitations, and imagined appearance. That monitoring competes for attention
needed to listen, think, and speak naturally, which can produce the hesitation
you feared.

The way out is not to manufacture perfect confidence before speaking. It is to
redirect attention, change your relationship to anxious thoughts, collect
better evidence, and practice direct participation without protective rituals.

## Play: Rehearse the First Five Minutes

<MeetingRehearsal />

Try every contribution type. Then remove the defensive qualifier. Notice that
the useful content survives perfectly well without an apology in front of it.

## 1. Shift Attention Externally

When entering a meeting, deliberately give attention to the room rather than to
an internal performance review:

- Take detailed notes on what people actually say.
- Track the decision, question, or constraint under discussion.
- Watch the speaker's cues for meaning, not for evidence that you are failing.
- When self-monitoring returns, redirect attention without arguing with it.

The aim is not to suppress anxiety. It is to stop assigning anxiety the job of
running the meeting.

<details>
<summary>🧠 Why external attention can help (optional)</summary>

The **Clark and Wells cognitive model of social anxiety** proposes that
threatening social situations can trigger self-focused attention and a distorted
internal image of how one appears to others. Attention spent monitoring that
image is attention unavailable for the conversation. Experiments on attention
training and reduced self-focus support external redirection as a useful
strategy, although it does not guarantee immediate reductions in physiological
arousal.

</details>

## 2. Create Distance From the Thought

When a thought appears such as “nobody cares about my work” or “I am going to
say something dumb,” add one phrase:

> “I am noticing the thought that my work does not matter.”

That sentence does not prove the thought false. It changes the thought from a
verdict into an event you can observe while choosing what to do next.

<Meme top="brain submitted: everyone will think this is dumb" visual="🧠📨" bottom="request received. not automatically approved." />

<details>
<summary>🧠 Cognitive defusion (optional)</summary>

In **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy**, cognitive defusion practices reduce
the literal grip of thoughts by changing how a person relates to them. Adding
“I am noticing the thought…” is a language-based way to create that distance.
Research supports defusion as one process that can reduce believability and
distress, but precise neural mechanisms and broad claims about specific brain
networks remain active research questions.

</details>

## 3. Keep a Fact vs. Impression Log

Low self-worth can make impressions feel like measurements. Build a two-column
log that forces them apart:

| Impression | Objective facts |
| --- | --- |
| “This report is useless and nobody cares.” | Leadership requested it, it was used in planning, and no factual errors were found. |
| “I contributed nothing in the meeting.” | I clarified one constraint and my question changed the next action. |

Write the entry shortly after the work or meeting, while concrete evidence is
available. Facts include observable actions, requests, decisions, feedback, and
outcomes. “Everyone secretly hated it” remains an impression, even when typed
with confidence.

<details>
<summary>🧠 Why written evidence can correct the record (optional)</summary>

**Cognitive restructuring** separates automatic interpretations from evidence
for and against them. A written log also counters selective attention and
confirmation bias by making disconfirming evidence harder to discard. The useful
mechanism is disciplined comparison, not forcing the brain into positivity or
claiming that one region simply overrides another.

</details>

## 4. Speak Within the First Five Minutes

Commit to one small contribution before anticipatory anxiety has the entire
meeting to write fan fiction about your downfall:

- Validate a point and add one reason.
- Ask a basic clarifying question.
- Give a concise status update.
- Name one relevant constraint or piece of evidence.

The contribution does not need to be impressive. It needs to teach your nervous
system that participation can occur before perfect readiness.

<details>
<summary>🧠 Micro-exposure and new learning (optional)</summary>

Exposure-based approaches ask a person to enter a feared situation while
reducing avoidance. **Inhibitory learning theory** emphasizes learning a new
association—“I can speak while anxious and the predicted catastrophe may not
occur”—rather than waiting for fear to disappear. Speaking early can reduce the
time available for anticipatory rumination, but anxiety does not follow a fixed
schedule and safety learning is built through repetition.

</details>

## 5. Drop Defensive Safety Behaviors

Identify behaviors intended to prevent judgment:

- “This might be a dumb question, but…”
- Over-explaining before reaching the point.
- Speaking too quickly to escape attention.
- Apologizing when no repair is needed.
- Filling every pause because silence feels dangerous.

Practice one concise sentence, then pause. If the interaction goes well, your
brain gets a chance to attribute the result to your actual ability to
participate—not to the apology you placed in front of it.

<details>
<summary>🧠 Why safety behaviors can maintain fear (optional)</summary>

Models of social anxiety propose that safety behaviors prevent clean corrective
learning. If a person believes “I survived only because I apologized first,” a
successful interaction does not update the underlying threat belief. Some
safety behaviors also directly impair communication by increasing self-focus,
speed, or ambiguity. Reduce them gradually when that makes practice more
manageable.

</details>

## Try It Yourself: One Meeting Experiment

For your next meeting, write this plan before joining:

1. **External target:** the decision, speaker's words, or available evidence.
2. **Early contribution:** one question, validation, update, or constraint.
3. **Phrase to remove:** one apology or qualifier you habitually use.
4. **Defusion line:** “I am noticing the thought that…”
5. **Evidence afterward:** one impression and three observable facts.

Run this as an experiment for three meetings. Measure whether you contributed,
what happened next, and which prediction was or was not supported. Do not grade
the experiment by whether anxiety vanished.

:::note When to get more support
Persistent social anxiety, panic, or low self-worth can deserve support beyond
self-guided practice. A qualified mental-health professional can tailor CBT,
ACT, or exposure-based work to your circumstances. Seek urgent local support if
distress includes thoughts of harming yourself.
:::

## Three Things to Keep

- Move attention from your internal threat monitor to the actual conversation.
- Treat anxious thoughts as mental events, then compare impressions with facts.
- Speak early and directly enough to learn that safety behaviors are optional.

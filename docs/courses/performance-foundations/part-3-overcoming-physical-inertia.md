# Part III: Overcoming Physical Inertia

The window frame needs fixing. You are on the sofa. Between those two facts lies
an emotional distance roughly equal to the Pacific Ocean.

<Meme quote="Ideas are easy. Execution is everything." author="John Doerr" />

Physical inertia occurs because transitioning instantly from a low-arousal, sedentary state to a high-effort physical project requires a sudden spike in nervous system arousal and kinetic energy. When the brain calculates the gap between your current rest state and the effort needed to repair a window frame, it registers that friction as intense mental resistance.

## Four Ways to Cross the Gap

To bypass the barrier, apply these psychological strategies:

- **Lower the activation threshold to a micro-step:** The brain resists complex, multi-step physical projects, but it easily permits trivial movements. Decouple the start of the process from the task itself. Instead of aiming to repair the frame, commit only to an absurdly small micro-action, such as **putting on work shoes** or **picking up a single screwdriver**. Once the body initiates movement on a non-threatening step, kinetic momentum takes over naturally.
- **Use a physical bridge to shift your nervous system:** Trying to think yourself into feeling energetic while remaining seated rarely works because physiology leads psychology. **Change your physical state before attempting the task** through a low-friction bridge—stand up, splash cold water on your face, or stretch for 60 seconds. Elevating your heart rate slightly before approaching the project signals to your nervous system that it is time to act.
- **Decouple physical movement from readiness:** Waiting to "feel ready" or "feel energetic" is a psychological trap. Physical energy follows action, not the other way around. Accept that the body will feel heavy and resistant for the first few minutes, and recognize that **you can move your limbs while feeling sluggish**. The feeling of motivation usually arrives five minutes after physical engagement begins.
- **Separate preparation from execution:** Contemplating both the setup (finding tools, clearing the area) and the execution (doing the repair) creates cognitive overload when baseline energy is low. **Set up the workspace without committing to the repair itself.** Gather the tools and place them by the window, then step away if needed. Removing the environment setup friction makes jumping into the task much easier later.

<summary>🧠 The behavior model (optional)</summary>

These strategies reduce the size of the perceived action, alter immediate
physiological state, and remove setup decisions from the execution moment. The
useful mechanism is not magical kinetic momentum; it is a sequence of easier
actions that lowers initiation cost and creates new cues for the next action.
Treat claims such as “motivation arrives after five minutes” as a practical
heuristic rather than a biological guarantee.

## Try It Yourself: Stage Tomorrow's Start

Choose one physical task you have delayed. Do only this tonight:

1. Put the first tool or item in plain sight.
2. Write the first physical movement on a note: “Put on shoes,” “open the box,”
	 or “carry the ladder to the room.”
3. Choose a five-minute start time.
4. At that time, perform the movement before evaluating your energy.

Stopping after setup is allowed. The next start is now cheaper.

## Three Things to Keep

- Shrink the start until it is a movement, not a project.
- Change physical state before negotiating with readiness.
- Separate setup from execution when low energy makes both feel enormous.

# Shockingly Good Predictions

Postmortems love disasters. A forecast that was too pessimistic is easier to
ignore: everybody is happy, the graph is up, meeting over.

That wastes unusually valuable evidence. A positive surprise still proves your
model of the world was wrong.

⏱️ **~6 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 21-minute source video is optional

## Study the Error, Not the Victory Lap

The hosts revisit beliefs that reality exceeded: important companies emerging
outside Silicon Valley, nuclear power returning to strategic relevance,
missions attracting exceptional talent, and customers favoring growth over
hard-to-measure savings.

Their useful move is not “predict boldly.” It is decomposition. For any miss,
ask which hidden variable was underweighted. Was it market structure, technical
change, human motivation, or the buyer's actual incentive? Updating that
variable improves the next decision more than celebrating the outcome.

**Source-reading note:** the captions identify turns but not every speaker by
name, so ambiguous recollections are assigned to “one host.” The episode is a
retrospective conversation, vulnerable to hindsight and selection effects; its
claims are heuristics and cases, not calibrated forecasting evidence.

## Source Case: A Strange Nuclear Detour

One host recalls Sam Altman saying around 2013 that he planned to work on a
nuclear startup. The host initially wondered why. Altman's thesis was that
electricity underlies everything, policy had constrained nuclear development,
and abundant power would be foundational. Altman soon returned to YC and funded
nuclear companies, but the hosts now see the original direction as unusually
well aimed, especially as demand for computation and intelligence grew.

The lesson is not that every contrarian energy thesis wins. It is that a forecast
can look strange because it operates at a longer causal horizon. If abundant
intelligence requires enormous computation, and computation requires power,
energy becomes upstream of the visible software boom. The better question is
not “Is this fashionable now?” but “If the downstream future arrives, what
constraint becomes decisive?”

## Try It Yourself: Run a Positive-Surprise Review

Pick one result from the last year that beat your forecast. Spend two minutes on
four sentences:

1. **I expected** ___ **because I assumed** ___.
2. **Reality produced** ___.
3. **The variable I underweighted was** ___.
4. **A decision I will change in the next 30 days is** ___.

Keep the update narrow. “Anything is possible” is not learning; it is a shrug
wearing a lab coat.

## Buyers Reward Their Own Upside

The hosts describe a recurring software pitch: save a company five engineers,
claim a million dollars of value, and charge a fraction of it. The arithmetic
can be internally consistent while failing to match buying behavior. On a
public-company board, one host came to see how strongly markets and executives
prioritize revenue growth. Cost savings, especially hypothetical headcount
savings, may never produce layoffs or rank among the buyer's top priorities.

Growth products are not automatically superior. A saving can win when it is
large, immediate, measurable, and removes operational complexity. The episode
uses AWS as a qualification: customers could pay more per unit while avoiding
data-center capital costs and maintenance. Follow the buyer's cash flow and
constraints, not a spreadsheet value they may never realize.

<details>
<summary>🔭 Guardrails for learning from a good miss (optional)</summary>

Check three distortions. **Hindsight:** did the causal chain truly seem plausible
before the result? **Selection:** are you remembering one spectacular winner and
forgetting similar failures? **Transfer:** does the updated variable apply to
your next decision, or only explain this case? Preserve the surprise without
turning an anecdote into a universal rule.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Positive surprises deserve postmortems because they expose errors in your model.
- Trace a forecast through upstream constraints instead of judging it by current fashion.
- Customer value depends on buyer incentives and realized outcomes, not tidy savings arithmetic.

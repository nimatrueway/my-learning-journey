# How Startups Close Million Dollar Deals

A founder can make a product easy to try and still make it nearly impossible to
buy. The demo works. The champion is excited. Then procurement asks how the
price maps to value, who owns implementation, and what success means. The deal
does not explode; it slowly evaporates in a calendar full of “next steps.”

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 18-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel argue that large software contracts become
less mysterious when you stop treating them as oversized self-service sales.
The buyer is comparing a small set of familiar alternatives, managing risk, and
buying a business result. Your job is to fit that decision without becoming
interchangeable.

## Learn the Buyer's Grammar

People rarely evaluate an expensive purchase in isolation. They compare a few
vendors that use recognizable units, contract lengths, approval paths, and
service promises. A startup that invents a clever but alien pricing unit may
force the customer to translate the proposal before they can defend it
internally.

Start with four questions:

1. What alternatives will appear beside us in the buying memo?
2. How do those alternatives package and price the work?
3. Which budget already pays for this category of result?
4. Who must believe the risk is acceptable before a signature happens?

This is not an instruction to copy a competitor's product. It is a heuristic:
use a purchasing shape the customer can recognize, then differentiate on the
result you can deliver. Familiar packaging lowers translation cost; it does not
create demand by itself.

## Sell the Result, Not the Plumbing

Technical founders naturally describe mechanisms: models, APIs, latency, and
architecture. An executive buyer is usually accountable for revenue, cost,
risk, or speed. The mechanism matters only after the buyer can connect it to
one of those outcomes.

The reasoning is economic. If your tool saves an engineer an uncertain amount
of time, the buyer has little basis for a seven-figure price. If your team can
credibly help recover `\$10 million` in margin, a `\$1 million` contract can be
rational even when the underlying software looks small. Implementation support
may therefore be part of the product rather than an embarrassing service
attached to it.

Ask the customer, “How will you decide this worked?” Then make the answer
observable. A useful outcome statement has a baseline, a target, a deadline,
and an owner. “Improve logistics” is fog. “Reduce late shipments from 8% to 5%
within two quarters, measured by the operations team” can guide a contract.

## Source Case: Palantir's Claimed Deal Economics

The hosts use stories from former Palantir engineers to illustrate outcome
selling. In their telling, Palantir could place a small technical team inside a
customer, solve a problem worth many times the contract, and price against that
created value rather than the number of engineers or software seats involved.

The constraint was not merely shipping code. The vendor had to understand the
customer's operation, assemble a working solution, and remain accountable for
the result. That explains why “here is a tool; good luck” supports a different
price from “we will help produce the outcome.”

This is a speaker-reported, deliberately simplified example; the captions do
not provide contracts or audited economics. Treat the numbers as an intuition
about value-based pricing, not evidence that every embedded-services model has
Palantir's margins or that every buyer will accept outcome pricing.

## Try It Yourself: Draft a One-Page Deal Thesis

Choose one real target customer. In two minutes, write five lines: the costly
problem, its current baseline, a measurable target, the likely budget owner,
and the two alternatives the buyer will compare. Finish with one sentence
explaining why your proposed pricing unit will feel familiar to procurement.

Do not design a full proposal. Circle the weakest line and turn it into one
question for your next customer call. The output is a learning agenda, not a
fictional contract.

## Practice Upmarket Before You Feel Ready

The hosts describe seven-figure selling as an iterative capability. A founder
tries, hears no, learns which proof or process was missing, changes the product
or sale, and tries again. That loop can take a year or two in their experience.
Meanwhile, slow enterprise cycles leave room to run several conversations in
parallel instead of emotionally depending on one giant logo.

The caveat is important: moving upmarket is not automatically correct. A
self-service product with strong retention and efficient growth may not need
custom outcomes or a sales team. The heuristic applies when large customers
hold material value and comparable vendors already sell through assisted,
high-trust processes.

<details>
<summary>🧾 A large-deal readiness check (optional)</summary>

Before forecasting a contract, look for four kinds of proof:

- **Value:** The customer agrees the target outcome is economically meaningful.
- **Delivery:** Your team can implement without promising undefined heroics.
- **Evidence:** A pilot, reference, or operational baseline reduces belief risk.
- **Process:** You know the champion, economic buyer, procurement path, and next decision.

A missing item does not always kill the deal. It tells you which uncertainty the
next meeting should reduce.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Make packaging recognizable to the buyer before asking them to understand what is novel about the product.
- Connect technical capability to a measurable business outcome with an owner and deadline.
- Treat large-contract selling as repeated discovery, not a one-meeting act of confidence.

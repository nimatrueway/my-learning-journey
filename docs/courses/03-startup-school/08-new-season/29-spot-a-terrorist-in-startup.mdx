# How to Spot a Terrorist in Your Startup

One prestigious customer can become the whole roadmap. One brilliant recruit
can make every exception feel temporary. One anxious investor can turn the CEO
into a full-time feelings manager. The visible relationship looks valuable;
the company underneath it stops moving.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 19-minute source video is optional

The source uses “terrorist” as deliberately aggressive, colorful office-hours
language for an actor or request that hijacks a startup while creating little
or no reciprocal value. It is a metaphor for destructive organizational
behavior, not a claim about anyone's identity, nationality, religion, race, or
other protected characteristic. This lesson uses behavior-based terms after
explaining the source framing.

## Diagnose the Exchange, Not the Person

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel distinguish destructive relationships from
ordinary difficulty. A demanding colleague can still create enormous value. A
good customer can make one bad request. A well-intentioned investor may not
realize how much founder time they consume. The diagnostic question is not
“Are they unpleasant?” It is “Does the exchange repeatedly take control,
attention, or money away from the company's mission without returning value?”

The pattern often begins with dependency. A young startup receives interest
from a famous company while everything else is struggling. The logo and revenue
feel like rescue. Because losing the account seems existential, the customer
can dictate custom work, support, and roadmap. The hosts add an important
correction: founders sometimes create this leverage themselves because they
are desperate for anyone to answer their emails.

The same structure can appear internally. Prestige causes a founder to excuse
a recruit's escalating conditions. Hope focuses on potential while the actual
cost-benefit remains negative. With investors, endless questions can consume a
large share of a founder's week even though more detailed answers cannot fix
the underlying concern about company performance.

## Source Case: Revenue With the Wrong Shape

Michael recalls advising a startup that kept serving an almost unhelpable
customer. The required work would apply to very few other users and would never
make economic sense. He describes the founder as emotionally attached and
serving the customer from pity rather than advancing the company's mission. His
advice was to fire the customer.

The case makes the hosts' intermediate logic concrete. Revenue is not one
homogeneous variable. If an account requires loss-making bespoke work, diverts
the product from repeatable demand, and consumes the capacity needed to find
healthier customers, each dollar can reduce the odds of building the intended
business. That is why they say revenue quality matters.

But the label can hide responsibility. Later, they describe a perfectly
reasonable customer asking for a feature that will not improve the customer's
own results. The founder's job is to understand the business well enough to say
no. A harmful request does not make a person inherently harmful.

## Try It Yourself: Run a Reciprocity Audit

Choose one customer, recruit, employee, or investor relationship that feels
expensive. Use facts from the last 30 days and fill four rows:

| Test | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Value returned | Revenue, learning, shipped work, introductions, or decisions |
| Control surrendered | Roadmap, schedule, hiring rules, or founder attention |
| Repeatability | Whether the work benefits the next five customers or teammates |
| Forward cost | Hours and cash required during the next 30 days |

Then choose exactly one action: keep the relationship unchanged, set one
boundary with a date, redesign the exchange, or begin an orderly exit. Write the
sentence you will use. The exercise is bounded to one relationship and one
next action; it is not a license for a company-wide purge.

## Boundaries Need Due Process

This heuristic can be abused by leaders who dislike dissent, disability
accommodations, caregiving constraints, cultural differences, or people who
surface uncomfortable facts. The captions explicitly say they are not talking
about merely difficult people or ambiguous cases where someone creates real
value. Judge documented behavior and impact, not personality or identity.

For employees, use clear expectations, feedback, support, and lawful HR
processes. For customers, inspect contracts and transition costs. For investors,
agree on an information cadence rather than withholding material information.
Urgency does not remove obligations, and a metaphor should never replace
evidence.

<details>
<summary>🧾 Separate a hard relationship from a destructive one (optional)</summary>

A **hard but productive** relationship may involve conflict, strong opinions,
or unusual needs while its measured contribution remains positive. A
**destructive pattern**, in the speakers' framing, repeatedly expands demands,
captures decisions, and produces little value even after boundaries or support.

Before acting, ask whether expectations were explicit, whether the other party
had a fair chance to respond, whether accommodations or power differences are
relevant, and whether your own desperation created the dependency. If those
questions are unanswered, the diagnosis is premature.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- The source's loaded term is a metaphor for destructive behavior that hijacks a startup, never a label for a demographic group or identity.
- Measure reciprocal value, control surrendered, repeatability, and forward cost instead of equating difficulty with harm.
- Founders can create unhealthy leverage themselves, and boundaries still require evidence, fairness, contracts, and lawful process.

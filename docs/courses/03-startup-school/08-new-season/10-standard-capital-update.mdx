# Standard Capital Update

A founder can spend weeks editing a deck, collecting introductions, repeating
the same meeting, and waiting for a decision. The hidden cost is not only stress.
It is time taken from customers and product.

⏱️ **~6 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 14-minute source video is optional

Michael Seibel interviews Dalton Caldwell after Standard Capital's first
application cycle. Dalton presents the process as an attempt to bring some of
YC's batch mechanics to Series A investing. This is a report from the fund's own
operator, not an independent comparison of fundraising models.

## A Process Is a Product with Users

Fundraising has inputs, latency, rules, and failure states. A founder should be
able to answer:

- What evidence must we prepare?
- How many meetings and decision makers are involved?
- When will a yes or no arrive?
- Which terms are fixed, founder-selected, or negotiable?
- What company milestone becomes possible if the round closes?

Ambiguity transfers cost to the founder. Every unclear rule invites extra decks,
backchannels, speculative meetings, and negotiation strategy. Explicit rules can
reduce that work, but only when applicants read them and genuinely prefer the
tradeoffs.

## Source Case: Nine Decisions in a Two-Week Window

Dalton says Standard funded nine companies in its first cycle. He describes an
application deadline, first-round invitations within roughly five to seven days,
two meetings for finalists, and final decisions two days after the in-person
meeting. The whole selection period took about two weeks.

On the founder side, the stated interaction was an application that often took a
few hours, a 20-minute first interview, a 30-minute second meeting, and delivery
of diligence documents the company should already maintain. The fund absorbed
the work of reviewing those materials. Dalton also reports that application
volume was much higher than expected and required about three times the reading
time he had scheduled.

That surprise supports a process lesson: reducing user effort can shift work to
the operator. A fast founder experience requires enough reviewer capacity,
clear evidence requests, and a decision calendar. Efficiency does not mean the
work disappears.

## Try It Yourself: Write a Round Spec

In 20 minutes, make a one-page document with six fields: capital required,
milestone unlocked, runway after the round, maximum dilution you accept,
evidence already available, and the date by which you need a decision.

Add one valuation rationale using your traction and relevant comparable rounds.
Finally, choose no more than five investors whose actual process fits the spec.
The output is a decision document, not a pitch deck; stop at one page.

## Naming Your Price Changes the Burden

Standard's unusual rule in this cycle was to ask founders to state their own
valuation. Dalton says the fund did not negotiate: if it accepted the company,
it accepted the submitted price. Some founders chose valuations that appeared
proportional to traction. Others apparently copied prices from rounds they had
heard about and were surprised to receive a rejection rather than a counteroffer.

Dalton's heuristic is approximately linear: asking twice the valuation demands
roughly twice the strength in growth, market, or other company quality. That is
his description of Standard's selection logic, not a universal valuation law.
Market competition, ownership targets, capital needs, and investor strategy can
produce different outcomes elsewhere.

The broader lesson is to understand the mechanism before optimizing inside it.
A fixed, opt-in process serves founders who want speed and certainty. A founder
who wants price discovery across a competitive process may rationally choose a
traditional fundraise instead.

cohorts, key contracts, product-security material, incorporation documents, and
a short explanation of major risks. Remove personal customer data where it is
not required, grant the minimum access necessary, and track who can view it.

The source says well-run companies should be able to provide diligence material;
this checklist is an original starting point, not Standard Capital's published
request list or legal advice.

## Three Things to Keep

- An efficient fundraising process makes evidence, terms, meetings, and decision timing explicit.
- Lower founder effort requires real reviewer capacity; process work moves rather than vanishes.
- A valuation should connect to traction and financing goals, and its meaning depends on whether negotiation is available.

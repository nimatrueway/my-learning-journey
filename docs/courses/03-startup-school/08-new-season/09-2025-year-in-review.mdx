# 2025 Year In Review

One person says AI is a bubble. Another says it is already changing everything.
They may be looking at different layers of the same system and arguing as if
only one can be true.

⏱️ **~6 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 26-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel range across San Francisco, depopulation,
self-driving cars, AGI, AI financing, and abundance. The useful lesson is not a
set of predictions to memorize. It is a way to split broad claims into parts
that can be true, false, early, or risky independently.

## Replace One Big Verdict with Four Ledgers

For any technology wave, track four ledgers:

1. **Capability:** What can the technology reliably do now?
2. **Adoption:** Who uses it in recurring work, and how deeply?
3. **Economics:** Where are value, cost, margin, and financing accumulating?
4. **Experience:** Which people feel better or worse outcomes in daily life?

This decomposition prevents two common errors. Real capability does not prove
that every investment will earn a return. Financial excess does not prove that
the underlying technology is a hoax. Aggregate abundance does not ensure that
housing or another essential good feels affordable to a household.

## Source Case: Useful Fiber, Painful Financing

In discussing whether AI is a bubble, Dalton separates the internet from the
financing of fiber-optic deployment. The internet created durable value, and
later companies and consumers benefited from extensive fiber. Yet some people
who financed or built excess capacity lost money. A useful substrate and a bad
investment cycle coexisted.

He applies that analogy cautiously to debt-funded data centers and graphics
processors. His concern is not that AI products create no customer value. It is
that some layer of a large buildout may be overbuilt or financed with obligations
that become painful when assumptions change. That is a personal risk heuristic,
not evidence that a specific present-day project will fail.

Michael adds a different time dimension: even if model development stopped,
business adoption of existing tools could continue producing gains because he
believes adoption remains early. Again, this is the speaker's assessment, not a
measured forecast supplied by the episode.

## Try It Yourself: Create a Prediction Ledger

Choose one claim about AI, your city, or your market. Give yourself 15 minutes.
Write one sentence in each of the four ledgers: capability, adoption, economics,
and experience. Mark every sentence as **observed**, **inferred**, or **predicted**.

Then add one dated update rule: “By **date**, if **observable signal** happens, I
will move from **current belief** to **revised belief**.” Keep the signal narrow
enough that a colleague could determine whether it occurred without debating
your intentions.

## Progress and Affordability Can Diverge

The episode's abundance discussion supplies another application. Technology can
make communication, clothing, or other goods dramatically more available while
people still experience pressure from housing and everyday costs. Michael says
affordability is likely the stronger short-term political message, while
abundance better describes the work of increasing supply and government
capacity. He also says this is not his area of expertise.

That admission is important. A year-in-review conversation blends personal
observation, analogy, and forecast; it is not a controlled evidence review.
Depopulation timelines, AGI timelines, local policy, and market consequences
have different evidence bases. Preserve the labels. A vivid analogy can suggest
what to inspect, but it cannot establish how large an effect is or when it will
arrive.

<details>
<summary>🗂️ An uncertainty label set (optional)</summary>

Use one label beside each review claim:

- **Observation:** directly seen or measured in the period.
- **Source report:** stated by another person or organization and not independently checked here.
- **Inference:** an explanation connecting observations.
- **Heuristic:** a reusable rule of thumb from experience.
- **Forecast:** a claim about a future state or timeline.

Add a confidence level only after choosing the label. “High confidence forecast”
and “high confidence observation” are different kinds of statements. This label
set is an original synthesis inspired by the episode's disagreements and
qualifications.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Capability, adoption, economics, and lived experience can move in different directions at the same time.
- A valuable technology can coexist with overbuilding, fragile financing, or losses at one layer of its stack.
- Record what would change your mind; a review should improve future judgment, not freeze a confident story.

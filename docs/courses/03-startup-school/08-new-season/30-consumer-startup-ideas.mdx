# How to Find Consumer Startup Ideas

“Make the current app 10% nicer” is a crowded place to search for a consumer
startup. A better clue may be an experience you cannot see at all because only
heads of state, wealthy families, or well-connected insiders know it exists.

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 30-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel propose a discovery heuristic: find an
outcome currently delivered through money, staff, expertise, or privileged
access, then ask whether technology can make that outcome cheap and available
to many more people. AI is one possible cost reducer, not the idea by itself.

## Search Above the Existing Baseline

Early internet products often competed with paper, physical inventory, or
nothing. Napster made individual songs accessible without buying an entire CD;
streaming removed the video-store trip; digital maps replaced handwritten
turn-by-turn directions with a live location. The hosts remember these products
as feeling dramatically better because the old baseline was so weak.

Once a category moves online, another online version faces a higher bar. The
premium-experience heuristic changes the comparison. Instead of polishing the
ordinary workflow, investigate what happens when a person has effectively
unlimited resources. Break that service into outcomes, identify the expensive
inputs, and ask which input technology can compress.

The intermediate logic is:

1. High spending reveals outcomes people care deeply about.
2. Premium services already demonstrate one way to produce those outcomes.
3. Software, networks, automation, or AI may reduce the scarce labor or
   coordination required.
4. Lower cost and easier access can create a new mass-market experience.

This is a source of hypotheses, not validation. A premium service may rely on
human trust, judgment, or scarcity that does not compress cleanly.

## Source Case: The Health Experience You Cannot See

One host describes struggling to find a good doctor for an injury. A wealthy
investor pointed him toward a different, more expensive pathway that was still
within reach and delivered a noticeably different experience. The surprise was
not merely better bedside manner; it was discovering that an alternate system
existed at all.

From that story, the hosts ask what health care looks like for a head of state.
Perhaps care is coordinated, records arrive before the appointment, the right
specialist is found quickly, and follow-up does not depend on the patient
managing every handoff. Those details are inferences to investigate, not facts
established by the anecdote. The startup question is which desired outcome can
be reproduced safely for ordinary users at far lower cost.

They connect this to Brian Chesky's reported Airbnb brainstorming method:
describe a normal check-in, then imagine five-, six-, and increasingly absurd
star versions. A car meeting the guest at the airport may reveal a real
coordination need; a parade reveals less. Exaggeration is useful because it
escapes incremental assumptions, after which feasibility and demand must cut
the list back down.

## Try It Yourself: Run a Premium-to-Mass Sprint

Choose one domain you understand: health, education, travel, finance, home
care, legal help, or another daily need. Set a five-minute timer and write:

1. The ordinary experience in one sentence.
2. The outcome an extremely well-resourced person buys instead.
3. The people, information, coordination, and trust required to produce it.
4. One input technology could make at least ten times cheaper or faster.
5. A 48-hour test with three target users.

The test must expose demand, not just technical feasibility. Offer a manual
version of the improved outcome, state the price or commitment required, and
record whether all three users accept, decline, or ask for something different.

## Do Not Automate the Disappointment

The hosts criticize consumer AI products that replace a human interaction with
a chatbot or voice agent while delivering roughly the same or worse outcome.
Immediate phone pickup can beat an hour on hold, but lower service cost alone
does not create a premium consumer experience. A product that feels like a
95%-as-good imitation may lack the dramatic benefit that powered earlier
consumer breakthroughs.

Health care also carries safety, privacy, regulatory, and equity constraints.
Coordination software is not automatically clinical judgment, and a head of
state's service may depend on resources that should not or cannot be automated.
In every domain, investigate which part users value before assuming the visible
luxury is the product.

<details>
<summary>🧪 Questions for a premium-experience interview (optional)</summary>

Ask for a recent episode rather than opinions:

- What outcome were you trying to secure, and what made it important?
- Who coordinated the work, and what did they know that you did not?
- Where did money, reputation, or relationships change the path?
- Which part would you refuse to replace with software?
- What would have to be true for an ordinary user to receive the same outcome?

Do not ask for confidential medical, financial, or legal details. You need the
service mechanism and desired outcome, not private records.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Look for valuable outcomes hidden behind wealth, staff, expertise, or access rather than starting from the ordinary product baseline.
- Decompose the premium service and use technology to compress a scarce input; AI alone is not the consumer benefit.
- Validate demand and outcome quality because a cheaper imitation, especially in high-stakes domains, may only automate disappointment.

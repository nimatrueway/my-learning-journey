# A Founders Guide To Selling Your Company

An acquisition headline compresses months of uncertainty into one clean number.
Founders live in the uncompressed version: ambiguous interest, different buyer
motives, employee consequences, and a process that can vanish after consuming
the company's attention.

⏱️ **~8 min** · ~1,200 words at 200 wpm + 2 minutes of practice · the 31-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel demystify acquisitions by asking what the
buyer is actually purchasing and who inside the buyer can authorize it. Their
guidance is a strategic heuristic, not valuation, tax, fiduciary, employment,
or legal advice. Those questions require a board and qualified specialists.

## Start With the Buyer’s Job

The source separates several acquisition motives:

- **Talent:** The buyer wants a team it cannot hire efficiently one person at a time.
- **Product or technology:** A capability would take too long or carry too much execution risk to build internally.
- **Revenue or customers:** The business adds an economic stream or distribution position.
- **Strategic defense:** The company could strengthen a competitor or threaten an important market.

The same startup can be worth different amounts under each thesis. A team valued
as talent may receive employment packages while investors recover little. A
growing revenue asset may be judged on future economics. Therefore, “What are
we worth?” is incomplete. Ask: **What costly problem does this specific buyer
solve by owning us now?**

## Source Case: Great Buyers Expect to Capture Upside

Dalton names Google's purchase of YouTube and Facebook's purchases of Instagram
and WhatsApp as celebrated corporate-development deals because the assets later
became worth far more to the acquirers. The buyer paid substantial prices and
still captured enormous upside.

This reverses a weak founder assumption that a large company has spare money to
hand out. Corporate-development teams are rewarded for buying assets that
become more valuable than their purchase price. A strong sale thesis therefore
connects the startup to future value inside the buyer; it does not appeal to the
buyer's ability to afford generosity.

These famous outcomes are exceptional and evaluated with hindsight. They
illustrate buyer incentives, not a comparable-company valuation method.

## Find the Sponsor Behind Corporate Development

The hosts describe corporate development as a gatekeeper and facilitator. It
can screen opportunities, coordinate diligence, and negotiate, but an executive
with budget usually sponsors the transaction. Warm enthusiasm from a process
owner is not the same as internal conviction from a decision maker.

Map the buying system:

1. **Champion:** Who experiences the strategic problem and wants the company?
2. **Sponsor:** Which executive can allocate budget and political capital?
3. **Process owner:** Who runs diligence, negotiation, and coordination?
4. **Blockers:** Which finance, legal, security, product, or board approvals can stop it?
5. **Clock:** What internal planning cycle, runway limit, or competitive event creates urgency?

If no sponsor can explain why the deal matters, more meetings may only make the
pipeline look alive.

## Create Options Before You Need an Exit

The episode highlights a catch-22: acquirers often show the most interest when
the company also has strong financing demand and credible growth. The founder
then has less reason to sell, which improves negotiating power.

Do not manufacture fake offers. Build real alternatives: sufficient runway,
continued customer growth, financing possibilities, and more than one plausible
buyer. An acquisition process can fail late. A company that pauses operations
for one friendly conversation may arrive at “no deal” with weaker leverage than
it started.

## Try It Yourself: Build a 30-Minute Buyer Map

Choose one plausible acquirer and set a 30-minute timer. Write one sentence for
the value thesis, then name the likely champion, sponsor, process owner, two
blockers, and one urgency trigger. Add your best alternative if the deal dies.
Finally, list what founders, employees, and investors might each receive.

Stop at one page. Do not contact the buyer or circulate valuation claims until
the board and relevant advisers have reviewed the situation.

## Price Is Not the Whole Outcome

Acquisition announcements can blur consideration paid for shares, retention
packages, assumed obligations, and employee offers. The source emphasizes that
some “acquisitions” primarily hire selected team members; other employees may
receive no offer, and investors may recover little.

Founders must examine certainty, form of payment, vesting, retention,
indemnities, tax treatment, closing conditions, employee selection, product
continuity, and what happens if the transaction fails. Duties also vary by
jurisdiction and company documents. A seemingly lower offer may be superior if
it is more certain or treats stakeholders better; that conclusion is contextual,
not a rule.

<summary>🧮 An offer-comparison frame (optional)</summary>

Compare offers across five columns rather than one headline:

| Dimension | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Consideration | Cash, stock, earnout, retention, or assumed liabilities? |
| Certainty | Financing, approvals, diligence, and closing conditions? |
| Time | How long to sign and close, and what happens to runway? |
| People | Who receives offers, on what terms, and who is excluded? |
| Future | Product fate, founder role, vesting, restrictions, and integration risk? |

Have legal, tax, and financial advisers model the actual distribution. This
frame only exposes the questions.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Acquisition value depends on the specific problem the buyer solves by owning the company now.
- Corporate development runs process, while an executive sponsor with budget usually creates real deal momentum.
- Compare stakeholder outcomes, certainty, and obligations rather than treating the headline price as the whole transaction.

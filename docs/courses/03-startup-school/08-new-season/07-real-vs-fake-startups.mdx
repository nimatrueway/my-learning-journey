# Real vs Fake Startups

The calendar is full. The team has a polished deck, an office, investor calls,
and a launch announcement drafted. One awkward question remains: what changed
for a customer this week?

⏱️ **~7 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 9-minute source video is optional

Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel use “real” and “fake” as deliberately sharp
labels for a softer distinction: work that produces evidence versus activity
that reproduces startup aesthetics. Their labels are a coaching heuristic, not
a moral classification of founders or a scientific test.

## Trace Activity to an External Result

A real operating loop has an external endpoint. Code reaches a user. A sales
call reveals an objection. A launch creates usage. Support work prevents a
customer from leaving. Even a failed experiment is real when it changes what
the team knows.

Performative work terminates inside the company. Hiring makes the org chart
look mature. Fundraising becomes the product. An office signals commitment
without improving delivery. None of those actions is inherently useless, but
each needs a causal link to product, customer, or learning evidence.

For any large block of work, complete this sentence:

> We did **activity**, which changed **observable result**, so next we will
> **decision**.

If the middle stays blank, the activity may be theater. The fix is not to work
longer. It is to shorten the path from effort to reality.

## Source Case: Google's Perks Were Not the Product

The hosts describe a founder copying the visible furniture of successful
companies: rent an office, hire people, take investor meetings. In their Google
example, this imitation mistakes catered lunch and workplace signals for the
mechanism that mattered. Google first had to build a search product people
wanted; the recognizable company machinery came around that achievement.

The case explains why imitation is seductive. Visible practices are easy to
copy, while the hidden chain of technical judgment, customer response, and
iteration is not. A founder can therefore feel culturally correct while making
little contact with the problem.

The opposite caricature is also wrong. Dalton and Michael point to Google and
Stripe as companies sometimes mythologized as purely technical, self-serve
machines. In practice, they argue, both developed formidable distribution and
sales capabilities. PostHog is their more recent developer-tool example: a good
product still had to be explained repeatedly so prospective users knew it
existed.

The boundary is not “building good, marketing bad.” It is whether building,
selling, and marketing connect a useful product to actual users.

## Try It Yourself: Run a 24-Hour Output Audit

Set a timer for 10 minutes. List yesterday's three largest work blocks. Beside
each, write one observable output: shipped behavior, customer conversation,
usage change, signed commitment, or decision-quality learning.

Choose the weakest row. Within the next 24 hours, convert it into one contact
with reality: put the prototype in front of one user, ask one prospect for a
decision, publish the technical explanation, or remove the meeting and ship the
smallest test. Keep the scope to one person, one artifact, and one day.

## Improve Starting Conditions, Not Appearances

Michael offers a charitable explanation for drift into performance. A founder
may have urgency but poor starting conditions: little time, no technical
cofounder, weak access to customers, or an environment with few relevant peers.
The temptation is to compensate with visible motion, such as outsourcing a
product one cannot evaluate or raising before understanding the problem.

His heuristic is to spend grit on improving the starting conditions. That could
mean learning enough to judge technical work, moving closer to users, finding a
complementary collaborator, or creating enough runway for focused work. It does
not imply that every founder must relocate or work full-time; those are
context-dependent advantages, and constraints such as caregiving, geography,
and finances are real.

<details>
<summary>🧪 A reality-contact scorecard (optional)</summary>

Score a planned activity from 0 to 2 on four questions:

- **Product:** Will something usable change?
- **Customer:** Will a real user or buyer respond?
- **Learning:** Could the result alter a decision?
- **Distribution:** Will the right people become more likely to discover or adopt it?

A low score does not automatically make the work wasteful. Legal setup,
security, hiring, and maintenance can be necessary infrastructure. It does mean
you should name the downstream result and keep the cost proportional. This
scorecard is an original exercise, not a metric proposed by the speakers.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Real startup work ends in product, customer, distribution, or decision-quality evidence.
- Copy the causal mechanism behind a successful company, not its most visible rituals.
- Technical excellence and active marketing can coexist; obscurity is not proof of seriousness.

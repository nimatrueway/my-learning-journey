# Startup Mechanics: Keep the Company Fundable

The least glamorous startup work has a nasty habit: it becomes urgent exactly
when the company is fundraising, hiring, or being acquired. A missing signature
can sit quietly for years and then stop a deal cold.

⏱️ **~10 min** · includes 2 minutes of practice · the 58-minute source lecture is optional

Kirsty Nathoo's core argument is not that founders should become lawyers or tax
experts. It is that they should know which company mechanics must be completed,
use standard processes where those processes fit, bring in specialists when
they do not, and retain proof that the work happened.

:::caution A 2017 US startup lecture, not current legal advice
The lecture was recorded in 2017 and concentrates on venture-backed US
companies. Entity choices, securities documents, tax filing methods, employment
rules, service providers, and cross-border consequences can change or depend on
your facts. Treat the principles below as an issue-spotting guide. Verify current
requirements with qualified legal and tax professionals in your jurisdiction.
:::

## Incorporate When the Project Becomes a Company

Incorporating too early creates administration around an experiment that may
disappear. Incorporating too late leaves valuable activity attached to the
founders personally. Nathoo identifies practical triggers: multiple people are
creating meaningful intellectual property, the product is ready to accept
payments, the work creates liabilities, or the company is preparing to raise
money.

Formation creates a separate legal entity that can own IP, sign contracts, hold
money, issue shares, and carry liabilities. That separation must exist in
practice too. Customer revenue belongs in a company bank account, not mixed with
a founder's personal funds, and work created before formation needs a documented
path into the company.

The lecture recommends a Delaware C corporation for the venture-backed US path
because it was familiar to startup investors and counsel. That is a contextual
recommendation, not a universal law. A bootstrapped business, a company operating
outside the US, or a founder with unusual tax or immigration circumstances may
need a different answer. The durable lesson is to choose the structure for the
company you intend to build, not the cheapest form someone happens to know.

## Source Case: A Tiny Formation Error Became a Six-Month Blocker

Nathoo describes a YC company formed as a Connecticut LLC on local advice. When
YC invested, the company attempted to convert into a Delaware C corporation.
Three financing rounds later, new counsel discovered that the conversion had
been completed incorrectly and was invalid.

The next financing stopped while four law firms reconstructed the chain. Nathoo
says the repair took six months and cost about half a million dollars. The
original error was simple; the accumulated consequences were not.

The point is not “hire the most expensive lawyer.” Standard formation can often
use a reputable, current workflow. Nonstandard facts need relevant expertise.
In either case, the founders remain responsible for confirming that documents
were signed, assets transferred, approvals recorded, and final copies retained.

## Founder Equity Is a Future-Work Agreement

Allocating founder equity forces an important conversation: Who is full time?
What does each person expect to contribute? Does everyone consider the result
fair? Nathoo argues that co-founder splits should usually be roughly equal
because the difficult years are still ahead. An idea, prototype, early sale, or
three-month head start is small beside a company that may take a decade to build.

Equity also needs paperwork. Founders purchase shares, the company records the
ownership on a capitalization table, and the shares normally vest. A common
startup schedule is four years with a one-year cliff: before the first
anniversary none has vested, then 25% vests, followed by monthly vesting through
year four. If a founder leaves early, the company can repurchase the unvested
portion under the governing documents.

Vesting protects the people who continue building, reassures investors that the
founders still have an incentive to stay, and makes employee vesting less
hypocritical. Nathoo also highlights the US Section 83(b) election for certain
restricted stock. The deadline is short and the consequences are personal, so
founders should get current tax advice immediately rather than relying on a
course summary or an old filing workflow.

## Financing Changes the Cap Table

A priced round sells shares at a price established in that round. A convertible
instrument supplies money now and determines shares later under its terms. The
lecture uses the YC SAFE as its convertible example and explains how a valuation
cap can reward an early investor for taking greater risk.

The arithmetic is less important than the habit: model the capitalization table
before accepting money. Every issuance changes ownership. Founders can own a
smaller percentage of a more valuable company, but raising too much at a low
valuation can sell away a large stake before the company knows how the money
will create progress.

“Simple” does not mean consequence-free. Nathoo notes that even a SAFE requires
company approval and a completed transaction. A verbal commitment is not cash;
fundraising is not finished until the agreement is executed, the approval is
recorded, and the money reaches the company account. SAFE forms and conventions
have changed since this lecture, so use current documents and advice.

## Hiring Creates Obligations, Not Just Output

The label “contractor” does not make someone a contractor. Nathoo contrasts a
specialist who controls how they deliver a defined project with an employee who
works inside the company's continuing operations under its direction. The
classification affects payroll, taxes, employment protections, and other duties,
and the governing tests vary by place.

Both relationships need written terms that assign relevant IP to the company.
Employees require a real payroll process rather than founders improvising tax
withholding. Early employees should also understand their equity in both shares
and percentage terms, along with vesting and exercise conditions. A large share
number without the denominator communicates almost nothing.

Nathoo recommends meaningful equity for early employees because they take risk,
shape the company, and may accept less cash than established employers offer.
Vesting makes generosity compatible with the chance that a hire will not work
out. The practical principle is reciprocal clarity: the company documents what
it receives, and the employee can understand what they receive.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Mechanics Ledger

Choose the next real milestone: incorporation, founder shares, a financing,
first contractor, or first employee. Create five columns:

| Decision | Document | Approval | Money movement | Proof retained |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What must be chosen? | What must be executed? | Who must authorize it? | What must be paid, deposited, or withheld? | Where is the signed final record? |

Fill one row from facts, not memory. Mark unknowns explicitly. Assign one founder
to resolve each unknown with the appropriate professional or service provider,
and add a due date. A folder full of unsigned drafts does not count as proof.

## Keep a Diligence-Ready Trail

Nathoo closes with a plain operational rule: keep signed company documents in a
safe, organized place and give one person responsibility for the system. These
records are needed during the most stressful moments, including fundraising and
acquisition diligence.

The same separation applies to company money. Investor capital belongs to the
company and should be spent to improve its chance of success. Nathoo recounts a
founder using substantial company funds for a Las Vegas trip, advertising it on
Facebook, and being fired when co-founders and investors found out. Legal
mechanics cannot replace judgment, but they make responsibility visible.

<details>
<summary>🧾 A minimal issue-spotting checklist (optional)</summary>

- **Formation:** entity choice, governing documents, board and officer actions, IP assignment, bank account, and registrations.
- **Founder equity:** signed purchase documents, payment evidence, cap table, vesting terms, and time-sensitive tax advice.
- **Financing:** current instrument, scenario-modeled dilution, authorization, signatures, funds received, and updated records.
- **Hiring:** correct classification, IP and confidentiality terms, payroll and local compliance, equity approval, and a comprehensible grant explanation.
- **Records:** one accountable owner, signed final documents, access controls, backups, and a recurring review before diligence creates urgency.

This is a prompt for professional review, not a complete compliance list.

</details>

## Three Things to Keep

- Incorporate when IP, payments, liability, or financing make the project a real company, and choose a structure that fits the intended path.
- Treat equity and fundraising as cap-table events with signed documents, recorded approvals, completed money movement, and retained proof.
- Classify and pay workers correctly, document IP ownership, explain equity clearly, and keep the company continuously ready for diligence.

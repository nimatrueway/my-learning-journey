## The hook

A microphone records one number after another: air pressure, sampled perhaps 16,000 times every second. Four seconds of speech is therefore 64,000 values. Where are the words? The speaker? The slammed door in the background?

They are not separate fields. They are overlapping patterns at different frequencies and times. An audio model's first job is to turn that long, twitchy waveform into a representation where those patterns are easier to learn.

## Turn sound into patches 🎮

Most modern speech models convert a waveform into a **spectrogram**: horizontal position is time, vertical position is frequency, and brightness is energy. It behaves enough like an image that we can divide it into patches, project each patch into an embedding, and send the resulting sequence through a transformer.

<MediaTokenViz initialMode="audio" />

**Things to try:**

1. Double the clip length. Token count doubles, but full-attention comparisons grow by roughly four times because attention costs $O(n^2)$.
2. Increase patch size. The sequence gets cheaper, but each token smears more time and frequency together.
3. Switch to video for a preview of the next lesson. A few seconds can explode into tens of thousands of tokens.

That tradeoff is real. Fine time resolution helps distinguish quick sounds such as `t` and `k`; fine frequency resolution helps separate tones. Smaller patches preserve detail but demand more memory and compute.

## Three useful families

Audio models differ mostly in what they consume and what they must emit.

**Audio classifiers** map a clip to one or more labels: dog bark, siren, music genre, speaker identity. An encoder compresses the sequence, then a classification head reads a pooled representation. This is the same pattern as the cat classifier in Module 4, with spectrogram patches instead of pixels.

**Speech recognizers** map a long audio sequence to a shorter text sequence. Encoder-only systems often use **CTC loss**, which learns alignments without requiring every transcript character to be timestamped. Encoder-decoder systems such as Whisper encode audio patches, then autoregressively decode text tokens. Cross-attention lets each generated word look back at the relevant moments in the recording.

**Audio generators** predict waveforms, spectrograms, or compressed audio tokens. Neural codecs first turn sound into discrete codes, much as a text tokenizer turns prose into token IDs. A transformer predicts those codes; a decoder turns them back into sound. Text-to-speech, music generation, and voice conversion use variations of this recipe.

## Tiny example: speech recognition

The high-level API hides preprocessing, model inference, and text decoding, but the conceptual pipeline is exactly the one above:

```python
from transformers import pipeline

transcribe = pipeline(
    task="automatic-speech-recognition",
    model="openai/whisper-tiny.en",
)

result = transcribe("meeting.wav")
print(result["text"])
```

Underneath that call:

```text
waveform → log-Mel spectrogram → patch embeddings → transformer encoder
         → text-token decoder → transcript
```

The model does not literally hear words. It learns statistical links between sound patterns and token sequences from paired audio and transcripts.

## What breaks in practice

Audio data has unusually sharp distribution shifts. A recognizer trained on studio speech may struggle with a phone microphone, an unfamiliar accent, crosstalk, or a cafe espresso machine conducting its own interview.

Accuracy alone can hide the damage. For transcription, inspect **word error rate** and actual mistakes across accents, languages, noise levels, and recording devices. For event detection, inspect false positives over long quiet recordings; a model that is 99% accurate per window can still produce an exhausting stream of bogus alerts.

There is also a privacy boundary: voices can reveal identity, health clues, location, and nearby conversations. Collect only what the task needs, define retention before recording, and treat consent as part of the data pipeline rather than a checkbox after training.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Why log-Mel spectrograms? (optional)</summary>

A short-time Fourier transform measures which frequencies are present in overlapping windows. A **Mel filter bank** groups frequencies using a spacing closer to human pitch perception. Taking the logarithm compresses the huge range between quiet and loud energy.

The result is not a perfect account of sound, and phase information is usually discarded. It is simply a compact, stable representation that has worked well for speech. Some models instead learn directly from raw waveforms, trading hand-designed preprocessing for more data and compute.

</details>

## Try it yourself

Record the same sentence three times: close to the microphone, across the room, and with music playing. Run the tiny Whisper example on each. Write down substitutions, deletions, and insertions rather than judging the transcript as vaguely “good” or “bad.” You are performing a tiny distribution-shift evaluation.

## Recap 📌

- Audio models usually transform waveforms into time-frequency features or learned audio tokens
- Classification uses an encoder and head; transcription often uses CTC or an encoder-decoder transformer
- Longer, finer-grained audio preserves detail but increases token count, compute, and evaluation burden

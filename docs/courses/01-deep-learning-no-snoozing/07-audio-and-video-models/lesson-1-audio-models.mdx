# 7.1 — Audio models: from waveforms to words

You say “feed the cat.” A cupboard slams halfway through. Your recording contains one mixed signal, not neat tracks labelled `words`, `cat`, and `cupboard`.

How does a speech model find words in that mixture? Follow three steps: **measure the vibration, summarize short moments, predict text**. None of those steps discovers a ready-made word hidden in the file.

## 1. A waveform is a pressure diary

A microphone turns changes in air pressure into a changing electrical signal. A recorder samples that signal: at **16 kHz**, it stores 16,000 amplitude measurements per second, per channel. Four seconds of mono audio contain 64,000 numbers.

One sample is not a letter or a pitch. Pitch emerges from a repeating pattern across samples; speech emerges from patterns changing over time. Louder sound usually means larger amplitude, not more samples. A stereo recording has two channels, not twice the duration.

**Sample rate is the clock attached to those numbers.** Treating 48,000 samples as 16 kHz makes one second play for three seconds, at a lower pitch. Resampling computes a new sequence on a new clock; merely changing the label corrupts the input.

## 2. Ask which frequencies are present right now

A **spectrogram** summarizes many short, overlapping waveform windows. Horizontal position is time, vertical position is frequency, and brightness indicates energy. It is a measurement, not a photograph of sound.

Imagine a steady whistle followed by a clap. The whistle makes a roughly horizontal bright band: similar frequency, sustained over time. The clap makes a brief vertical burst: many frequencies at one moment. Speech combines moving bands and short bursts. The cupboard slam overlaps them; converting to a spectrogram does not remove it.

A **log-Mel spectrogram** groups frequencies into perceptually motivated bands and compresses the energy range. Now the model sees a manageable table of features. There are still no words, only evidence from which words might be inferred.

## 🧪 Give the audio engineer a token budget

Some audio transformers divide spectrograms into rectangular **patches** and turn each patch into an embedding vector, just as a vision transformer does with images. A patch can cover several moments and frequency bands; it is not a text token.

Start at **4 seconds, 16×16 patches**. Predict before moving anything:

1. There are 400 time steps and 80 bands: $25 \times 5 = 125$ patches, not 64,000 waveform tokens.
2. Move to **8 seconds**. There are 250 patches and 62,500 token pairs instead of 15,625: twice the sequence, four times the full-attention comparisons.
3. Return to **4 seconds** and choose **8×8 patches**. There are 500 patches and 250,000 pairs. Halving both patch dimensions gives four times as many tokens and sixteen times as many pairs.

Those are pair counts per attention head per layer, not seconds of runtime or total model memory. Larger patches give the encoder a coarser grouping of evidence; they do not literally blur the input, and more tokens do not guarantee more accurate transcripts. The question is whether the representation preserves the distinctions your task needs.

## Three useful families

Audio models differ mostly in what they consume and what they must emit.

**Classification asks “what happened?”** A sound-event classifier might label the same recording as speech plus an impact. An encoder builds features; a classification head reads a summary and predicts labels. This resembles the image classifier in Module 4, but with audio features as its input. An event label is not a transcript.

**Transcription asks “what was said?”** Whisper takes log-Mel features through two convolutional layers, which mix nearby information and reduce the time-axis length, then a transformer encoder. A text decoder generates subword tokens, using the encoded audio as context. It does **not** use the rectangular patch grid above. Module 8 explains [how the decoder consults the encoder](/courses/deep-learning/module-8/lesson-2-cross-attention).

**Generation asks “what should it sound like?”** Some systems predict compressed audio codes produced by a neural codec; its decoder turns those codes into a waveform. These codes represent sound, not spelling. Other generators predict spectrograms or use diffusion. “Transformer” alone does not tell you the input representation or output task.

## Tiny example: speech recognition

Use a short English recording you made yourself. The [mini-project](/courses/deep-learning/module-7/mini-project) provides installation and file preparation. Passing samples together with their actual rate avoids relying on an external command-line audio decoder:

```python
from transformers import pipeline
import soundfile as sf

transcribe = pipeline(
    task="automatic-speech-recognition",
    model="openai/whisper-tiny.en",
)

samples, sample_rate = sf.read("clean.wav", dtype="float32")
if samples.ndim == 2:
  samples = samples.mean(axis=1)
result = transcribe({"raw": samples, "sampling_rate": sample_rate})
print(result["text"])
```

Underneath that call:

```text
waveform + sample rate → log-Mel spectrogram → convolutions → transformer encoder
         → text-token decoder → transcript
```

The pipeline can resample when needed; the rate you supply must describe the samples you actually loaded. `tiny.en` is English-only. The first run downloads model weights. The result is a prediction, not evidence that those exact words were spoken: recognizers can invent plausible text on silence or noise.

## What breaks in practice

Audio data has unusually sharp distribution shifts. A recognizer trained on studio speech may struggle with a phone microphone, an unfamiliar accent, crosstalk, or a cafe espresso machine conducting its own interview.

Accuracy alone can hide the damage. For transcription, inspect **word error rate** and actual mistakes across accents, languages, noise levels, and recording devices. For event detection, inspect false positives over long quiet recordings; a model that is 99% accurate per window can still produce an exhausting stream of bogus alerts.

There is also a privacy boundary: voices can reveal identity, health clues, location, and nearby conversations. Collect only what the task needs, define retention before recording, and treat consent as part of the data pipeline rather than a checkbox after training.

<details>
<summary>🤓 Why log-Mel spectrograms? (optional)</summary>

A short-time Fourier transform measures which frequencies are present in overlapping windows. A **Mel filter bank** groups frequencies using a spacing closer to human pitch perception. Taking the logarithm compresses the huge range between quiet and loud energy.

The result is not a perfect account of sound, and phase information is usually discarded. It is simply a compact, stable representation that has worked well for speech. Some models instead learn directly from raw waveforms, trading hand-designed preprocessing for more data and compute.

</details>

## 🐾 Three things to keep

- A waveform needs its correct sample rate; a spectrogram describes frequency energy over time, not words.
- Match the representation to the model: patch-based audio encoders and Whisper's convolutional frontend are different designs.
- Judge outputs against a reference and recording conditions; fluent text can still be wrong.
